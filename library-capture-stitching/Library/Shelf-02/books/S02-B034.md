# Baseline Requirements for gsplat: Why Pure Rotation Fails Training

Why does a gsplat training run that looks fine for the first few hundred iterations end up producing a scene full of flat, streaky, transparent smears instead of a walkable room? The usual answer has nothing to do with gsplat's code. It has to do with what the images fed into it were allowed to see of the space, before gsplat ever entered the picture.

Gaussian splatting does not learn a scene from pixels alone. It learns a scene from pixels plus a starting point cloud, and that starting point cloud comes from COLMAP's structure-from-motion stage. As the sibling volume *What a Gaussian Splat Is and Why It Needs a Baseline* lays out, the original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper is explicit about this dependency: the method "starts from the sparse point cloud produced by camera-calibration/SfM" and represents the scene as a set of 3D Gaussians seeded from those points, specifically to avoid wasting capacity computing in empty space. Splatfacto, nerfstudio's implementation, states the practical consequence even more bluntly in its own documentation: it "works much better when initialized from pre-existing SfM geometry... than from random initialization, which yields lower quality." Random initialization is the fallback path, not the intended one. The intended path runs through COLMAP.

That means gsplat's actual entry requirement is not "a folder of JPGs." It is "a folder of JPGs from which COLMAP could triangulate 3D points." Those are very different bars, and pure rotation clears the first while failing the second completely.

## What triangulation actually needs

Triangulating a 3D point requires two things: the same physical point visible in at least two images, and those two images taken from different positions in space. The second requirement is the one operators forget, because a phone held at arm's length and pivoted in place produces images that look different from each other. Different framing, different pixels, apparently different views. But if the camera's optical center never moved, only rotated, every ray leaving that center toward a given scene point still passes through the exact same 3D location the whole time.

The two-view geometry literature describes this precisely: when two camera positions differ only by rotation and not translation, "all corresponding rays intersect along the same 3D line, so absolute depth/scale cannot be recovered — there is no parallax to disambiguate it." Parallax is the apparent shift of a scene point relative to more distant background when the observer's position changes. It's the reason you can judge distance by moving your head side to side but not by turning your head in place. A phone pivoted on a tripod, or a hand rotated from the wrist without stepping, generates images with plenty of visual variety and zero parallax.

The consequence for COLMAP is direct: "standard two-view pose estimation (essential-matrix-based) becomes degenerate under pure rotation." Degenerate here means the math has no unique solution to fall back on, not that it produces a slightly-off answer. COLMAP can often still work out how the camera rotated between frames, since rotation-only sequences are "not fully uninformative, just unusable for triangulating 3D structure." What it cannot do is place any 3D point at a real distance from the camera, because every candidate distance along that ray fits the observations equally well.

## Why this is also the definition of a panorama

Here is the detail that makes rotation-only capture such a natural trap for anyone who has also done 360 or panorama work: pure rotation about the optical center is not a failure mode in that world, it is the entire operating assumption. Classic automatic panorama stitching aligns images "under the assumption the camera only rotates about its optical center (no translation)... i.e., pure rotation, which is exactly the degenerate case that defeats SfM triangulation, is the case panorama stitching relies on." An Insta360 X4 Air producing a stitched 2:1 equirectangular JPG, or a phone shooting a stitched panorama, is doing correctly and deliberately the exact thing that ruins a photogrammetry sequence. The habits that make good panoramas (stand still, pivot from one point, keep the nodal point fixed) are the habits that make bad SfM input. An operator who has internalized "hold the camera steady and turn" from panorama work has internalized the wrong instinct for the room-by-room walk-through that Orbit Capture needs.

## What actually happens inside the pipeline

When rotation-only frames get fed to COLMAP anyway, the failure doesn't necessarily announce itself as a crash. COLMAP's incremental mapper may still find an initial pair and start registering images, since it can often solve the rotational component. What breaks is triangulation: points3D.txt ends up sparse or empty for the affected sequence, because there is no valid depth to assign. The per-point ERROR value in points3D.txt, the mean reprojection error, "is only updated after a global bundle adjustment runs," and bundle adjustment has nothing to refine when there's no triangulated geometry to adjust in the first place.

Hand that thin, depth-less point cloud to gsplat and the densification machinery has almost nothing to build on. gsplat's DefaultStrategy grows new Gaussians based on image-plane gradient signals (grow_grad2d, defaulting to 0.0002) and splits or clones existing ones based on their 3D scale (grow_scale3d, prune_scale3d), all of which assume the seed points already encode plausible position and size in 3D space. Feed it points that were never really triangulated, just guessed at along an ambiguous ray, and the optimizer has to invent depth from photometric loss alone. That is the classic gsplat failure signature: Gaussians stretch into long, thin, misoriented streaks trying to explain a 2D image with structure that has no reliable 3D anchor, because there was no baseline to establish one.

## What this means for the operator

The fix is not a gsplat setting. It's a capture discipline decided before the phone comes out of the pocket: every position in a room needs to be photographed from at least two meaningfully different camera positions, not just two different camera orientations. Stepping sideways a foot between shots, not just pivoting on the spot, is what turns a sequence of pretty pictures into a sequence COLMAP can actually triangulate. The companion volumes *The Rotation-Only Trap: Diagnosing a Degenerate Baseline* and *Filtering Frames with Insufficient Parallax Before Training* go further into recognizing this failure in COLMAP's own logs and screening it out of a capture bundle before it ever reaches gsplat. But the root idea is simple enough to carry into every room of a shoot: gsplat doesn't need photos of a space, it needs photos of a space taken from different places in it. Rotation is free. Baseline is the thing you have to walk for.
