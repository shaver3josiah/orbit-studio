# When to Trust a Low Registration Score and Reshoot Instead

A registration score is a number, and numbers invite a single reflex: if it's low, fix it. For a solo operator standing in a half-empty room with a phone in hand, that reflex is expensive. It can mean re-running a pipeline three times with different parameters when the honest answer was to walk back in and shoot twenty more frames. The question this book answers is how to tell those two situations apart, quickly, without guessing.

## What the score is actually measuring

Registration, in COLMAP's incremental pipeline, means an image has been successfully placed into the 3D reconstruction with a known pose. The sibling volume "Reading a Registration Health Score: <60% Warning, ~95% Healthy" covers how that percentage is computed and what the warning bands mean. This book assumes you already have the number in hand and asks what to do next.

The pipeline's own logging gives a clue about where to look. COLMAP's incremental mapper prints lines like "Registering image #241 (230)" followed by "=> Image sees 3755 / 8696 points," a directly parseable ratio of points seen to points available per image (COLMAP incremental mapper console log format, PR #2420 discussion). That per-image ratio is more diagnostic than the room's overall percentage, because it tells you whether failure is spread evenly across the room or concentrated in a handful of frames. A room where every image sees a healthy fraction of the point cloud but a few images sit near zero looks completely different, under the hood, from a room where every image is mediocre.

Real-world registration percentages vary widely across capture conditions, from roughly 46% up through 92-100% depending on the dataset and parameters used. That range itself is a useful sanity check: a mid-range score is not automatically a crisis, and a very high score does not automatically mean the room is done, since COLMAP will silently produce multiple disconnected sub-models rather than fail outright when not everything registers into one connected reconstruction (Tutorial — COLMAP official docs). A low overall number can hide a room that's actually fine but split into two solid pieces that never got stitched together.

## The two failure shapes, and why they need different fixes

Distinguish two shapes of low score.

The first is a *parameter-shaped* failure. The room was covered adequately, the camera settings were reasonable, but the reconstruction settings weren't matched to the scene. COLMAP's own FAQ singles out low-texture, low-overlap, and drastic-illumination scenes as cases needing different settings: DSP-SIFT with affine feature-shape estimation, guided feature matching, and disabling `ignore_two_view_tracks` so two-view-only tracks still triangulate (Frequently Asked Questions — COLMAP official docs). For weak-texture rooms specifically, the documented remedy is higher input resolution, a larger patch-window radius, and a lower `filter_min_ncc` threshold during dense stereo. None of that requires the operator to go back. It requires re-running the pipeline against the same JPGs with different knobs turned.

The second is a *coverage-shaped* failure. No amount of parameter tuning fixes a hallway that was never actually photographed from enough angles, or a corner where the operator swung the phone too fast and left a gap in overlap. Cultural Heritage Imaging's photogrammetry guidelines put a number on the minimum standard here: roughly 66% overlap between consecutive frames, achieved by moving the camera about a third of its field of view between shots, with every point on the subject appearing in at least three separate frames. If a room's registration failure traces to a stretch of wall that only ever appears in one or two frames, that is a hole in the data, not a hole in the settings. Rerunning COLMAP with different flags cannot manufacture parallax that was never captured.

The decision rule follows from that distinction: look at where the low-ratio images cluster before you touch a single parameter.

- If low-registration images are scattered evenly across the whole room's image set, with no obvious one wall or one dark corner responsible, suspect a scene-property problem (texture, lighting, overlap density) that a parameter change addresses globally. Adjust and rerun.
- If low-registration images cluster spatially, meaning they all correspond to the same physical stretch, that is a coverage hole. No pipeline setting recovers geometry from frames that were never taken. Send the operator back.
- If the sparse output shows two internally-consistent but disconnected models rather than one low-scoring model, that's neither of the above. It's a stitching problem, and the fix is `model_merger` against images shared between the two sub-models, followed by a full global bundle-adjustment pass to clean up the alignment (COLMAP model_merger usage and limitations, GitHub issues + docs). That's a pipeline-side fix, not a reshoot, provided the two halves each registered well on their own.

There's a specific trap worth naming: perceptual aliasing. Repetitive environments (rows of identical doors, uniform office cubicles, a corridor of matching windows) produce visually similar but spatially distinct patches, which can fool matching into false associations rather than honest failures to match at all (Perceptual aliasing and false loop closures in repetitive environments, arXiv 2508.13488). A false-positive match is worse than a missing one, because it corrupts the reconstruction with confident-looking wrong geometry rather than an obvious gap. If a room is visually repetitive, a moderate registration score deserves more suspicion, not less, since the number itself may be counting matches that shouldn't have been accepted.

A second trap sits underneath both failure shapes: pure rotation. If a stretch of the walk involved the operator swiveling in place rather than translating through space, the geometry becomes degenerate. All corresponding rays intersect along the same line and there is no parallax to recover depth from, no matter how many features get detected and matched (pure rotation as a degenerate two-view geometry configuration). This is worth checking before blaming either parameters or coverage, because it looks like a matching failure but is actually a motion failure, and the fix is neither a rerun nor a request for more still photos of the same spot. It requires actual lateral movement through the room on the reshoot, which the sibling volume "Deciding On-Site Whether a Room Needs a Reshoot" covers in more depth as an on-site judgment call.

## A practical checklist

Before rerunning anything, pull up the per-image registration ratios, not just the room average. Then ask three questions in order:

1. Are the low-scoring images spread across the whole room, or clustered on one wall or corner? Clustered means a coverage hole; go back and shoot that spot with proper overlap.
2. Does the sparse output actually contain two separate, well-registered models rather than one bad one? If so, this is a merge job, not a reshoot.
3. Is the room repetitive-looking or does the failing stretch correspond to a swivel rather than a walk? Both call for more care in what gets recaptured, not just more of the same.

Only after coverage and motion are ruled out does it make sense to reach for feature-extraction and matching parameters, since those settings can meaningfully help a scene with poor texture or lighting but cannot invent structure the camera never saw.

## What this means for the reader

A low registration score is a symptom with more than one possible cause, and treating it as a single problem wastes both compute time and operator time. The fastest fix is usually the one that matches the failure's shape: parameters for scenes that were shot well but processed with the wrong settings, a merge for reconstructions that split cleanly into two good halves, and a reshoot for the one failure mode no software setting can undo, which is a spot in the room that was simply never photographed with enough overlap or motion. Getting that triage right the first time is what keeps a one-person capture operation moving room to room instead of stalling in front of a terminal re-running the same job. Once the triage above points to a reshoot, the process for actually flagging and dispatching it can be automated end to end, which is where "Automating a Reshoot Request from a Failed Registration" picks up.
