# When to Fall Back to Manual Pose Priors from the Rig

When do you stop trying to make COLMAP figure out where your camera was, and just tell it?

That is the question this book answers, and it deserves a direct answer up front: you fall back to manual rig pose priors only after you have exhausted the cheaper fixes, and only when the failure mode is specifically about geometry the images themselves cannot resolve. Not before. Feeding COLMAP known poses is the last tool in the box, not the first, because it trades away the thing that makes structure-from-motion useful in the first place: the ability to recover a scene from nothing but photographs.

## What "known rig pose priors" actually means

COLMAP has a real, documented mechanism for this. A rig, in COLMAP's model, is a multi-sensor system whose sensors hold a fixed relative pose to one reference sensor that defines the rig's origin; a synchronized capture across all sensors at once is a frame. If you know the geometry of your rig ahead of time, you supply it as a JSON config, consumed by `colmap rig_configurator --database_path ... --rig_config_path rig_config.json`. That config carries `cam_from_rig_rotation` (a quaternion) and `cam_from_rig_translation` (xyz) per sensor, plus optionally the camera model name and parameters for each one. Once that's loaded, you can set `--Mapper.ba_refine_sensor_from_rig 0` during bundle adjustment, which locks the relative sensor poses so they don't drift apart during optimization.

That last flag is the entire point of the feature. Bundle adjustment jointly refines 3D points, camera motion, and often intrinsics by minimizing reprojection error across every observation, and it will happily "refine" your rig geometry into something subtly wrong if the visual evidence is thin or misleading. Locking `ba_refine_sensor_from_rig` tells the solver: trust the physical rig, not the pixels, for this relationship.

It's worth noting COLMAP's default path runs the other direction. When you don't know rig geometry in advance, the documented workflow is to reconstruct without rig constraints first, then run `rig_configurator` against the completed reconstruction to average the relative sensor poses out of all the registered images. That's COLMAP inferring the rig from a successful reconstruction. What this book covers is the inverse and much rarer situation: supplying rig geometry manually because the reconstruction can't succeed without it.

## The failure this actually fixes

Manual pose priors solve one class of problem: the sparse model can't converge because it lacks the geometric information to disambiguate camera position, not because features are missing or matches are wrong. The clearest version of this is a degenerate baseline, covered in full in the sibling volume "The Rotation-Only Trap: Diagnosing a Degenerate Baseline." When two viewpoints differ only by rotation, with no real translation between them, every corresponding ray intersects along the same line in space, and there is no parallax left to recover depth or scale from. Standard two-view pose estimation is fundamentally degenerate in that configuration. No amount of better feature matching fixes it, because the information was never in the images.

If your capture pattern for a room forces near-pure-rotation captures at some fixed point (spinning in place for a 360 sweep, for instance, before an Insta360 X4 Air's dual-fisheye stitch even gets involved), COLMAP has nothing to triangulate against at that spot. This is exactly where a known rig geometry earns its keep: if you know, mechanically, how far apart and at what relative orientation those camera positions actually sat, you can hand COLMAP the answer instead of asking it to derive an answer that isn't derivable from the images.

Contrast that with texture-poor rooms, covered in "Common Failure Signatures: Texture-Poor Rooms in the Sparse Model." A blank hallway or a plain drywall room fails for a different reason: there's plenty of potential parallax, but not enough distinctive visual content to find and match features reliably. COLMAP's own FAQ addresses that case directly, recommending DSP-SIFT with affine feature-shape estimation, guided matching, and disabling `ignore_two_view_tracks` so two-view-only tracks still get triangulated. ALIKED features are also noted to produce more repeatable matches than SIFT specifically when overlap is limited, texture is scarce, and illumination shifts are drastic. None of that is a pose-prior problem. It's a feature-matching problem, and it has feature-matching solutions. Reach for manual rig poses on a texture-poor room and you've solved nothing, because the camera positions were always recoverable in principle; the pipeline just couldn't see enough to find them on its own.

So the diagnostic question is not "did registration fail," it's "why did it fail." Rig priors are a targeted fix for degenerate geometry, not a general-purpose rescue for a struggling reconstruction.

## The cost of skipping the diagnosis

Manual pose priors carry a real cost that's easy to underweight: they remove COLMAP's ability to independently verify your rig geometry against the visual evidence. Locking `ba_refine_sensor_from_rig` means any error in your measured rig offsets (a bracket that's shifted a few millimeters, a stitching pipeline that centers the equirectangular projection slightly off from where you assumed) gets baked into the model permanently instead of being corrected by bundle adjustment. You are substituting your own measurement for the solver's optimization, and your measurement has to actually be right.

That's also why getting your rig metadata correct beforehand matters more here than almost anywhere else in the pipeline. The companion volume "Extracting Camera Intrinsics and Rig Metadata from the Bundle" covers how to pull accurate per-camera intrinsics and rig offsets out of your capture bundle before you ever get to this fallback. If those numbers are wrong going in, forcing them as fixed priors doesn't just fail to help, it actively locks a wrong answer into the bundle adjustment where it can no longer be questioned.

There's a second cost worth naming plainly: this book's own source material stops short of one thing. COLMAP's official documentation describes the rig-config JSON schema and the `rig_configurator` workflow, but there isn't a well-documented, general procedure for deriving accurate `cam_from_rig_rotation` and `cam_from_rig_translation` values for an arbitrary handheld or bracket-mounted rig from scratch; that measurement work is genuinely on you, using whatever mechanical or calibration means your specific rig allows.

## What this means for the solo operator

Practically, this is the order of operations. First, look at the actual failure. If it's a texture problem, fix matching parameters or feature choice; that's cheap and doesn't touch pose data at all. If it's a genuinely degenerate baseline, a spot in your walkthrough where the capture geometry itself gives COLMAP nothing to triangulate, that's when you measure your rig, build the rig-config JSON, run `rig_configurator`, and lock the sensor-from-rig relationship during mapping. Do this room by room if you need to, not as a blanket policy across every capture, because most of your rooms will never need it and locking priors you don't need only removes flexibility the solver would otherwise use correctly.

Manual pose priors are not a hack; they're the honest admission that some capture geometries simply don't carry enough information for automatic recovery, and that a rig you built and measured yourself is sometimes a better source of truth than a photograph. Use it exactly there, and nowhere else.
