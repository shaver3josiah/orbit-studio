# Registration Diagnostics as a Feedback Loop to the Field Operator

Where should a registration health score live? The obvious answer is a log file, timestamped and archived next to the raw images, checked by an engineer if something breaks later. That answer is wrong, or at least incomplete, and this book argues the opposite: the score belongs in front of the person who was holding the camera, while they are still standing in the room that produced it.

Here is the shape of the problem. COLMAP's incremental mapper builds a reconstruction by registering images one at a time against the growing model, and it logs this as it happens: lines like "Registering image #241 (230)" followed by "=> Image sees 3755 / 8696 points" (per the PR #2420 discussion of COLMAP's console log format). That ratio, points seen versus points available, is a live signal of how well each image is anchoring into the model. COLMAP even documents that it will happily produce multiple disconnected sub-models if not every image ends up registered into one connected reconstruction (per the COLMAP tutorial docs) rather than failing loudly. A room that didn't register cleanly doesn't throw an error. It just quietly becomes its own island, or gets dropped, and the operator finds out days later when the walkable scene has a hole where the kitchen used to be.

By then the room is gone. The furniture has moved, the light has changed, the crew has left the building. The fix that would have taken ninety seconds on site now requires a callback, an apology, and a second trip. This is the entire case for pushing diagnostics back to the field: every registration failure has a moment when it is cheap to fix and a moment, usually about a day later, when it becomes expensive. The health score's only job is to make sure the operator sees the cheap moment.

Why does the score need a human at all? Because most registration failures trace back to decisions a human made with a camera, and only a human standing in the room can act on that information immediately. Consider the known failure modes documented across the research this library draws on. Weak-texture surfaces, like a blank hallway wall, starve the feature matcher of anything distinctive to lock onto (COLMAP's FAQ). Drastic illumination changes between adjacent shots degrade descriptor matching, since SIFT-family descriptors are built from local intensity gradients that shift with lighting rather than being fully illumination-invariant (the illumination-matching evaluation literature cited alongside COLMAP's own FAQ note on this). And pure rotation, an operator pivoting in place rather than walking through space, is a genuinely degenerate case for structure-from-motion: when two camera positions differ only by rotation, all corresponding rays intersect along the same line and there's no parallax to recover depth from, no matter how good the features are (the rotation-only pose geometry literature). None of these are bugs in COLMAP. They are captures that needed to happen differently, and the operator is the only one who can make them happen differently, because the operator is the only one still in the building.

This is the crux of the argument: a health score sitting in a log is diagnostic information arriving at the wrong end of the pipeline. It tells the engineer what went wrong long after the only person who could fix it has left. Route the same number back to the operator's phone or headset in real time, or at worst at the end of each room, and it becomes something else entirely: a coaching signal. Low score, reshoot this hallway with more overlap. Low score, that room had a window blowing out the exposure, come back with a different angle. The score doesn't need to explain the linear algebra behind bundle adjustment for this to work. It needs to say, clearly and immediately, "this didn't work, try again now."

There's a second, slower effect this produces that a static log can never deliver: it trains the operator. A technician who reshoots a weak-texture hallway once, sees the score improve, and understands why, will start recognizing weak-texture hallways before they shoot them. A technician who pivots in place, gets flagged for it, and re-walks the sequence with real translation will start walking more deliberately on the next job, before any flag appears. This is the actual mechanism of skill development in a field role: fast, specific, repeated feedback tied to a concrete action, not a quarterly review of aggregate statistics. Feed the score back at the point of capture often enough and the operator's baseline technique rises, and the flags themselves become rarer over time. That is the entire point of catching a mistake in the field rather than in the office: it's not just that this room gets fixed, it's that the next fifty rooms get shot better in the first place.

None of this requires operators to become COLMAP experts. The health score can and should be a distillation, and how you build that distillation from raw COLMAP output is its own problem, covered in Scripting a Registration Health Report from COLMAP Output. What this book insists on is only where that report's output goes: not exclusively into an archive for later review, but onto a screen the operator can see before they've left the room. And a low score is not automatically a verdict that the operator failed. Sometimes a low number is a false alarm, or a room that genuinely can't score higher without disproportionate effort, and knowing which is which is a judgment call covered in When to Trust a Low Registration Score and Reshoot Instead. Feeding the raw score straight to the field without that judgment layered in risks nagging operators into reshooting rooms that never needed it, which erodes trust in the signal faster than any bug would.

Building this loop also assumes the diagnostic system and the capture tool are talking about the same rooms, the same image sequences, the same session, in a way that survives the trip through COLMAP and back. That correspondence between what was shot and what gets reconstructed is exactly the concern of The Manifest as a Contract: Keeping Capture and Reconstruction in Sync, and it's a quiet prerequisite for everything argued here. A health score that can't be traced back to the specific room and specific pass that produced it is not actionable, no matter how quickly you deliver it.

The practical implication for anyone building an Orbit Capture tool is simple to state and only moderately annoying to implement: don't treat registration diagnostics as a debugging artifact for engineers. Treat them as a UI element for operators, delivered in the field, phrased as an action rather than a statistic, and tied to the specific room that triggered it. The log file can still exist. It should. But it is the secondary consumer of this data, not the primary one. The primary consumer is the person who can still do something about it, standing in the room, camera in hand, before the light changes and the moment is gone.
