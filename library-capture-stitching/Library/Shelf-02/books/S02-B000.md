# The Reconstruction Shelf: From a Bundle of Crops to a Walkable Splat

Shelf 01 ends the moment a solo operator zips up a folder of crops and a manifest and walks away from the building. This shelf starts exactly there, with `bundle.zip` sitting on disk, and it owns everything between that zip file and a Gaussian splat someone can fly a virtual camera through. That territory has a specific shape: stitch dual-fisheye frames into equirectangular panoramas where the capture rig demands it, recover camera poses and a sparse point cloud with COLMAP, refine that model with bundle adjustment, export it into a form gsplat can train on, and watch that training turn points into renderable splats. Every stage depends on the one before it having told the truth, and every stage leaves a diagnostic trail that says whether it did.

For a solo operator, this shelf matters because it is where a bad walkthrough finally gets caught, or doesn't. A registration health score, a count of disconnected sparse models, a training loss curve that refuses to drop: these are the pipeline's only way of saying "something you shot didn't work," and reading them correctly is what separates a fast reshoot from a wasted afternoon of retrained splats that were never going to look right. For the engineer building the tool, this shelf is the actual software: the ingestion module, the orchestration script, the retry and logging layer that keeps a batch of real jobs from dying quietly at 2 a.m.

## The foundational cluster: what each stage is, in plain terms

The first ten books exist to name the pieces before anything gets scripted. "From Photos to a Walkable Scene: The Reconstruction Pipeline at a Glance" lays out the whole chain in one pass, stitch or ingest, recover pose, train a splat, ship a scene, so that every later book has a place to slot into. "Structure from Motion in Plain Terms: What COLMAP Actually Solves" and "Feature Matching: How Software Finds the Same Corner Twice" explain the two-eyed trick underneath COLMAP: find the same physical point in two photos, and the geometry of where those two rays cross tells you where that point sits in space. "Bundle Adjustment: Refining Pose and Points Together" covers the optimization pass that reconciles every pose and every point into one mutually consistent model once the rough version exists.

A second thread in this cluster is specific to the Insta360 lane of capture. "Equirectangular Projection: Mapping a Sphere onto a 2:1 Rectangle" and "Dual-Fisheye Capture and Why the Insta360 X4 Air Needs a Stitch Step" explain why an X4 Air's two fisheye sensors have to be merged into one flat panorama before anything downstream can touch the footage, and why that panorama's aspect ratio is a hard 2:1, not a suggestion. "What a Gaussian Splat Is and Why It Needs a Baseline" closes the conceptual half of this cluster by naming the one requirement that ties stitching and structure-from-motion together in opposite directions: panorama stitching wants pure rotation, splat training wants real translation and parallax, and confusing the two is the single most common way a capture quietly fails.

The remaining foundational books are the first code. "Reading the Bundle: Opening manifest.json and crops/*.jpg in Python" writes the smallest useful reader, using nothing but `zipfile` and `json`, that confirms a bundle is structurally sound before it goes near a reconstruction. "Setting Up a COLMAP + gsplat Environment in Python 3.11" gets the toolchain every later script assumes actually installed and working. "A First Pass: Feeding a Bundle Straight Through COLMAP" runs an unmodified bundle through COLMAP's automatic reconstructor end to end, deliberately with no tuning, so you have a baseline before you start adjusting anything.

## The applied cluster: reading what the pipeline is telling you

Once the pipeline runs, it starts talking back, and this cluster teaches you to listen. "Reading a Registration Health Score: <60% Warning, ~95% Healthy" is the load-bearing volume here: it explains what that single percentage actually measures (the fraction of images that got a solved camera pose) and is honest that the 60/95 thresholds are a product convention layered on top of COLMAP's own numbers, not something COLMAP defines itself. "Why Some Rooms Register and Others Don't" names the four things that starve a registration: texture, overlap, exposure, and motion, and shows how a blank-walled bathroom, a rushed hallway, a sunlit atrium, and a camera panned instead of walked are four different failures wearing the same low score.

Around that core sit the books that turn a bad score into a diagnosis rather than a shrug: "Diagnosing a Failed COLMAP Run from Its Sparse Output," "Loop Closure and Why COLMAP Needs to Recognize Return Visits," "The Rotation-Only Trap: Diagnosing a Degenerate Baseline," and the pair on exposure and white balance drift across rooms. On the code side, this cluster also scripts the controllable version of COLMAP's stages ("Running COLMAP Feature Extraction and Matching from a Script," "Choosing COLMAP Matcher Settings for Indoor Room Sequences," "Running Incremental Mapping and Reading the Reconstruction Stats") and turns the raw numbers into the health report itself in "Scripting a Registration Health Report from COLMAP Output." "When to Trust a Low Registration Score and Reshoot Instead" is where this cluster resolves into a decision rule: a mid-range score sometimes means tune a parameter, and sometimes means an entire wing of the building never made it into the model, and only looking at which images dropped out tells you which.

## The implementation cluster: building the actual pipeline

This is the largest cluster on the shelf, and it is where the essays above become running code. "Building the Bundle Ingestion Module: manifest.json to a COLMAP Project" turns a validated bundle into a proper COLMAP project directory. "Parameterizing COLMAP Runs Per Rig Type from manifest.json" and "Extracting Camera Intrinsics and Rig Metadata from the Bundle" make that ingestion rig-aware instead of guessing camera parameters blind. "Exporting COLMAP Poses and Point Clouds for gsplat Training" is the bridge book: it reads `cameras.bin`, `images.bin`, and `points3D.bin` through `pycolmap`, inverts each camera's pose into the camera-to-world form every trainer expects, and writes a flat manifest plus point-cloud archive that doesn't require linking against COLMAP at all. "Setting Up a gsplat Training Run from a COLMAP Sparse Model," "Monitoring gsplat Training Loss and Point Cloud Growth," and "Exporting a Trained Splat for a Walkable Viewer" carry the pipeline the rest of the way to a scene someone can actually walk through.

Everything in this cluster converges on "Writing an End-to-End Bundle-to-Splat Orchestration Script," which chains ingest, reconstruct, and train into one script that takes a `bundle.zip` in and a trained splat out, treating each stage as a subprocess with its own log file rather than something to babysit line by line. Alongside it sit the harder edge cases that a single-session, single-room assumption doesn't cover: "Handling Multi-Session Buildings: Merging Separate Scans" and "Merging Overlapping Room Scans into One Coordinate Frame" for buildings shot across more than one visit, and "The Manifest as a Contract: Keeping Capture and Reconstruction in Sync," which argues that `manifest.json` is the real interface between Shelf 01's world and this shelf's, and has to change on both sides together or not at all.

## The advanced cluster: the edge cases that only show up at scale

The last five books are what breaks once you are running this pipeline on real buildings, over and over, rather than on one clean demo. "Handling Repeating Geometry: Avoiding False Loop Closures in COLMAP" solves the failure mode where two visually identical but physically distinct rooms fool the reconstruction into merging them. "Tuning gsplat for Low-Texture and Low-Light Interiors" adjusts densification and opacity settings for the sparse, noisy point clouds that bad lighting and blank walls produce, rather than pretending every interior behaves like a well-lit showroom. "When to Fall Back to Manual Pose Priors from the Rig" covers the genuine last resort, feeding COLMAP known rig poses when automatic recovery simply won't converge. "Building a Continuous Registration Health Dashboard Across Scans" and "Hardening the Pipeline: Retrying, Logging, and Failing Loud" close the shelf by turning a script that works once into a system that survives a fifteenth real job: retries for genuinely transient failures, structured JSON-lines logging instead of scraped stdout, and loud, immediate failure (a stalled training loss, a suspiciously low registration fraction) instead of quietly handing bad output downstream.

## A reading path through the shelf

Start with "From Photos to a Walkable Scene: The Reconstruction Pipeline at a Glance" to get the whole shape in your head before any single stage swallows your attention. From there, read the rest of the foundational cluster roughly in order, essays before code: the SfM and feature-matching pair, then bundle adjustment, then the Gaussian-splat and equirectangular essays, then the three foundational code books ending on a first unmodified COLMAP run. That gives you a working pipeline and a vocabulary for what it just did.

Move into the applied cluster next, and read "Reading a Registration Health Score" and "Why Some Rooms Register and Others Don't" back to back; those two books do more to make the rest of the shelf legible than anything else on it. The remaining applied essays and scripts are worth reading in full once, then treated as reference: come back to "Choosing COLMAP Matcher Settings for Indoor Room Sequences" or "Reading a Pose Visualization" when a specific run looks wrong, rather than trying to hold all of it in memory up front.

The implementation cluster is where you actually build the tool, and it rewards being read in dependency order rather than book-number order: ingestion, then rig parameterization and intrinsics, then the export and training pair, then the orchestration script that ties them together, and only then the multi-session and manifest-contract books once a single-session pipeline is solid. Treat the advanced cluster as reference material from day one: you likely don't need "Handling Repeating Geometry" or "Tuning gsplat for Low-Texture and Low-Light Interiors" until a specific building forces the issue, but you'll want to know they exist before that building shows up, and "Hardening the Pipeline" is worth a first read as soon as you intend to run this on more than one job unattended.

## What carries over from Shelf 01

Shelf 01 is where reconstruction quality actually gets decided, not fixed. Everything this shelf's diagnostics catch, a low registration score, a rotation-only baseline, a texture-poor room that splits the sparse model in two, traces back to a specific discipline (or lapse of it) during capture: overlap, standoff, locked exposure, steady translation through the room. Carry forward the four failure modes named in "Why Some Rooms Register and Others Don't" (texture, overlap, exposure, motion) as the same four things Shelf 01 teaches you to get right on-site, and carry forward `manifest.json` itself as the literal contract between the two shelves: the crops, the rig metadata, and the project structure that Shelf 01's capture app produces are exactly what "Reading the Bundle" and "Building the Bundle Ingestion Module" expect to receive. When this shelf's health score comes back low, the fix usually isn't a parameter on this side of the seam. It's a reshoot, governed by what Shelf 01 already taught you to watch for in the room.
