# What a Gaussian Splat Is and Why It Needs a Baseline

Every Orbit Capture session ends at the same question: what does gsplat actually train, and why does it care so much about how you moved through the room while shooting?

Start with the representation itself. A Gaussian splat is not a mesh and not a photograph stitched into a sphere. It is a scene made of thousands or millions of individual 3D Gaussians, each one a small soft-edged blob with a position, an orientation, a size in three dimensions, an opacity, and a color that can shift with viewing angle. The original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper (Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler, and Drettakis, 2023) proposed this as a full replacement for the implicit neural fields used by NeRF-style methods. Instead of asking a neural network "what color is this point in space," you place explicit, editable Gaussians directly in space and let a rasterizer draw them. That rasterizer is fast: the paper reports real-time rendering at 1080p, 30 frames per second or more, for complete unbounded real scenes, with quality matching or slightly beating Mip-NeRF360 while training in roughly 35 to 45 minutes against Mip-NeRF360's roughly 48 hours. gsplat, the open-source library this pipeline actually runs on, refines that further. It cuts memory use to about a quarter of the original implementation on standard benchmarks and trains up to 10 percent faster at equivalent quality, with an alternative MCMC densification strategy pushing memory down even further by capping the total Gaussian count outright.

None of that speed comes free of a starting point. A splat is not painted from nothing. Both the original paper and gsplat's own strategy documentation are explicit that Gaussians are seeded from a sparse point cloud, then split, cloned, and pruned during training based on how much each one is struggling to explain the images (its accumulated gradient, its size, its opacity). If you have read "From Photos to a Walkable Scene: The Reconstruction Pipeline at a Glance," you already know where that seed point cloud comes from: COLMAP's structure-from-motion stage, which triangulates a sparse cloud of 3D points along with a camera pose for every registered photo. gsplat and nerfstudio's Splatfacto both confirm this dependency directly: Splatfacto's own documentation states it works much better when initialized from real SfM geometry than from random initialization, which yields visibly lower quality. This is why the two halves of Orbit Capture's pipeline are inseparable. COLMAP does not just find where the camera was. It hands gsplat the skeleton the splat grows on.

Here is where the baseline problem enters, and it is worth being precise about what "baseline" means before going further: it is the physical distance between two camera positions, not a setting you toggle in software. Triangulation, the method COLMAP uses to place a 3D point in space, works by finding where two or more camera rays intersect. Two rays from two different vantage points pin down a single point through parallax, the same way your two eyes, offset by a few centimeters, let you judge depth. Take that offset away and the geometry collapses. The literature on degenerate two-view configurations makes the failure mode exact: when two camera positions differ only by rotation, with no translation between them, every corresponding ray from the two views lies along the same line in space rather than crossing it. There is no intersection to solve for, no parallax to disambiguate distance, and so no depth and no scale can be recovered from that pair, no matter how sharp the images are or how many features match between them. Standard essential-matrix pose estimation is documented as becoming degenerate under exactly this condition.

This is not a hypothetical edge case for Orbit Capture, because pure rotation is precisely what a 360 camera captures by design when you spin it on a tripod. Panorama stitching's whole mathematical foundation, going back to classic automatic stitching techniques, assumes the camera rotates about a single optical center with no translation at all, so that one homography can align every frame into a seamless sphere. That is a feature for building a panorama. It is a liability for building a splat, because the same assumption that makes stitching clean is the assumption that starves triangulation of the parallax it needs. A capture session that never moves the camera, only rotates it, can produce a beautiful equirectangular image and a completely unusable COLMAP reconstruction, because every pair of views in that sequence sits in the degenerate case described above. The sibling volume "Baseline Requirements for gsplat: Why Pure Rotation Fails Training" works through this failure in more operational depth: what it looks like in a COLMAP log, and how much actual translation between capture points is enough to recover the scene reliably.

The practical implication for a solo operator walking a building room by room is simple to state and easy to forget in the field: movement between shots is not incidental to the workflow, it is the workflow. Whether you are shooting overlapping stills with a locked-exposure phone or capturing 360 frames with an Insta360 X4 Air that gets stitched from dual fisheye to equirectangular afterward, the camera has to physically relocate between captures, not merely pivot. A tripod spin at the center of a room, no matter how many frames it produces, gives COLMAP nothing to triangulate against itself; it only becomes useful once it is one node among several taken from different floor positions. Once COLMAP has that translation to work with, it can output the sparse point cloud and camera poses that gsplat needs as a seed, and the details of how those poses and points actually get formatted and handed off are covered in "Exporting COLMAP Poses and Point Clouds for gsplat Training."

One honest limitation in these notes: the sources here document gsplat's training mechanics, densification strategy, and its dependency on SfM seeding in solid detail, but they do not include gsplat's exact rasterization function signature or call-level API, so this book stops at the conceptual and geometric level rather than the code level.

What this means for the reader is a shift in how you think about a capture session before you ever open COLMAP or gsplat. A Gaussian splat is a photograph of parallax as much as it is a photograph of a room. The blobs, the colors, the training curves are all downstream of one physical fact captured or not captured on site: did the camera actually move. Walk the room. Do not just spin in place and call it done.
