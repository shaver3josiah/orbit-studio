# From Point Cloud to Splat: What Training Actually Optimizes

When gsplat trains on your walkthrough, what is it actually changing, iteration by iteration, to turn a sparse point cloud into a scene you can walk through?

It helps to start from what it is not doing. It is not solving for camera positions again, that job already happened in COLMAP. By the time gsplat opens your workspace, the poses in `images.bin` and the points in `points3D.bin` are fixed history: COLMAP's bundle adjustment already refined camera motion and 3D point coordinates jointly against reprojection error, as the sibling volume on bundle adjustment describes for the SfM stage. gsplat inherits that geometry as a starting condition, not as something it keeps negotiating over. What it optimizes instead is a different object entirely: a cloud of 3D Gaussians, sometimes called splats, each one a small blob of color and opacity floating in space, and the four things training adjusts on every one of them are position, covariance, opacity, and color, exactly as described in the original Gaussian splatting paper (3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering, Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler, and Drettakis, 2023). That paper is the one to read alongside this book for how a splat is defined in the first place; this one is about what happens to it once training starts, which is the territory the companion volume on monitoring loss and point cloud growth watches from the outside.

Start with position. Each Gaussian begins at a 3D point COLMAP triangulated, but that starting point was optimized for camera geometry, not for how the scene actually looks when rendered from novel angles. Training nudges each splat's center a little at each step, following the gradient of a rendering loss: render the scene from a training camera's known pose, compare the result pixel by pixel to the real photo taken from that pose, and push every visible splat's position (along with its other properties) in the direction that would have made the render closer to the photo. Do this across thousands of iterations and thousands of camera views, and splats drift into the positions that best explain everything the cameras actually saw, not just where the point cloud happened to seed them.

Covariance is the shape parameter, and it is arguably the more interesting one because it is where "point cloud" stops being an accurate mental model. A Gaussian's covariance controls its scale and orientation in 3D, meaning a splat is not a sphere, it is an ellipsoid that can stretch long and thin to represent an edge, or flatten to represent a wall, or shrink to a near-point to represent fine detail. The 3DGS paper describes this as anisotropic covariance optimization, and it is one of the paper's three core contributions alongside the SfM-seeded initialization and the tile-based rasterizer. Training adjusts covariance the same way it adjusts position, through the same rendering-loss gradient, so a splat that starts as a rough blob at a COLMAP point ends up shaped like whatever local surface geometry actually reduces reprojection error across all the views that see it. This is also where things can go wrong in a way worth naming: long, spikey Gaussians are a known artifact of this optimization, called out specifically in nerfstudio's Splatfacto documentation, and they get controlled with scale regularization during training rather than fixed after the fact.

Opacity is the simplest of the four to describe and the one most directly tied to whether a splat survives training at all. Each Gaussian carries an opacity value, and low-opacity splats contribute almost nothing to any rendered image, which means their gradient signal is weak and they tend to drift toward zero. gsplat's default densification strategy formalizes this with a hard floor, `prune_opa` at 0.005 by default in the Strategy API, below which a splat is removed outright. Opacity optimization is therefore not just about how solid a surface looks, it is the mechanism that continuously culls the population, and it works alongside a separate reset step, `reset_every` at 3000 iterations by default, that periodically knocks all opacities back down and forces the optimizer to re-earn them, which prevents splats from coasting on stale high-opacity values that no longer reflect what the renders need.

Color is the fourth parameter, encoded per splat (commonly as spherical harmonics coefficients, so a splat's apparent color can shift with viewing angle rather than being a single flat RGB value), and it is optimized on the same per-pixel comparison as everything else. A splat's color adjusts to match what cameras actually recorded from every direction they saw it, which is how gsplat picks up view-dependent effects like a glossy floor catching different light from different rooms, or a window that looks blown out from one angle and merely bright from another.

All four of these move together, every iteration, driven by one loss signal: render, compare to a real photo, backpropagate the pixel error into position, covariance, opacity, and color simultaneously. There is no stage where gsplat fixes color and only tunes shape, or fixes position and only tunes opacity, they are one optimization problem with four kinds of unknowns. What makes this workable instead of an intractable mess is the same thing that makes bundle adjustment workable in COLMAP: gradient descent doesn't need a closed-form answer, it needs a loss surface it can descend, and the fast tile-based rasterizer in gsplat (and the original 3DGS work before it) makes rendering fast enough to compute that gradient thousands of times over.

The other half of what gsplat does alongside adjusting these four parameters is deciding how many splats exist at all, since the initial COLMAP point cloud is rarely the right density everywhere. gsplat implements this as densification strategies, per the gsplat library paper: the original ADC approach splits or clones a Gaussian when its accumulated positional gradient crosses a threshold (`grow_grad2d`, 0.0002 by default), an Absgrad variant that avoids gradient sign-cancellation between opposing views, and an MCMC strategy that treats the whole population as samples from a distribution and adds noise instead of explicit split-or-clone decisions, capping the total count directly (`cap_max` at one million Gaussians by default). Position, covariance, opacity, and color are what training adjusts on each existing splat; densification is the separate but simultaneous process of deciding where new splats need to be created or old ones removed, and it runs on its own schedule, `refine_start_iter` at 500 through `refine_stop_iter` at 15000 for the default strategy, checked every `refine_every`, 100 iterations. Watching that population size change over training, alongside the loss curve, is exactly what the companion volume on monitoring gsplat training covers.

None of this matters unless it produces something you can actually load into a viewer at the end, and that is a different problem: exporting the trained splat cleanly is covered in its own volume, since the parameters described here are runtime training state, not yet a portable file. But understanding what the optimizer is touching changes how you read a stalled or noisy training run. If loss plateaus early, it is worth asking whether it is a position problem (splats stuck in the wrong place because the initial point cloud registration was thin), a covariance problem (surfaces staying blobby because there is not enough view overlap to constrain their shape), or an opacity and densification problem (too few splats surviving the prune threshold in a detailed region). The four parameters give you a vocabulary for that diagnosis instead of treating a bad splat as one undifferentiated failure.
