# Dual-Fisheye Capture and Why the Insta360 X4 Air Needs a Stitch Step

Why does a single button press on a 360 camera produce two photos instead of one? That is the question this book answers, because it is the first thing an operator hits when they point an Insta360 X4 Air at a room and expect a walkable scene to fall out the other end.

The X4 Air captures its 360-degree field of view with two fisheye lenses mounted back to back, each on a 1/1.8-inch sensor, shooting up to 8K30fps in 360 mode ("Insta360 X4 Air — product announcement / specs"). One lens looks forward, one looks backward, and each one bulges its view into the extreme wide-angle distortion that "fisheye" implies: straight lines curve, the edges of the frame compress, and the useful field of view balloons past what a normal lens could cover. In single-lens mode the same hardware can shoot one of those lenses alone at an ultra-wide 170-degree field of view, up to 4K60fps. But 360 mode uses both lenses at once, and that is where the two-file problem starts. Each fisheye lens covers more than a hemisphere, so their fields of view overlap generously where they meet on the sides, and each produces its own raw file. Two files, two projections, two centers of perspective. Nothing about a raw fisheye pair is a usable panorama yet.

A panorama, in the format that every downstream tool in this pipeline expects, is an equirectangular image: a flat rectangle where the horizontal axis is longitude and the vertical axis is latitude, mapped with no further transformation or scaling, so pixel position corresponds linearly to pan and tilt angle ("Equirectangular Projection — PanoTools.org Wiki"). That rectangle also has a hard shape requirement: width must be exactly twice height, a strict 2:1 ratio, in order to cover the full 360 degrees horizontally and 180 degrees vertically. Google's own photo-sphere metadata standard confirms the same convention from the consumer side: a full, uncropped equirectangular photo sphere example runs 4000 by 2000 pixels, precisely 2:1, and the GPano XMP fields (FullPanoWidthPixels, FullPanoHeightPixels, and the cropped-area fields for partial spheres) all assume that rectangle as the reference frame ("Photo Sphere XMP Metadata (GPano) — Google for Developers"). The book Equirectangular Projection: Mapping a Sphere onto a 2:1 Rectangle goes deeper into why that shape and that mapping are the accepted contract, but the short version is: nothing reads your panorama correctly unless it already looks like that rectangle. A raw fisheye photo, circular and distorted, does not.

So two overlapping fisheye circles have to become one flat 2:1 rectangle before the image is usable for anything, whether that is viewing the panorama in a phone app or feeding it into the room-by-room capture pipeline this library documents. That transformation is the stitch. It has to find where the two lenses' overlapping content matches, warp each fisheye image into its share of the equirectangular grid, and blend the seam so it disappears into the surrounding scene rather than reading as a visible scar down the middle of a wall or across a doorway.

It is worth being precise about what kind of matching problem stitching is, because it is a different problem from the structure-from-motion matching that COLMAP does later in the pipeline, and the difference is not incidental. Panorama stitching assumes the camera only rotates about its optical center, or that the scene is roughly planar, which is exactly the pure-rotation case: a single fixed viewpoint, images that differ only in the direction they are looking, aligned with one homography per overlap region (image-stitching survey literature, e.g. quasi-homography warps, arXiv 1701.08006). That assumption holds close to perfectly for a 360 camera's two lenses, because they are rigidly mounted a few millimeters apart on the same body looking in opposite directions from what is, for practical purposes, one point in space. When two camera positions differ only by rotation and not by translation, all corresponding rays intersect along the same line, so there is no parallax to disambiguate depth or scale (pure rotation as a degenerate two-view geometry configuration). That is a liability for structure-from-motion, which depends on parallax to triangulate 3D points, but it is exactly the assumption panorama stitching relies on to succeed. The dual-fisheye stitch is not trying to recover depth. It is trying to align two views of the same sphere from what is effectively one shared viewpoint, and pure rotation is the friendly case for that job, not the hostile one.

Insta360's own stitching engine leans on that overlap deliberately. It uses an optical-flow-based algorithm that compares the overlapping regions of each lens's image, and for video, adjacent frames from the same lens, to route the seam around actual scene content instead of along a fixed geometric line ("Insta360 optical-flow stitching explainer," flagged in the shelf notes as based on a search-engine summary after a direct fetch returned HTTP 403, so treat the exact wording as approximate rather than quoted). Insta360 offers this at multiple quality and speed tiers, from a basic Optical Flow mode up through a faster "New Optical Flow" mode and an AI Flow mode built on their Disflow technology. The company's SDK documentation separates the concern cleanly into two parts: a Camera SDK that handles connection, shooting control, and file listing, and a Media SDK that handles preview, stitching, and export, with an explicit note that in-camera video stitching is not supported for the unstitched dual-fisheye stream, meaning stitching for video has to go through the Media SDK regardless ("Insta360 Developer Resources — SDK Guide"). Stills follow the same logic: what the camera writes to its memory card after a 360 shot is the dual-fisheye pair, not a finished equirectangular JPG, and something has to run the stitch before that JPG exists.

For a solo operator walking a building room by room, this has a direct practical consequence. The files coming off the X4 Air in 360 mode are not yet the JPGs this library's capture pipeline expects to feed into COLMAP. They need to pass through a stitching step first, whether that is Insta360's own desktop or mobile app, or an automated call into the Media SDK, before they become the flat 2:1 equirectangular frames that a structure-from-motion and Gaussian-splat pipeline can treat like any other photograph of the scene. Skip that step and you have a folder of fisheye pairs that no downstream tool recognizes as a coherent image at all.

The companion volumes handle the two sides of actually doing this: Stitching Dual-Fisheye to Equirectangular with Insta360 Tools walks through doing the stitch by hand with Insta360's own software, and Automating the Dual-Fisheye-to-Equirect Stitch Step in the Pipeline covers folding that same conversion into an unattended batch process so the operator never has to think about it shot by shot. What this book asks you to carry forward is simpler: the X4 Air's two lenses are not a redundancy or a gimmick, they are the mechanism by which it captures a full sphere at all, and the stitch is not an optional polish pass. It is the step that turns two distorted, overlapping fisheye circles into the one flat rectangle every other tool in this pipeline is built to read.
