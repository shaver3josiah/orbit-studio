# Structure from Motion in Plain Terms: What COLMAP Actually Solves

Point a phone at a room, walk it around, and take a hundred overlapping stills. What do you actually have afterward? A hundred flat, unrelated grids of pixels. None of them know where they were taken from. None of them know how far away anything in the frame is. And yet somehow, a piece of software called COLMAP can take that pile of JPGs and hand back two things: a precise 3D position and orientation for every camera that took a picture, and a sparse cloud of 3D points marking where real surfaces sat in the room. That process is called structure from motion, and understanding what it solves, not just that it works, is the difference between operating your capture rig and being mystified by it when it fails.

Start with the question underneath the question: why is this hard at all? A single photo is a projection. It takes the three-dimensional world and flattens it onto a two-dimensional sensor, and in doing so it throws away depth. You cannot look at one photo of a room and know if the doorway is two meters away or ten, not with certainty, because the same 2D pattern of pixels could be produced by a small nearby wall or a large distant one. One image is fundamentally ambiguous about the third dimension. This is the same reason a single eye struggles with depth perception while two eyes, with their slightly different vantage points, do not.

That's the trick structure from motion borrows from your own binocular vision, and it's the same trick behind the sibling volume on Feature Matching: How Software Finds the Same Corner Twice. If you can identify the same physical point in the room, say the top corner of a window frame, in two different photos taken from two different positions, and you know roughly how those two photos relate, then you have parallax. The window corner appears in a slightly different place relative to the frame edges in each photo, and that difference encodes distance. Two rays, one from each camera through its image of the corner, meet at a single point in space. Where they meet is where the corner actually is.

But here's the circularity that makes the problem genuinely interesting: to triangulate that 3D point, you need to know where the two cameras were. And to know where the cameras were, you'd normally want to know what's in the scene. COLMAP has neither at the start. It has to recover both at once, from nothing but the overlap between images. That's what "structure from motion" names literally: structure (the point cloud) recovered jointly with motion (the sequence of camera poses), each one solved using the other as leverage, bootstrapped from a scene COLMAP has never seen.

The official COLMAP tutorial describes this as unfolding in three stages. First, feature detection and extraction: the software scans every image and picks out distinctive local patches, corners, textured blobs, anything a computer can recognize again later, and encodes each one as a compact descriptor. Second, feature matching and geometric verification: pairs of images get compared, candidate correspondences are proposed based on how similar their descriptors are, and then those correspondences get checked against the rules of actual camera geometry to throw out coincidental look-alikes. Third, incremental structure-and-motion reconstruction itself, where images get registered into a shared 3D coordinate system one at a time and new points get triangulated as they go.

That third stage is the heart of it, and it's worth walking through concretely, because "incremental" is a specific strategy, not just a word. COLMAP does not try to solve for all cameras and all points simultaneously from a cold start, that would be numerically unstable with nothing to anchor it. Instead, according to the tutorial, it seeds the whole reconstruction from a single well-chosen initial pair of images, images with strong, reliable matches between them, and triangulates the first handful of 3D points from that pair alone. Then it grows the model one image at a time: take an unregistered image, find which of its detected features match 3D points already in the growing cloud, and if there are enough good matches, solve for where that camera must have been standing to see those points from that angle. Once the new camera is placed, triangulate any additional points its matches reveal that weren't visible before, and repeat with the next image. It's less like solving an equation and more like assembling a puzzle where each new piece has to agree with everything already on the table.

This incremental approach is COLMAP's default and its most battle-tested path, robust across a huge range of real capture conditions, but it has a cost: repeated bundle adjustment, the optimization pass covered in Bundle Adjustment: Refining Pose and Points Together, that periodically re-tightens all the poses and points solved so far, is the bottleneck, and it gets slower as the image count grows because every new image potentially perturbs everything before it. COLMAP does offer an alternative for that reason. A global pipeline, including the GLOMAP method now integrated as COLMAP's global mapper, instead performs rotation averaging across the whole network of image-to-image relationships at once, solving all camera orientations simultaneously rather than growing the model image by image, and can be markedly faster on large collections, though it tends to want more peak memory during that joint solve and leans more heavily on having decent focal-length estimates going in. A hierarchical mapper exists as a middle path too, partitioning a large scene into overlapping submodels, reconstructing each one, and merging them back together afterward. Whichever mapper does the work, the output is the same shape: a set of cameras.bin and images.bin entries recording pose per photo, and a points3D.bin file recording the sparse cloud, together with the mean reprojection error for each point once bundle adjustment has run.

There is one genuine failure mode worth naming plainly here, because it explains a lot of confusing behavior in the field. Not every photo you feed COLMAP is guaranteed to end up in the final model. If an image simply doesn't share enough reliable matched points with anything already registered, whether from motion blur, a blank wall, or a lighting change between rooms, COLMAP will not force it in. Per the tutorial, this can even split your capture into multiple disconnected models rather than one unified scene, and the documented remedies are running denser matching or manually specifying which pair of images should seed the reconstruction. This is the direct, mechanical reason overlap discipline while shooting matters as much as it does: every image needs enough shared, trackable content with its neighbors to be pulled into the same puzzle, or it gets left on the table.

Zoom out and the payoff of all this is concrete rather than academic. What COLMAP hands back, cameras with known poses plus a sparse point cloud, is not the finished walkable scene. It's the scaffolding the next stage needs. A Gaussian-splat trainer takes that same sparse point cloud as its literal starting point, seeding each initial Gaussian at a real 3D location the reconstruction already found, rather than guessing blindly, which is why splat quality traces back so directly to how solid this stage was. Get the structure-from-motion stage right, meaning enough overlap, enough distinct features, and a clean registration of nearly every shot, and everything downstream has real geometry to build on. Get it wrong and no amount of clever training later can invent depth the photos never captured. The full arc from stills to scene is the subject of From Photos to a Walkable Scene: The Reconstruction Pipeline at a Glance, but this stage is where the three-dimensional world first gets pulled back out of a stack of flat images, and it happens through nothing more exotic than finding the same corner twice and doing the geometry that follows from it.
