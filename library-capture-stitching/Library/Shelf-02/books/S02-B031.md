# Interpreting Bundle Adjustment Residuals as a Health Signal

You have just run a capture. COLMAP has chewed through the images, registered most of them, and produced a sparse model. The question that matters right now is not "did it work" in some final sense, it's "should I trust this enough to keep going into dense reconstruction and gsplat training, or should I walk back into the building and reshoot room 4." The full registration score answers a version of that question, but it answers it late, after the entire mapper run has finished. There's an earlier signal sitting inside the same output, and it's worth learning to read: the reprojection residual that bundle adjustment leaves behind on every triangulated point.

## What the residual actually is

Bundle adjustment is the optimization step that takes COLMAP's initial estimates of camera poses and 3D point positions and refines them together, jointly, against the actual pixel observations of those points across every image that sees them. The name comes from the bundles of light rays converging on each camera's optical center; the adjustment nudges cameras and points until those rays agree with each other as well as possible. This process is almost always the final refinement step of a feature-based reconstruction pipeline, which is exactly why its leftover error is such a useful diagnostic: it's the last thing computed before the reconstruction is called done for that stage.

The concrete number lives in COLMAP's points3D output. Each 3D point in points3D.txt carries an ERROR field, which is the mean reprojection error in pixels for that point, averaged across every image in its track. This field is not populated as a side effect of triangulation; it's only meaningful after a global bundle adjustment has actually run. That's an important detail for a health check: read the ERROR column before bundle adjustment has run over the current state of the model, and you're reading stale or placeholder numbers, not a real signal.

Reprojection error in pixels is a satisfying unit to reason about because it maps directly to what you'd see if you overlaid a point's predicted image location on top of where the matching feature was actually detected. A point with an ERROR of 0.4 pixels is essentially nailed down: the geometry agrees with the images to well under a pixel. A point sitting at 3 or 4 pixels of error is being pulled in inconsistent directions by its observations, which usually means something upstream is wrong, not that bundle adjustment did a bad job. Bundle adjustment doesn't create error; it exposes error that was already latent in the matches feeding it.

## Why this is worth checking before the registration score

The registration score you'd normally check is essentially "what fraction of my images made it into the model." That's a real signal, but it's a summary statistic computed at the very end of the mapper's incremental growth, image by image. The companion volume Running Incremental Mapping and Reading the Reconstruction Stats covers reading that per-image growth as it happens, including COLMAP's console log lines like "Registering image #241 (230)" followed by "=> Image sees 3755 / 8696 points," which give a registered-versus-total-points ratio per image as the model grows. Reprojection residuals are a different axis on the same problem: instead of asking whether an image got in at all, they ask how well the points that did make it in actually agree with their observations.

The practical reason to look at residuals early is that a bad capture can still produce a deceptively high registration percentage. Images can register against a weak, poorly constrained local geometry and still count as "registered." A residual distribution that's drifting upward, or that has a long tail of high-error points concentrated in one area of the model, tells you the geometry underneath those registrations is shaky well before the overall percentage would flag it. For a solo operator walking room to room, that distinction matters: you want to know the capture of room 4 was weak while you're still in the building, not after you've driven home and started a dense reconstruction on a model that's technically "95% registered" but structurally soft in one wing.

## Reading the shape of the residuals, not just the average

A single mean reprojection error for the whole model is a blunt instrument. What's more useful is looking at where the error concentrates. Since points3D.txt lists a TRACK for every point, the images and features that contributed to it, you can bucket high-error points by which images they appear in and get a rough map of which parts of the capture are unhealthy. A spike of high-residual points whose tracks all pass through the same handful of images is a strong hint that those images have a matching problem, not a general bundle adjustment problem.

That matching problem is often illumination-driven. Descriptor-based matching, including the SIFT-family descriptors COLMAP uses by default, is not fully illumination-invariant: it tolerates a limited range of exposure change before match quality measurably degrades, and COLMAP's own FAQ documentation calls out drastic illumination changes as a condition where matching gets fragile. This is why the capture discipline behind Orbit Capture insists on locked exposure across a room walkthrough. If exposure or white balance drifts between adjacent images, particularly across a room-to-room boundary where lighting genuinely changes, that shift can produce matches that hold together well enough to survive geometric verification but leave elevated residuals after bundle adjustment. That specific room-boundary framing is an inference from general descriptor-illumination sensitivity rather than a directly published finding, and it's worth treating as a working hypothesis to test against your own capture data rather than settled fact.

The other classic source of a residual spike is repetitive structure, hallways, rows of identical doors, matching cabinetry. This is the reconstruction-geometry analogue of the false loop closure problem that shows up in SLAM: visually similar but spatially distinct places can produce near-identical descriptors, and if enough of those ambiguous matches get accepted, the geometry they're forced into fights itself under bundle adjustment. You won't always see a total registration failure from this. You'll sometimes see a model that fully registers and then carries a stubborn cluster of high-residual points exactly where the repetitive geometry sits, which is a useful thing to know is a distinct failure mode from the sparse-output symptoms covered in Diagnosing a Failed COLMAP Run from Its Sparse Output.

## What to actually do with the number

In practice this becomes a simple triage habit. After a mapper run, before committing to dense reconstruction or a gsplat training pass, pull the ERROR column out of points3D.txt (or the equivalent Reconstruction object if you're scripting against pycolmap) and look at two things: the overall distribution, and whether high-error points cluster by image or by region. A tight, low distribution with no strong clustering is a green light. A distribution with a heavy tail concentrated in a specific set of images is a targeted problem you can go fix, either by reshooting that area with more overlap, checking your exposure lock, or slowing down through repetitive geometry, rather than a vague "something's wrong somewhere" that only the final registration percentage would have told you about, later, and less specifically.

This is also why bundle adjustment deserves to be understood on its own terms and not just as a black box that runs at the end of the pipeline. The mechanics of what it's actually optimizing, and why its output is trustworthy as a diagnostic in the first place, are covered in Bundle Adjustment: Refining Pose and Points Together. Reading residuals as a health signal only works if you trust that the number reflects real geometric disagreement and not an artifact of how the optimizer converged, and that trust has to be earned by understanding the process, not assumed.

The habit this book is really arguing for is a small one: check the residuals before you check the registration score, because they tell you where to look, not just whether to worry.
