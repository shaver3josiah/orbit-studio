# Equirectangular Projection: Mapping a Sphere onto a 2:1 Rectangle

Point a 360 camera at a room and it captures light from every direction at once, a full sphere of information. A JPG is a flat rectangle. Something has to give, and the question this book answers is: what exactly happens in that flattening, and why does it demand such a specific shape?

The answer is the equirectangular projection, and its rule is almost embarrassingly simple. Take the sphere and describe every point on it the way you'd describe a point on Earth: longitude and latitude. Then draw a rectangle where the horizontal axis is longitude and the vertical axis is latitude, with no further transformation or scaling applied. Longitude runs the full 360 degrees around; latitude runs 180 degrees from pole to pole. Because one axis covers twice the angular range of the other, a full, uncropped equirectangular panorama must be exactly twice as wide as it is tall, a 2:1 aspect ratio (Equirectangular Projection, PanoTools.org Wiki). Google's own photo-sphere spec uses this same convention: a canonical full sphere example is 4000x2000 pixels, precisely 2:1 (Photo Sphere XMP Metadata, Google for Developers).

That "no further transformation" clause is the part worth sitting with, because it's what makes the projection cheap and also what makes it weird to look at. A straight vertical line in the real world stays straight in the image, and the horizon sits flat across the center. But content near the poles, the ceiling and the floor, gets smeared horizontally. Stand a broom in the corner of a room and its handle stays a clean vertical line; the ceiling tiles around it stretch into odd arcs as they approach the top edge. That's not a bug in the capture, it's the geometry of unwrapping a sphere onto a flat rectangle. Something always stretches when you flatten a curved surface, the way Greenland looks enormous on a Mercator world map. Equirectangular projection keeps the horizon undistorted at the cost of warping the poles.

Why does any of this matter to a solo operator walking through a building with an Insta360 X4 Air? Because the 2:1 rectangle isn't just a nice convention, it's the file format contract that everything downstream depends on. The X4 Air doesn't shoot equirectangular directly. Its two fisheye lenses each grab a roughly 170-degree hemisphere, and those two circular fisheye images have to be warped and blended together into that flat 2:1 rectangle before anything else can use them, a process covered in its own volume, Stitching Dual-Fisheye to Equirectangular with Insta360 Tools. The dual-fisheye files coming straight off the camera are a different animal entirely, and the sibling volume Dual-Fisheye Capture and Why the Insta360 X4 Air Needs a Stitch Step explains why you can't skip that conversion. Equirectangular is the destination format, not the native capture format, and the shape of that destination is fixed by the projection math described above, not by preference.

Once you have an equirectangular JPG, the 2:1 ratio becomes a thing you check, not just admire. If the width and height drift even slightly off 2:1, the file is quietly lying about how much of the sphere it covers. The PanoTools wiki gives a concrete cautionary example: a panorama sized 1650x824, one pixel short of a true 2:1 ratio, which is close enough to fool a casual glance but can confuse downstream panorama-viewing software that assumes the ratio is exact. That's a small numeric slip with an outsized consequence, because every pixel's position in an equirectangular image is being read as an angular coordinate. If the ratio is off, the angles are off, and pan/tilt no longer means what the format assumes it means. This is exactly why validating an export is its own step in the pipeline rather than a rubber stamp; the volume Validating an Equirectangular Export: 2:1 Ratio and Width > 3840px covers the practical check worth running on every file before it goes further, including why width matters on top of ratio, since a small equirectangular image spreads the same 360 degrees of detail across fewer pixels and loses resolution exactly where a viewer might want to zoom in.

There's a second reason the 2:1 rectangle matters, and it's less about display and more about what a later stage in the pipeline can and cannot do with the image. The gsplat and nerfstudio ecosystems that turn a batch of stills into a walkable 3D scene are built around the assumption that each input image was taken with a perspective camera, the ordinary rectilinear model where straight lines in the world stay straight in the image and each pixel corresponds to a simple projection through a lens center. An equirectangular image does not obey that model; it's a longitude/latitude map, not a perspective projection, and the Gaussian rasterization pipeline in tools like nerfstudio's Splatfacto explicitly does not support fisheye or equirectangular camera models (Splatfacto, nerfstudio documentation). That's not a minor caveat, it's the reason the Insta360 lane in this whole workflow needs extra handling rather than being fed straight into the same COLMAP-plus-gsplat pipeline as ordinary phone photos: the equirectangular frame has to be treated differently, whether that means slicing it into perspective-like sub-views or routing it through a path built to expect that shape, a topic that belongs to other volumes on this shelf rather than this one. What this book can tell you plainly is the boundary: equirectangular in, perspective assumed downstream, and that mismatch is a real seam in the pipeline, not a detail to wave away.

So the equirectangular projection is best understood as a contract with three clauses. First, the geometry: longitude maps to horizontal position, latitude to vertical, no other transformation, which forces the 2:1 ratio and predicts exactly where distortion will show up (at the poles, not the horizon). Second, the metadata convention built on top of that geometry, where fields like FullPanoWidthPixels and CroppedAreaImageWidthPixels let a viewer know whether it's looking at a complete sphere or a cropped slice of one (Photo Sphere XMP Metadata, Google for Developers). Third, the practical consequence for this pipeline: a file that satisfies the geometry cleanly is trivial to validate and reason about, and a file that doesn't quite hit 2:1 is a silent source of angular error, while even a perfect equirectangular file still needs deliberate handling once it reaches a Gaussian-splat trainer built for perspective cameras.

For the solo operator, the practical takeaway is small and specific: when the X4 Air lane hands you a JPG, its shape is the first fact worth confirming, not the last. Get the ratio right and the width generous, and the rest of the pipeline has a fighting chance of treating the file as what it claims to be, a full sphere, faithfully unwrapped.
