# Bundle Adjustment: Refining Pose and Points Together

Why does the sparse model you get out of COLMAP look almost right the moment images stop registering, and then get quietly better without you doing anything? The answer is a single optimization step running underneath the whole capture pipeline, and understanding it changes how you read every log line COLMAP prints.

Here is the problem it solves. You walked a room with a phone, shooting overlapping stills, or you shot 360 frames with an Insta360 X4 Air and stitched them to equirectangular JPGs. Each image is a guess: the feature matcher found correspondences between pairs of photos, and from those correspondences COLMAP estimated where the camera was standing and which 3D points it was looking at. But every one of those estimates carries error. The pixel location of a matched feature is noisy. The two-view pose computed from any single pair is only as good as that pair's geometry. If you built the whole model by chaining together pairwise estimates, the small errors in each link would compound, and by the time you'd registered fifty images the far end of the room would be subtly warped relative to the near end.

Bundle adjustment is the correction for that. As Wikipedia's summary of Hartley and Zisserman's *Multiple View Geometry* puts it, bundle adjustment jointly refines the 3D point coordinates, the camera motion parameters, and optionally the camera's optical properties, using the actual image observations of those points from every viewpoint that saw them. The name comes from the "bundles" of light rays that travel from each 3D point through each camera's optical center to land on that camera's sensor. Adjustment means moving the whole bundle of rays, across all cameras and all points at once, until they agree with each other as closely as possible.

"Agree with each other" has a precise meaning: reprojection error. Take a 3D point, take the estimated pose of a camera that observed it, and mathematically project that point back into that camera's image plane. If the reconstruction were perfect, the projection would land exactly on the pixel where the feature was originally detected. It never does, because both the point and the pose are estimates. The gap between where a point should land and where it was actually observed is the reprojection error for that observation. Bundle adjustment's job is to move every camera pose and every 3D point simultaneously so that the sum of these errors, across every observation in the whole model, is as small as possible.

The word "simultaneously" is the entire point, and it's why bundle adjustment isn't the same operation as the pairwise pose estimates that come before it. A two-view pose estimate only has to satisfy the geometry between two images. Bundle adjustment has to satisfy every image that shares a point with every other image that shares that point, all at once. Move one camera and you change the reprojection error for every point it sees. Move one point and you change the error for every camera looking at it. This is why bundle adjustment is described as almost always the final refinement step of a feature-based 3D reconstruction pipeline, not an early one: it needs an already-decent starting guess for poses and points before it can converge to something better, rather than searching blind.

You can see the arithmetic of this coupling directly in COLMAP's output format. The points3D.txt file records, for every 3D point, a TRACK list of every (image, feature) pair that observes it, plus an ERROR field: the mean reprojection error in pixels for that point across its whole track. Critically, that ERROR value is only meaningful after a global bundle adjustment has run. Before that, it doesn't yet reflect a jointly optimized model. This is one reason the companion volume *Interpreting Bundle Adjustment Residuals as a Health Signal* treats those per-point errors as a diagnostic: a room that bundle-adjusts to a low, evenly distributed error is a room where every camera pose and every triangulated point are mutually consistent, and a spike in error on one cluster of points is a place where the joint solution couldn't reconcile what different cameras were reporting.

This joint structure also explains why solving bundle adjustment is computationally different from solving an ordinary least-squares problem of the same size. If you wrote out every camera parameter and every 3D point coordinate as one enormous vector of unknowns, the matrix you'd need to invert to solve the optimization would be huge, but it has a specific, exploitable shape. Camera parameters don't interact with other cameras' parameters directly, and 3D points don't interact with other points directly; the only cross terms are between a camera and the points it sees. That produces a Hessian matrix with a sparse block structure, block-diagonal for the camera parameters, block-diagonal for the point parameters, and a dense but limited set of cross blocks connecting the two. Solvers exploit this with the Schur complement rather than attempting a brute-force matrix inversion, which is what makes bundle adjustment over thousands of points and dozens of cameras tractable at all. You don't need to implement this yourself, but it's worth knowing it's there: it's the reason bundle adjustment scales the way it does, and why it gets noticeably slower, not linearly but superlinearly, as a room's image count grows.

That growth cost is also exactly why COLMAP runs bundle adjustment repeatedly rather than once. In the default incremental mapping pipeline, a new image is registered, its newly visible points are triangulated, and then a local (sometimes global) bundle adjustment runs to fold that image's contribution into the existing model before the next image is added. Because pose and structure are solved jointly and repeatedly as the model grows, errors don't get a chance to silently accumulate the way they would if you only chained pairwise estimates. The tradeoff, documented directly in comparisons of incremental versus global structure-from-motion in COLMAP, is that repeated bundle adjustment is the actual bottleneck that makes incremental mapping slow on large image collections. This is the mechanism the volume *Running Incremental Mapping and Reading the Reconstruction Stats* walks through step by step, and it's also why COLMAP offers a global mapper that solves all camera poses at once via rotation averaging before running a single joint optimization, trading some robustness for speed on larger jobs.

None of this changes what bundle adjustment is solving for, though. Whether it runs once at the end or dozens of times along the way, it's always the same operation: take every camera pose and every 3D point that the feature matching and triangulation stages proposed, and move all of them together until the rays connecting them agree with the pixels that were actually observed. It's the step that turns a pile of individually-plausible guesses into one internally consistent model. If you want to understand where those initial guesses came from in the first place, that's the subject of *Structure from Motion in Plain Terms: What COLMAP Actually Solves*.

For the solo operator walking a building room by room, the practical upshot is this: bundle adjustment is the thing that makes a scene walkable and correct rather than merely stitched-looking. Every gaussian-splat scene trained downstream inherits whatever consistency, or inconsistency, bundle adjustment left behind in the sparse point cloud and camera poses. A model with low, even reprojection error hands the splat trainer a clean scaffold to build on. A model where bundle adjustment struggled, visible as high or uneven error concentrated in one part of the scene, hands the trainer a scaffold with a fault line in it, and no amount of splat training will fully hide a room whose underlying geometry never actually agreed with itself.
