# Exposure Drift's Downstream Cost: What Feature Matching Sees

What actually happens, at the pixel level, when the same wall shows up brighter in frame 40 than it did in frame 39?

That is the question this book answers, because "keep your exposure locked" is the kind of field advice that sounds like superstition until you trace it through the pipeline. An operator walking a room with a phone or an Insta360 X4 Air is not just taking pictures. Every frame is about to become input to a feature detector that has no idea what a wall is, only what its local pixel gradients look like. Exposure drift changes those gradients. And once the gradients change enough, the descriptors built from them stop agreeing with each other, even when they are describing the exact same corner of the exact same room.

## The descriptor doesn't see the wall, it sees the gradient

Feature matching, as covered in "Feature Matching: How Software Finds the Same Corner Twice," works by finding a distinctive point in one image and building a small numeric fingerprint of the pixel intensities around it, then hunting for the nearest matching fingerprint in another image. SIFT and its relatives build that fingerprint from local intensity gradients, not raw pixel brightness. This is why they tolerate some lighting change: a gradient is a comparison between neighboring pixels, and comparisons survive small, uniform brightness shifts better than absolute values do.

But the tolerance has a ceiling. Research evaluating classic descriptors like SIFT, SURF, and ORB under varying illumination and affine transformation found that matching accuracy doesn't fail at some clean threshold, it degrades measurably as the illumination gap between two images grows (Descriptor matching sensitivity to illumination change, arXiv 1804.10855). That is the mechanism behind exposure drift's cost. It's not binary. A slightly brighter frame still mostly matches. A frame that auto-exposure has pushed two stops brighter, because it swung its meter onto a dark doorway, starts losing matches steadily until COLMAP simply cannot find enough of them to connect that frame to its neighbors.

COLMAP's own FAQ backs this up from the other direction. Its documented guidance for scenes with "limited view overlap, little scene texture, and drastic illumination changes" is to fall back on DSP-SIFT with affine feature-shape estimation, use guided feature matching, and disable the two-view-track filter so that pairs of images can still triangulate a point even without denser support (Frequently Asked Questions, COLMAP official docs). Those are all compensations for matching that has already gotten harder. The FAQ also notes that ALIKED features can produce more repeatable matches than SIFT specifically in scenes with drastic illumination changes, which tells you the tool's own maintainers treat exposure variation as a distinct failure mode worth a different extractor, not a rounding error worth ignoring.

## Why exposure drift compounds room to room

An automatic-exposure phone camera constantly re-meters against whatever fills the frame. Point it out a bright window, it darkens the whole shot to compensate. Turn back to a shadowed corner, it brightens everything up. None of this is a bug in the camera; it's the camera doing its job for a single photograph. The problem is that a single photograph isn't the job here. The job is dozens of overlapping frames that all have to agree with each other well enough for the same physical corner to produce a matchable descriptor in each one.

This is close cousin to a different failure covered in "White Balance Drift and False Color Matches Across Rooms," where the color temperature of a scene shifts from room to room and descriptors that lean on color cues start agreeing for the wrong reason. Exposure drift and white balance drift are not the same mechanism, one is a brightness/gradient problem and the other is a hue problem, but they share a symptom: features that should match don't, or worse, features that shouldn't match start looking similar enough that the matcher accepts a bad pair. Locked exposure and locked white balance are the same discipline applied to two different channels of the same image.

The room-to-room framing matters because it's where the discipline is easiest to break without noticing. Inside one room, lighting is roughly consistent, so an operator who lets auto-exposure run for that room might not see obvious problems. But hallways, doorways, and threshold shots are exactly where exposure tends to swing, because they're where the field of view changes fastest, from a lit room, to a dim hallway, to another lit room. Those threshold frames are also the ones doing the structural work of connecting one room's photo cluster to the next. Weak matches there don't just cost you a few points, they can be the difference between one connected model and two separate ones that COLMAP registers independently, a failure mode explored in "Why Some Rooms Register and Others Don't."

## Quantifying the payoff

The honest note here is that no source in this shelf's research measures "exposure drift of N stops costs M percent of matches" as a controlled experiment; that specific number wasn't found in open documentation and shouldn't be invented. What the notes do support is the shape of the cost, not stated as a single number, and the field-side fix required to avoid it.

The Cultural Heritage Imaging photogrammetry guidelines, which predate today's Gaussian-splat pipelines but describe the same feature-matching-dependent process, are specific about the fix: shoot in aperture-priority or full manual mode, avoid apertures smaller than f/11 to sidestep diffraction blur, keep ISO as low as usable, lock focus manually, and keep the exposure metering point on the subject so it stays consistent across the sequence. That last point is the crux. A single meter point held constant is what prevents the camera from re-negotiating brightness every time the frame composition changes, which is the root cause of the drift described above.

The downstream reward for that discipline shows up as fewer wasted images, not a bigger number. Every frame that fails to match cleanly either gets stitched into the wrong place, drags down the mean reprojection error that bundle adjustment reports in points3D.txt, or fails to register at all and gets left out of the connected model entirely. COLMAP's incremental mapper logs exactly this, printing lines like "Registering image #241 (230)" that show how many of a frame's points actually found support in the existing model. A frame shot under drifted exposure is a frame more likely to show up in that log with a weak ratio, or not show up as registered at all.

## What this means in the field

Locking exposure costs nothing in the field. It costs a few seconds of setup per room, dialing in a manual exposure or locking auto-exposure to a fixed value before starting the walk, and it removes an entire class of matching failure before a single frame is shot. Compare that to the alternative: shooting on auto, discovering after the COLMAP run that a hallway sequence didn't register, and either re-shooting the room or fighting the FAQ's fallback options (guided matching, ALIKED, denser sequential matching) to rescue a dataset that didn't need rescuing in the first place.

The lesson generalizes past this one shelf. Every discipline this library preaches for field capture, locked exposure, locked white balance, deliberate overlap, exists because some later stage of the pipeline is more brittle than a human eye watching a live viewfinder would guess. The descriptor doesn't know it's looking at the same wall. It only knows the numbers it was handed. Give it the same numbers twice, and it will do its job.
