# The Manifest as a Contract: Keeping Capture and Reconstruction in Sync

What is manifest.json, really? It looks like bookkeeping: a list of image filenames, maybe some GPS tags, maybe a rig type. Easy to treat as an afterthought you generate at the end of a capture and consume at the start of a pipeline run. That framing is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that eventually breaks a real building scan at 2am when nobody is around to debug it.

The manifest is not metadata about the capture. It is the interface between the capture side and the reconstruction side. Everything the operator's app knows about how a scene was shot, and everything COLMAP and gsplat need to know in order to process it correctly, has to cross that boundary through this one file. Treat it as a contract, in the software-engineering sense of the word, and a lot of otherwise-mysterious failures stop being mysterious.

## Why "just a list of files" undersells it

Think about what actually has to travel from the phone or the Insta360 X4 Air to the reconstruction pipeline. It is not just "here are 400 JPGs." It is:

- Whether these images share one camera model or several, because COLMAP's feature_extractor has a `--ImageReader.single_camera` flag (and a `single_camera_per_folder` variant) that decides whether intrinsics get estimated once or per-image group, and getting this wrong either wastes shared-intrinsic accuracy or forces a false shared model onto genuinely different lenses (Command-line Interface — COLMAP official docs).
- Whether the capture came from a rig with multiple sensors at fixed relative offsets, which changes the pipeline from "just SfM" to "SfM plus a rig_configurator pass consuming a JSON config with cam_from_rig_rotation and cam_from_rig_translation," and which further changes whether `--Mapper.ba_refine_sensor_from_rig` should be locked during bundle adjustment (Rig Support — COLMAP official docs).
- Whether images are sequential (a walkthrough) or unordered, because that decision routes matching to `sequential_matcher` instead of `exhaustive_matcher` (Feature Extraction and Matching — COLMAP official docs).
- Whether a given image is a stitched 360 equirectangular JPG or a normal phone still, because gsplat's rasterizer (via Splatfacto) assumes a perspective camera model, and fisheye or equirectangular images are not currently supported for the rasterization pipeline (Splatfacto — nerfstudio documentation). If that fact does not survive the trip from capture to reconstruction, someone finds out the hard way, deep into a training run.

None of this is exotic. It is exactly the kind of thing a solo operator's capture app already knows in the moment of shooting, and exactly the kind of thing the reconstruction pipeline needs told to it explicitly rather than guessed. The manifest is the only place that handoff happens. If it does not carry the fact, the fact is gone by the time COLMAP starts.

## A contract has two signatures

Calling it a "contract" is not just a metaphor for "important file." A contract means: two parties agree on a shape, and neither party gets to unilaterally change what they promise without the other side finding out. That is the discipline that matters here.

Consider what happens without it. The capture app's engineer adds a new field, say a `rig_type` string, to help the reconstruction side skip straight to the right COLMAP quality preset. If the engineer building the ingestion module was not looped in, the field sits unread. The reconstruction side keeps using `--quality MEDIUM` for everything, oblivious that the manifest is now telling it "this was an Insta360 X4 Air rig, use the equirectangular-aware path." Building the Bundle Ingestion Module: manifest.json to a COLMAP Project is the volume that walks through parsing that manifest into a working COLMAP project directory, and it only works if the schema it parses against is the schema capture is actually writing.

Or run the failure the other direction. The reconstruction engineer decides the pipeline needs to know exposure-lock status per image, because inconsistent exposure across a sequence is exactly the kind of thing that degrades descriptor matching (SIFT and related gradient-based descriptors are not fully illumination-invariant, and match quality degrades measurably as illumination divergence increases between two images, per the descriptor-matching evaluation literature). So the reconstruction side starts expecting an `exposure_locked` boolean. If the capture app was never updated to write it, every manifest silently reports "unknown," and the parameter tuning that Parameterizing COLMAP Runs Per Rig Type from manifest.json depends on has nothing to key off. The schema drifted on one side and the other side never noticed, because nothing forced them to notice together.

This is the core argument of this book: manifest.json cannot be "the capture team's format" that the reconstruction team happens to read, nor "the reconstruction team's expected input" that capture happens to produce. It has to be one shared schema, versioned and changed as a single unit, because every field in it is a promise from one side that the other side is relying on to make a concrete technical decision, not decoration.

## What breaks first, and what that tells you

The place this shows up fastest in practice is registration diagnostics. When COLMAP's incremental mapper runs, it logs lines like "Registering image #241 (230)" followed by "=> Image sees 3755 / 8696 points," giving a directly parseable registered-versus-total ratio per image (COLMAP incremental mapper console log format, PR #2420 discussion). A healthy manifest schema lets you trace a bad registration ratio back to a specific field: this image's rig offset was wrong, or this image's camera group was misassigned, or this equirectangular frame never should have gone through the perspective pipeline at all. Registration Diagnostics as a Feedback Loop to the Field Operator is built entirely on that traceability existing. But traceability only exists if the diagnostic on the reconstruction side and the field written on the capture side are talking about the same thing, with the same name, the same units, the same optionality. A diagnostic that says "camera model mismatch" is useless to an operator in the field if the manifest field it is pointing at was renamed three versions ago on the capture side and nobody updated the message.

That is the practical cost of treating the manifest loosely. It is not an abstract data-modeling complaint. It is the difference between a field operator getting a message that says "recheck rig config for room 4" and a field operator getting a crash, or worse, a silently degraded splat that looks fine in the walkthrough and is subtly wrong.

One honest limit on this argument: the exact wire-level shape a manifest schema should take, field by field, version by version, is a design decision this book's research base does not settle from outside sources. Nothing in COLMAP's own documentation or the gsplat literature specifies an "Orbit Capture manifest.json" schema, because that schema is this project's own contract to write, not something borrowed from a paper. What the research does support, solidly, is the underlying reason that contract has to move as one piece: every field crosses a real boundary where a capture-time fact becomes a reconstruction-time decision.

## What this means for the reader

If you are the operator: every choice you make in the field, exposure lock, rig or handheld, single sweep or full three-row coverage, is data the reconstruction side needs, not a detail you can leave implicit. If you are the engineer building either side, treat a manifest schema change the way you would treat an API version bump: change it deliberately, change it on both sides together, and never let a field get added or renamed without the other half of the pipeline knowing it happened. The manifest is small. That is exactly why it is easy to underestimate, and exactly why every silent mismatch in it turns into a mystery on the far side of a two-hour reconstruction run.
