# Why Some Rooms Register and Others Don't

You walk the same building, shoot the same way, feed the same bundle of JPGs into the same COLMAP pipeline, and one room comes back a clean walkable model while the next one comes back a scatter of orphan cameras and a warning that half your images never joined the reconstruction. Nothing about your process changed. So why did the software treat two rooms so differently?

The honest answer is that "the software" isn't really the variable. Registration, in COLMAP's incremental pipeline, is a three-stage bet: extract features, match them across images, then grow a model one image at a time from an initial pair, triangulating new points as each new image joins (see the COLMAP tutorial docs). Every stage can fail quietly, and a room fails registration when it starves one of those stages of what it needs. Four things starve them: texture, overlap, exposure, and motion.

**Texture is the raw material.** Feature matching only works if there's something distinctive to match — a corner, a scuff, a grain pattern that looks different from its neighbors. A hallway of blank drywall, a glass conference table, a smooth white ceiling: these give the extractor almost nothing to grab onto. COLMAP's own FAQ addresses this directly, and its wording is worth sitting with: matching gets hard "particularly for scenes with limited view overlap, little scene texture, and drastic illumination changes." Notice that texture is only one leg of that sentence — it travels with overlap and lighting, which is the whole point of this book. For genuinely texture-poor rooms, COLMAP recommends DSP-SIFT with affine feature-shape estimation and guided matching, and separately notes that ALIKED features can out-perform classic SIFT specifically when overlap is limited, texture is sparse, and lighting swings are drastic. That's a meaningfully different failure mode from what the volume on Common Failure Signatures: Texture-Poor Rooms in the Sparse Model catalogs as symptoms in the point cloud itself — this book is about the cause, that one is about reading the wreckage.

**Overlap is what makes matching possible at all.** Two images can be individually sharp and richly textured and still fail to register together if they don't show enough of the same physical surface. Photogrammetry guidelines from Cultural Heritage Imaging put a number on this: aim for roughly 66% overlap between consecutive frames, which in practice means moving the camera about a third of its field of view between shots, and make sure every point on the subject lands in at least three separate frames. A solo operator sweeping a room too quickly, or skipping a corner because "there's nothing interesting there," is the single most common way to violate this rule. The three-frame minimum isn't a nicety — it's what feature matching draws on to triangulate a 3D point with any confidence, and a corner seen in only one or two frames often never triangulates at all. This is the same overlap discipline discussed at the descriptor level in Feature Matching: How Software Finds the Same Corner Twice — this book explains why the geometry has to be there before that matching can do anything with it.

**Exposure is the variable operators underestimate.** Feature descriptors like SIFT are built from local intensity gradients, and while they tolerate some lighting variation, they are not fully illumination-invariant — match quality degrades as illumination divergence between two images grows, and it degrades gradually rather than failing at one clean threshold (this is documented directly in comparative descriptor-matching evaluations under varying illumination and affine transformation). Locked exposure exists precisely to prevent this: if your capture app is re-metering every frame, a room with a bright window on one wall and a dark corner on the other will produce wildly different brightness readings depending on where the phone is pointed, and the same physical corner can look like two different features to the matcher. Rooms with mixed daylight and interior lighting, or rooms you shoot at different times of day in two passes, are exactly where this bites. COLMAP's FAQ groups "drastic illumination changes" with texture and overlap as the three things that break matching together, and exposure is the one entirely within the operator's control at capture time.

**Motion breaks the geometric assumption underneath everything.** Structure-from-motion needs parallax — real translation between camera positions — to recover depth and scale, because triangulation depends on rays from different viewpoints actually converging on distinct points in space. If the camera only rotates in place without translating, all corresponding rays intersect along the same line and there's no way to disambiguate depth; two-view pose estimation becomes degenerate under pure rotation, which is the same geometry that ordinary panorama stitching quietly relies on (panorama software assumes the camera pivots around its optical center precisely because that's the case SfM can't solve). A solo operator moving too little between frames, or an Insta360 X4 Air captured with the tripod planted in one spot and just panned, produces rotation-heavy footage that SfM interprets as unreliable structure even if the images themselves are sharp and well-lit. The fix is deliberate translation: walk the room, don't just turn in it.

Put these four together and the pattern behind "some rooms register and others don't" stops looking mysterious. A small, blank-walled bathroom is a texture problem. A long, narrow hallway shot too fast is an overlap problem. A sunlit atrium is an exposure problem. A room shot standing in the doorway swiveling the phone is a motion problem. Most failed rooms are actually two or three of these stacked, which is why COLMAP's log can end up reporting multiple disconnected sub-models instead of one connected reconstruction for a single physical room — the tutorial notes this happens whenever registration doesn't successfully link all the images together.

For the operator, this reframes what "shoot it again" should mean. Don't just retake the room with more photos — diagnose which leg failed. If you're watching the health score described in Reading a Registration Health Score: <60% Warning, ~95% Healthy dip mid-shoot, ask which of the four you skimped on: did you slow down for a wall with nothing to reflect? Did you drift into fast panning through a hallway? Did you re-meter exposure walking from a bright window into a dark closet? Did you spin the camera in place instead of walking a real path through the space? The answer is rarely "the software is bad at this room." It's almost always one of these four ordinary, fixable, physical choices made during the two minutes you spent capturing it.
