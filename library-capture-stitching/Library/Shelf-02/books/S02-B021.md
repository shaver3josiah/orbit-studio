# Choosing COLMAP Matcher Settings for Indoor Room Sequences

Which images should COLMAP compare against each other?

That question sounds trivial until you notice it scales quadratically. Feature extraction gives every image in your capture a bag of keypoints. Before any of that becomes a 3D model, COLMAP has to decide which pairs of images are worth comparing, and that decision is what the matcher does. Get it wrong on an Orbit Capture walkthrough, where one operator moves room by room through a real building, and you either wait far too long for a match graph nobody needed, or you save time by skipping comparisons that would have tied your rooms together into one coherent scene.

COLMAP ships several matcher commands: `exhaustive_matcher`, `sequential_matcher`, `vocab_tree_matcher`, `spatial_matcher`, and `transitive_matcher` (Command-line Interface, COLMAP official docs). Each answers the "which pairs" question with a different strategy, and for a solo operator shooting a building interior, the choice is not academic. It is the difference between a reconstruction that finishes overnight and one that finishes before lunch.

**Exhaustive matching is the default mental model, and it is the wrong one at scale.** The exhaustive matcher compares every image against every other image. COLMAP's own documentation frames this as suitable for small datasets, up to a few hundred images (Feature Extraction and Matching, COLMAP docs). A single room shot at reasonable overlap might land in that range. A full house, hallway included, will not. Exhaustive matching cost grows with the square of your image count, so doubling your shoot from 300 to 600 images does not double the matching work, it roughly quadruples it. For a solo operator who just walked eight rooms and a hallway, that is the difference between a coffee break and a lost afternoon.

**Sequential matching is built for exactly this capture pattern.** COLMAP's sequential matcher compares each image mainly against its near neighbors in capture order, and the documentation describes it as suited to video-like or ordered walkthrough sequences (Feature Extraction and Matching, COLMAP docs). This is worth pausing on, because it names your actual capture behavior. When you shoot Orbit Capture indoor sequences, you are not photographing a building in random order. You walk into room one, take a lap of overlapping stills, move to room two, and so on. That is an ordered sequence by construction. Sequential matching turns that order into a computational advantage: instead of comparing image 400 against images 1 through 399, it mostly compares it against its neighbors nearby in the sequence, which is where the real overlap actually lives for a walking capture.

The catch is right there in the name: sequential matching assumes neighbors in the file order are neighbors in space. That holds within a room. It does not automatically hold at the boundary where you leave room three and enter room four, and it definitely does not hold if you come back through room one at the end of the shoot to close the loop. This is the same tension explored in depth in *Loop Closure and Why COLMAP Needs to Recognize Return Visits*: a purely sequential match graph has no built-in way to recognize that you have returned to a place you already photographed, because "returned to" is a spatial fact and sequential order is only a temporal proxy for it. If your capture never revisits earlier ground, that gap costs you nothing. If it does, and a well-planned building walkthrough often does, a naive sequential pass will miss the connection entirely, and you may want the loop-closure-aware pass described in *Writing a Loop-Closure-Aware Matching Pass for Room Sequences* instead of taking sequential matching at face value.

**Vocab tree matching solves a different problem: recognition without order.** The vocab tree matcher retrieves likely-overlapping candidates by visual similarity rather than by position in a list, which is useful precisely when you cannot trust file order to reflect spatial adjacency, such as a return visit to an earlier room, or a shoot assembled from multiple sessions. Where sequential matching is fast because it trusts your capture order, vocab tree matching is more forgiving of a capture order it cannot fully trust, at the cost of needing a vocabulary structure to query against. For an indoor walkthrough with a genuinely linear path and no revisits, it is more machinery than the job requires. For a shoot that loops back on itself, it is closer to what the loop-closure problem actually needs.

**A practical default, and its edges.** For a straightforward, one-pass, room-by-room capture with no return visits, the sequential matcher is the right first choice: it matches the actual shape of your walkthrough, and it will run faster than exhaustive matching on anything beyond a trivial image count. If your capture is small (COLMAP's documentation's own "few hundred images" ceiling for exhaustive matching), it is reasonable to just run exhaustive and not think about it further, since the wall-clock difference will not matter. Where sequential matching starts to strain is at doorways and hallway transitions, where a purely sequential window may not look far enough sideways to catch the pair of images that actually bridges two rooms, and at any deliberate revisit, where sequential order actively misleads the matcher about what is nearby.

None of this happens by hand. The actual mechanics of calling `feature_extractor` and then one of these matcher commands from a repeatable script, rather than typing flags interactively, are covered in *Running COLMAP Feature Extraction and Matching from a Script*, which is the natural next stop once you have decided which matcher your capture calls for.

**What this means for the room-by-room operator.** Choosing a matcher is choosing what assumption you are willing to make about your own capture order. Sequential matching is the right default for a single, linear pass through a building, because it directly exploits the walking order you already produce for free. But the moment your route loops back on itself, whether by plan or because you forgot to shoot a corner and doubled back, that assumption breaks, and the fix is not to abandon sequential matching but to pair it with a way of recognizing return visits, which is exactly the seam between this book and the loop-closure volumes beside it. Know which assumption your matcher is making, and you know exactly where in your walkthrough it needs help.
