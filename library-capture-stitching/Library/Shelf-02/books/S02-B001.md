# From Photos to a Walkable Scene: The Reconstruction Pipeline at a Glance

How does a folder of overlapping phone photos, or a bundle of stitched panoramas, turn into a 3D scene someone can walk through on a screen? Not through magic, and not through a single black-box model either. It happens through a short chain of well-understood steps, each one solving a narrower problem than the last, and each one depending on the step before it having done its job honestly.

This book is the map of that chain. It does not go deep on any one stage; the sibling volumes in this shelf do that. What it does is show you the whole shape at once, so that when you read "Structure from Motion in Plain Terms: What COLMAP Actually Solves" or "What a Gaussian Splat Is and Why It Needs a Baseline" later, you already know where that piece sits and why it exists.

## Stage zero: getting frames that agree with each other

Before any reconstruction algorithm runs, you need a set of images that actually describe the same physical space from different vantage points. That sounds obvious, but it is where most of the real-world failure modes live.

If you are shooting with a phone, this means walking the space and taking overlapping stills with exposure locked, the same discipline photogrammetry practitioners have used for years: Cultural Heritage Imaging's guidelines recommend roughly 66% overlap between consecutive frames (moving about 34% of the field of view between shots), and insist every point on the subject appear in at least three frames. For round or full-coverage subjects, they recommend a shot every 10 to 15 degrees around the full circuit, with the first and last frames overlapping to close the loop.

If you are shooting with an Insta360 X4 Air instead, the frame doesn't exist yet as one flat image. The camera captures two fisheye views, one from each of its 1/1.8-inch sensors, that jointly cover the full 360-degree sphere. Those two fisheye images have to be stitched into a single equirectangular JPG before anything downstream can use them. Insta360's stitching relies on an optical-flow-based algorithm that compares the overlapping regions between the two lenses (and, for video, adjacent frames) to route the seam through actual scene content rather than a fixed geometric line. The output has a hard constraint worth internalizing now, because it will matter later: a full equirectangular panorama must be exactly 2:1, width to height. The PanoTools wiki's own cautionary example is a file that was one pixel short of true 2:1, which was enough to confuse downstream panorama viewers. Get this wrong at the stitch step and every stage after it inherits the error.

It's worth naming the assumption panorama stitching leans on, because it is the opposite of what comes next. Classic stitching aligns images under the assumption that the camera only rotated about its own optical center, with no real translation, so a single homography can warp everything into agreement. That is exactly the degenerate case that defeats the next stage: when two viewpoints differ only by rotation, all corresponding rays intersect along the same line, and there is no parallax to recover depth or scale from. Stitching wants pure rotation. Structure-from-motion wants the opposite: real translation, real parallax, a camera that actually moved through the room.

## Stage one: recovering where the camera was

Once you have a bundle of coherent images (whether raw phone stills or stitched equirectangular panoramas), the next job is figuring out where the camera stood and pointed for each one, and building a sparse 3D point cloud of the scene those cameras were looking at. This is structure-from-motion, and COLMAP is the tool this pipeline leans on to do it.

COLMAP's process runs in three recognizable stages, as its own tutorial lays out: feature detection and extraction on each image, feature matching and geometric verification between image pairs, and then incremental reconstruction, which registers images one at a time and triangulates new 3D points as each one joins. COLMAP can also solve this globally rather than incrementally, via rotation averaging across the whole set of views at once (GLOMAP, now integrated as COLMAP's global pipeline, does this while jointly estimating translations and points, trading some memory and reliance on good focal-length priors for substantial speed). Either way, the output that matters for everything downstream is a sparse model: a set of registered camera poses, a point cloud, and (crucially) an estimate of reprojection error, all held in the cameras, images, and points3D files that make up COLMAP's output format.

Every one of these poses is only as good as the frames feeding it, which is why stage zero matters so much. COLMAP's own FAQ notes that scenes with limited overlap, weak texture, or drastic illumination change are the hard cases, and that gradient-based descriptors like SIFT are not fully illumination-invariant: match quality degrades as lighting diverges between two views of the same spot, rather than failing at one clean threshold. That is exactly the failure mode a room-to-room walkthrough can trigger at doorways and windows, where exposure shifts fastest. The end-to-end mechanics of what COLMAP is actually optimizing, and how it decides an image is well-registered versus poorly registered, is the subject of "Structure from Motion in Plain Terms: What COLMAP Actually Solves."

The final refinement inside this stage, and inside almost every feature-based reconstruction pipeline, is bundle adjustment: a joint optimization of 3D point positions, camera poses, and often the cameras' own intrinsic parameters, all fit simultaneously to minimize reprojection error across every image that observed each point. It is the step that takes a rough, noisy set of estimates and pulls them into a mutually consistent, globally coherent model.

## Stage two: turning points into something you can render

A sparse point cloud is not a walkable scene. It's the skeleton, not the environment. The bridge from "here are camera poses and some 3D points" to "here is a scene I can fly a virtual camera through in real time" is Gaussian splatting.

The foundational method starts precisely where COLMAP leaves off: it seeds a set of 3D Gaussians directly from the sparse point cloud SfM produced, then optimizes and adaptively densifies those Gaussians (splitting, cloning, and pruning them) so the representation grows detail where the scene needs it and stays sparse where it doesn't. The payoff is real-time rendering, at 30 frames per second or better at 1080p, for scenes that took the prior generation of neural radiance field methods tens of hours to train and that rendered at a small fraction of a frame per second. gsplat, an open-source library that reimplements and extends this approach, reports comparable quality at roughly a quarter of the memory footprint and modestly faster training.

The dependency running through all of this deserves to be named plainly, because it's the reason stage one and stage two cannot be reordered or skipped: Gaussian splatting needs that SfM point cloud as its starting geometry. nerfstudio's own Splatfacto implementation documents this directly, noting that initializing from real SfM points performs much better than random initialization. Splatting is not an alternative to structure-from-motion; it is what you do once structure-from-motion has already told you where things are. Why that dependency exists, and what breaks when the baseline it needs isn't there, is the question "What a Gaussian Splat Is and Why It Needs a Baseline" takes on directly.

## What this means for the operator and the engineer

If you're the person walking the building with a phone or an X4 Air, the practical takeaway is that your discipline at capture time is not a nice-to-have, it is load-bearing for two entirely different downstream algorithms with opposite requirements. Panorama stitching wants rotation without translation; structure-from-motion wants translation with real parallax. Overlap, steady exposure, and enough frames per surface are what let both stages succeed on the same walkthrough.

If you're the engineer building the capture tool, the pipeline gives you natural checkpoints to instrument. A stitched panorama's aspect ratio is checkable before it ever reaches COLMAP. A sparse model's registered-image count and mean reprojection error are checkable before a Gaussian splat ever starts training on top of it, and the log format COLMAP emits when registering each image gives you a raw, parseable signal to build that check on. Once a bundle of manifest and crop files exists for a captured space, reading it back into Python to inspect or automate against is its own small, well-scoped task, covered in "Reading the Bundle: Opening manifest.json and crops/*.jpg in Python."

None of the individual stages here are mysterious once you isolate them. What makes the whole pipeline feel like magic on a first walkthrough is only that four or five separate, well-defined problems are chained together, each one silently depending on the last one having gone right. Once you can name each link, the chain stops being magic and starts being an engineering problem you can debug.
