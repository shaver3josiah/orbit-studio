# White Balance Drift and False Color Matches Across Rooms

Why does a scan that looks clean in every single room still produce a warped or split model where two rooms meet? You walked the hallway, you overlapped your shots, you didn't rush. The failure isn't in your coverage. It's in your white balance, and it shows up exactly at the boundary between lighting conditions, which is exactly where COLMAP needs its matches to be the most reliable.

## What white balance actually changes in the image

Auto white balance is a camera algorithm that guesses the color of the ambient light and shifts the image's color channels to cancel it out, so a white wall reads as white whether it's lit by a warm incandescent bulb or a cool window. It is a useful feature for a photo album and a liability for a feature-matching pipeline, because it recalculates its guess independently for every frame. Walk from a room lit by tungsten bulbs into a room lit by daylight through a window, and the camera doesn't gradually blend one white-balance setting into the other. It re-estimates, frame by frame, and near the doorway it can flip between two different color interpretations of what is structurally the same scene.

This matters because the descriptors COLMAP relies on for matching, SIFT and its relatives, are built from local intensity gradients: how brightness changes across small patches of pixels, not the raw color values themselves. That gives them real tolerance for lighting shifts, but not unlimited tolerance. The research behind this library's evaluation of descriptor behavior under illumination change is blunt about the shape of the failure: accuracy doesn't hold steady and then collapse at some threshold, it degrades measurably as the illumination gap between two images grows (arXiv 1804.10855, "Evaluation of Feature Detector-Descriptor for Real Object Matching under Various Conditions of Illumination and Affine Transformation"). A white-balance jump between rooms is exactly this kind of illumination divergence, expressed as a shift in the color and gradient structure of the same physical surfaces. COLMAP's own FAQ documentation echoes this from the pipeline's side, noting that matching struggles particularly in scenes with limited view overlap, little scene texture, and drastic illumination changes. A doorway threshold, where overlap between the two rooms' image sets is naturally already thinner than mid-room overlap, is precisely where a white-balance jump does the most damage. The sibling volume Exposure Drift's Downstream Cost: What Feature Matching Sees covers the general version of this problem; this book is about the specific case where the drift is in color temperature rather than brightness.

## Two ways it breaks the reconstruction, not one

It's worth being precise about what "breaks" means here, because white balance drift causes two distinct failure modes at a boundary, and they call for different mental models.

The first is a missed match: two images of the same corner, one shot under the tungsten-lit room's warm cast and one shot just past the doorway under daylight's cool cast, produce descriptors different enough that the matcher fails to link them at all. This thins out the connections across the boundary. If enough of these fail, the two rooms can end up under-connected or, in the worst case, reconstructed as separate models entirely, which COLMAP's tutorial documentation explicitly allows for: it will produce multiple disconnected models when not all images register into one connected whole. If that happens, the practical path forward isn't to re-shoot everything. COLMAP's model_merger command exists to fuse two separate reconstructions that share common registered images, using a similarity transform computed from those shared images' poses, though it's honest in its own limitations that this only works from the two models' poses rather than fresh re-triangulation, so a global bundle_adjuster pass afterward is the documented best practice to clean up the seam. Recognizing that you're looking at this failure mode in the first place, rather than something else, is the subject of Diagnosing a Failed COLMAP Run from Its Sparse Output.

The second failure mode is worse because it's silent: a false match. Two features that are not the same physical point but happen to look similar under their respective lighting conditions get accepted as correspondences. Where a missed match just weakens the boundary, a false match actively injects wrong geometry into it. COLMAP's geometric verification step, which checks that accepted matches are consistent with a plausible camera geometry, catches a lot of these, but not all of them survive rejection, particularly when the false match is only subtly wrong rather than obviously implausible. What gets through then corrupts the bundle adjustment that follows, because bundle adjustment jointly optimizes camera poses and 3D point positions against every accepted correspondence at once, using the reprojection error as its criterion. A handful of bad correspondences at a room boundary don't just produce a locally bad point, they pull on the whole optimization, since the underlying math ties every camera and point together into one sparse, block-structured system. This library's notes are candid that no single published source addresses this exact room-boundary framing directly; it's a reasoned synthesis from the descriptor-illumination literature above, not a specific documented case study, and it's worth naming that plainly here rather than dressing it up as established fact.

## What to actually do about it

The fix is upstream of COLMAP entirely: lock white balance before you start capturing, the same way this library's guidance elsewhere insists you lock exposure. Nearly every phone camera app has a manual or "pro" mode that lets you fix white balance to a single Kelvin value or a single preset for the whole walkthrough, instead of letting auto white balance re-decide per frame. Pick a value once, ideally suited to the dominant light source in the space, and hold it across every room, every doorway, every lighting transition. Yes, the resulting images will look a little wrong in some rooms, slightly too warm here, slightly too cool there. That's fine. COLMAP doesn't care what looks pleasant to a human eye; it cares whether the same physical corner produces a consistent descriptor across every frame that sees it, and consistency beats accuracy here every time.

If you're building the capture tool rather than just using it, this is a case for exposing white-balance lock as a first-class control in your capture UI, not an afterthought buried in a settings menu, and for treating a detected white-balance change between two overlapping frames as a warning sign worth surfacing to the operator immediately, before they've walked ten more rooms on the same mistake. A single locked setting, chosen once at the start of a scan, closes off an entire class of problems that otherwise only reveals itself minutes later in a stitched sparse model with a visible seam at every doorway you walked through.
