# Reading a Registration Health Score: <60% Warning, ~95% Healthy

You just fed ninety phone photos of a two-bedroom apartment into COLMAP. The tool spits back a number: registration health, 71%. Is that fine? Should you reshoot? What does 71% even mean, mechanically, about the scene you just captured?

This book answers that question directly. The registration health score is a single percentage standing in for a much more specific fact: what fraction of the images you fed the pipeline actually got a solved camera pose in the final reconstruction. Below 60%, the tool warns you. Around 95%, it calls the capture healthy. Everything in between is the pipeline telling you, in one number, how much of your walkthrough actually turned into usable 3D.

**What "registered" means**

COLMAP's reconstruction process, as laid out in its own tutorial, runs in three stages: feature detection and extraction, feature matching with geometric verification, and then incremental structure-and-motion reconstruction. That last stage is where registration happens. The mapper starts from a seed pair of images, solves their relative pose, triangulates the 3D points they share, and then tries to add one more image at a time: does this new image see enough of the points already in the model to solve its camera position too? If yes, it's registered. If no, it sits out.

You can watch this happen in real time. COLMAP's incremental mapper prints per-image log lines like "Registering image #241 (230)" followed by "=> Image sees 3755 / 8696 points" — a directly parseable ratio of points seen to points available, per image, as the reconstruction grows. The registration health score is the aggregate of exactly this: across your whole bundle, how many images made it into the model with a solved pose, versus how many you fed in.

An unregistered image isn't damaged or wrong. It's just an orphan. It failed to find enough shared, geometrically verified feature matches with the rest of the reconstruction to be locatable. The book "A First Pass: Feeding a Bundle Straight Through COLMAP" walks through what that first raw run looks like before you've learned to read its output — this book is about interpreting the number that run hands back.

**Why the score can fracture instead of just being low**

Here's the part that surprises people the first time: COLMAP doesn't fail loudly when registration is bad. It fails quietly, by building more than one model. If not every image ends up connected to the same graph of shared points, COLMAP will reconstruct multiple separate models rather than force everything into one. You might get a 95%-registered kitchen-and-living-room model and a completely separate, disconnected hallway-and-bedroom model, with no shared images to stitch them.

This is the mechanism behind "Why Some Rooms Register and Others Don't." A hallway with blank walls and no distinctive geometry gives the matcher nothing to lock onto — not enough features survive geometric verification, so the chain of overlap breaks there. Registration health, read at the whole-bundle level, can look moderate (say 71%) while actually describing two healthy sub-models glued together by a weak connective room. The score alone doesn't tell you which shape of failure you have; it just tells you that some meaningful fraction of your images didn't make it in.

**Where the 60% and 95% numbers come from**

To be direct about sourcing: COLMAP itself does not define a "registration health score" or these specific thresholds. That vocabulary and those cutoffs are Orbit Capture's own convention, built on top of what COLMAP actually reports (registered image count over total image count, visible in its console log and derivable from the sparse model's images.bin/images.txt). The underlying signal is real and well documented; the specific 60/95 boundary is a product decision layered on top of it, not a COLMAP-defined threshold, and this book won't pretend otherwise.

What grounds those particular cutoffs is observed variability. Across real-world photogrammetry runs, reported registration percentages swing widely depending on dataset and capture discipline, from roughly the mid-40s up into the 90s or a clean 100%. A 95% mark sits at the healthy end of that range: functionally complete, with only a stray image or two left out, which is normal even in a good capture (an image that's badly blurred, or aimed at a blank ceiling, will reasonably fail to register even when everything else works). A sub-60% score sits at the other end: it means a large chunk of your walkthrough is disconnected from the model, which is a different order of problem than one dropped frame.

**What to actually do with the number**

COLMAP's own documentation is not silent on remedies. When registration fails, the two documented fixes are performing additional, denser matching, or manually specifying the initial image pair the reconstruction seeds from. In practice, for a solo operator walking a building, the denser-matching route usually means retracing the weak room with more overlapping stills rather than trusting the tool to somehow find matches that don't exist in the data you gave it. No amount of reprocessing recovers texture information you never captured.

That's the real judgment call this score forces. A 95% health reading tells you the capture was sound and you can move on to gsplat training. A sub-60% reading tells you something structural is missing from the shoot itself: not enough overlap, a stretch of low-texture wall, a lighting jump between rooms that broke matching. The number in between is where "When to Trust a Low Registration Score and Reshoot Instead" earns its keep — a mid-range score can mean "close enough, minor gaps" or "one whole wing of the building never made it in," and only looking at which images actually dropped out, not just the aggregate percentage, tells you which.

**What it means for you**

Treat the registration health score as a smoke detector, not a grade. It doesn't measure how good your splat will look. It measures something narrower and more useful: whether the images you captured actually chained together into one connected, solvable model. Near 95%, trust the pipeline and move forward. Below 60%, don't argue with the number or tweak matcher settings hoping for a better roll — walk back into the room that broke the chain and shoot it again, with more overlap and steadier exposure. The score is COLMAP telling you where your coverage was thin. Believe it.
