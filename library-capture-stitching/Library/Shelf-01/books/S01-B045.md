# Troubleshooting a Session That Feels Wrong Before You Leave

You finish the last room, you are standing in the hallway with the phone still warm in your hand, and something nags at you. Nothing crashed. No error popped up. But the walk-through felt off in a way you can't quite name, and you know from experience that the feeling is rarely wrong. The question this book answers is simple: what do you actually check, right there on-site, before you pack up and drive away from a scan that might be unusable?

This matters because the failure mode of structure-from-motion is not a crash, it's a shrug. COLMAP will happily ingest a bad image set and produce a sparse reconstruction that looks plausible until you notice a wall folded in on itself or a whole room missing. By the time you're back at your desk running COLMAP and gsplat, the building is gone. You cannot walk back into a leased retail space or a client's closed office next Tuesday. So the checklist has to run in the field, in minutes, using nothing but your eyes, your phone, and what you already remember about how the pipeline breaks.

**Start with the shape of your walk, not the images themselves.** The single most common cause of a bad feeling is rotation without translation: you stood in one spot and pivoted the camera to cover a room instead of physically walking a path through it. This is a documented degenerate case in structure-from-motion. When two views differ only by rotation, with no baseline between the camera centers, the geometry that would let COLMAP triangulate depth simply isn't there, no matter how many well-matched features exist between the frames (per the arXiv survey on SfM degeneracy, "Reliable triangulation of 3D points fundamentally requires sufficient translational baseline between views"). COLMAP's own tutorial names this directly, warning against taking images from the same location and calling for high overlap between shots taken from genuinely different viewpoints. If you remember standing still and swinging your arm in a corner because the layout was awkward, that corner is a candidate for reconstruction failure, and no post-processing fixes it. The fix is on-site: walk it again, this time actually moving your feet between frames.

**Check for the rooms that never got three looks.** COLMAP's FAQ states this as close to a hard rule as the tool offers: "Make sure that each object is seen in at least 3 images." That's not a suggestion for texture-poor surfaces, it's described as a hard minimum for geo-registration and alignment estimation generally. If you remember rushing a small room, a closet, a stairwell landing, and getting two angles instead of a real loop, that's a real risk, not paranoia. The companion volume in this shelf, Deciding On-Site Whether a Room Needs a Reshoot, goes deeper into how to make that call systematically; the point here is just to notice the candidates while you're still standing in the hallway.

**Think about what the walls were actually made of.** Blank white walls, long featureless hallway sections, repetitive tile or brick, these are named risks in COLMAP's own tutorial, and a mural-digitization study in npj Heritage Science documents exactly why: hand-crafted keypoint detectors like SIFT degrade badly on weak-texture and repetitive surfaces because the algorithm can't find repeatable keypoints across viewpoints, and that becomes the bottleneck for the whole pipeline. On low-texture regions, matches skew toward outliers or simply become too sparse, which can propagate into unstable poses or outright registration failure for that patch of the building. If a room was mostly drywall and empty space, mark it mentally as fragile before you leave, not after the reconstruction comes back with a hole in it.

**Ask whether you were fooled by a repeating floor plan.** This is a subtler failure and it's specific to buildings, not objects. If the site has repeated rooms, identical hotel-style layouts, matching offices down a corridor, a warehouse with identical bays, there's a documented risk in visual SLAM literature: loop closure systems that try to recognize a revisited location can mismatch two genuinely different places that simply look alike, because visual similarity between different locations increases the risk of matching the wrong instance (PLOS ONE's study on indoor high-similarity scenes covers this directly, though it's worth being honest that this is robotics and SLAM research, not photogrammetry-capture literature, and this shelf is adapting it rather than quoting a photogrammetry-specific source). If you shot two nearly identical rooms back to back and your gut says "these could get confused," that instinct is worth trusting. It's exactly the kind of thing the sibling volume Handoff Discipline: What the Operator Owes the Pipeline exists to formalize: note it in the handoff so whoever runs reconstruction knows to watch for it.

**Check your lighting discipline, not just your exposure.** If you locked AE and AWB at the start of the session, as this library's capture method calls for, ask yourself honestly whether you actually held that lock the whole way through, or whether you toggled it off to fight a dark corner and forgot to re-lock it. Android's own camera2 documentation is explicit that AWB lock freezes the auto-white-balance algorithm's last computed values and "will not change color balance settings until" unlocked, and the same logic applies to AE lock. A session with locked exposure for eight rooms and unlocked exposure for the ninth is a session with an inconsistent color and brightness baseline stitched into the same image bundle, which is a subtler failure than a missing room but a real one. Fixed white balance is specifically recommended, according to photography references on this technique, for exactly this kind of multi-frame consistency task, the same logic that applies to panoramas and focus stacks applies here: auto white balance can drift frame to frame under mixed or changing light, and a technique depending on cross-frame consistency needs a fixed setting, not a re-metered one.

**Look for the shake, not just the blur.** You don't need to run variance-of-Laplacian analysis on-site (that's a desk-side triage step, not a field one), but you can eyeball your last few shots for obvious camera shake, especially any room where you rushed. The reciprocal rule for handheld shooting, minimum shutter speed of roughly 1 divided by focal length, exists precisely because camera shake and subject motion blur are two different problems with two different causes, and a phone walking a room at a brisk pace is more exposed to the shake kind. If your feed felt hurried near the end, a quick glance at the last handful of thumbnails for anything visibly smeared is worth the ten seconds it costs.

**And be honest about the surfaces you can't fix by shooting more carefully.** Reflective and transparent surfaces are a documented, acknowledged gap even in COLMAP's own FAQ, which offers no explicit guidance for them at all. If the space had a big mirrored wall or floor-to-ceiling glass, no amount of careful walking solves that on this pass. Note it, don't obsess over it, and move on.

None of this is a formal audit. It's a two-minute mental walk-through you do standing in the building, before the door closes behind you, running through the ways this specific pipeline is known to fail: no baseline, too few looks, no texture, false loop closures, broken exposure discipline, shake, and surfaces that were never going to cooperate. Catching the problem here costs you a few extra frames and five more minutes of walking. Catching it after you've left costs you the whole trip, which is exactly the argument made in The Cost of a Missed Room: Why Reshoots Are Cheaper On-Site. The feeling that a session went wrong is data. Spend two minutes turning it into a checklist before you drive away, because the building will not wait for you to notice the fold in the wall on your monitor next week.
