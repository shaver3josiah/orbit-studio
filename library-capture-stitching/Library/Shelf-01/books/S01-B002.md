# The Parallax Requirement: Why Pure Rotation Kills a Splat

Why does spinning in place with your phone, capturing every wall of a room from one spot, produce a scene that COLMAP and gsplat simply refuse to build? The footage looks complete. You covered every angle. And yet the reconstruction comes back empty, or worse, wrong. The answer is not a bug in the software. It is a hole in the geometry you handed it, and understanding that hole is the difference between a capture method that works and one that only looks like it should.

## What triangulation actually needs

A structure-from-motion pipeline like COLMAP does not see a room. It sees a set of 2D pixel coordinates across many photos, and its whole job is to work backward from those coordinates to a 3D point cloud plus a camera position for each photo. The way it does that backward calculation is triangulation: find the same physical point in two or more images, draw an imaginary ray from each camera through that point, and see where the rays cross. Where they cross is where the point lives in 3D space.

That crossing only tells you anything if the rays approach from different directions. Two cameras standing at the same spot, just tilted or turned to face a different wall, cast rays that are effectively parallel translations of each other rather than rays converging from separated vantage points. Research on SfM degeneracy describes this precisely: pure camera rotation, with no translation between viewpoints, is a well-known critical configuration in two-view geometry, one where the fundamental matrix relating the two images is not uniquely determined by the point correspondences at all (Structure-from-motion degeneracy under pure rotation and insufficient baseline). The math that is supposed to locate the point in space has no unique answer to give.

This is what "parallax" means in practical terms. Hold your finger up, close one eye, then shift your head sideways instead of turning it: your finger appears to move against the background because your eye changed position, not because it changed angle. That apparent shift against the background is the signal triangulation feeds on. Rotating in place removes it entirely, because rotating never changes where the camera actually is. It only changes which direction it happens to be pointed. Every photo in a pure-rotation sequence is still, geometrically, coming from the identical origin point.

## Why this fails quietly instead of loudly

The frustrating part is that a pure-rotation capture does not necessarily fail with an error message. It can produce a sparse reconstruction that registers cameras and still be unusable, because feature matching between the frames can succeed even while the depth estimation behind it cannot. The same research notes that reliable triangulation fundamentally requires sufficient translational baseline between views, and that without adequate parallax, depth cannot be resolved even when the features are abundant and well matched. You can have a beautiful set of matched keypoints between two frames and still have no way to know whether a given point is one meter away or ten, because both explanations satisfy the same rotation-only geometry equally well.

This is a large part of why some modern wide-baseline SfM pipelines deliberately delay full initialization until sufficient parallax has accumulated between frames, rather than trying to triangulate as soon as any two frames share matches. Triangulating too early, on a low-baseline pair, produces unreliable depth that then poisons everything built on top of it. COLMAP's own tutorial guidance says the same thing in plainer language: capture images with high visual overlap, but also, explicitly, do not take images from the same location. Overlap and standoff are covered in more depth elsewhere in this shelf (see Adaptive Overlap: When to Shoot Tighter Than 70-80% and Standoff Distance and the Geometry of a Room), but the rotation problem sits underneath both of those concerns. Overlap tells you how much of the frame should repeat between shots. Translation is what makes that repetition mean anything to a triangulation algorithm in the first place.

## Why gsplat inherits this problem rather than solving it

It is worth being precise about where in the pipeline this actually bites, because Gaussian splatting is sometimes talked about as if it were a more forgiving, more magical process than classical photogrammetry. It is not, at least not at this stage. The original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper is explicit that its method's input is the sparse point cloud produced during camera calibration by a standard structure-from-motion pipeline, meaning the technique does not train from raw uncalibrated photos at all. It depends on SfM camera poses and points as initialization (Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler, Drettakis, 3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering). The reference implementation that most gsplat training pipelines are built around confirms this in its expected directory layout: an images folder alongside a sparse folder holding the COLMAP camera and point data the optimizer is handed before a single Gaussian gets placed.

In other words, gsplat training does not fail on a pure-rotation capture because gsplat itself is picky. It fails because there is no COLMAP output to feed it. If COLMAP cannot triangulate a usable point cloud and cannot resolve reliable camera poses from a rotation-only sequence, the training script never gets a valid starting scene to optimize. The degeneracy happens upstream, at the geometry stage, long before any Gaussian is initialized or any loss is computed. This is worth internalizing precisely because it reframes the whole Orbit Capture workflow: the operator walking the room is not just building photographic coverage, they are manufacturing the translational baseline that the entire downstream pipeline, COLMAP first and gsplat second, structurally cannot function without.

## What this means for the walk

The practical fix is not subtle once the cause is clear. A capture pass has to move the camera through space, not just rotate it in place, and that motion has to happen at every point along the route, not just once at the start of the room. Standing in the center of a room and doing a slow 360 will feel thorough while shooting it and will collapse the reconstruction afterward, because every one of those frames still shares the same camera center. A walk that traces a path along the room's perimeter, or through its middle at a few different stopping points, gives triangulation actual separated vantage points to draw rays from. This is the one physical habit an operator needs to hold onto through every room in a building: keep the feet moving, not just the phone turning, because it is the feet that create the parallax the entire splat depends on. The photoset itself still has to satisfy other conditions covered elsewhere in this shelf, laid out in What Makes a Photoset Reconstructable, but none of that matters if the baseline was never there to begin with.
