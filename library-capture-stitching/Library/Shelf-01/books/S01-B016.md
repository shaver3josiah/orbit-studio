# AE Lock: What It Freezes and What It Doesn't

When you tap AE lock in a camera app, what actually stops moving? That question sounds pedantic until you are three rooms into a solo capture and the reconstruction comes back with a visible brightness seam between two photos you were certain shared identical exposure. The honest answer is that AE lock freezes less than most people assume, and the gap between what it freezes and what you think it freezes is exactly where orbit capture bundles go wrong.

Android's Camera2 API defines the behavior precisely, and the wording matters more than it looks like it should. `CONTROL_AE_LOCK` is described as controlling "whether auto-exposure is currently locked to its latest calculated values" (Android Developers, CaptureRequest reference). Read that again slowly. It does not say the exposure is locked to a value you chose. It says the exposure is locked to whatever the auto-exposure algorithm most recently landed on. AE lock is a freeze-frame on the algorithm's last output, not a manual override. The distinction sounds academic until you realize it means the exposure you get is whatever the meter happened to converge to at the exact instant you engaged the lock, and if that instant caught the algorithm mid-adjustment, the frozen value inherits that instability. This is also why Camera2's own documentation warns that because the camera pipelines in-flight requests, the settings that end up locked don't necessarily match the settings in the most recent capture result you saw on screen. There is a small window where you are locking blind.

Auto-white-balance works the same way and it is worth stating plainly because people conflate the two locks and assume one covers the other. `CONTROL_AWB_LOCK` "locks auto-white-balance to its latest calculated values" (confirmed via the Android javadoc mirror, Microsoft Learn), and it is a completely separate control from AE lock. Locking exposure does nothing to white balance, and locking white balance does nothing to exposure. For an orbit capture bundle destined for COLMAP and gsplat training, you generally want both locked together, because color drift between frames is just as damaging to reconstruction as brightness drift. A wall that shifts a few hundred Kelvin between two overlapping stills is a wall that gsplat has to reconcile as if it were two different surfaces.

Now for what AE lock does not touch, which is the part that catches people. Exposure compensation, `CONTROL_AE_EXPOSURE_COMPENSATION`, is documented as a separate, independently-configured control (Android Developers, CaptureRequest reference; cross-referenced in the ControlAeLock javadoc alongside ControlAeMode and ControlAePrecaptureTrigger as distinct fields). It lives on top of the AE algorithm's own metering, typically adjustable across a device-reported range around -3 to +3 EV steps. Here is the trap: AE lock freezes the exposure the algorithm computed, but it has no opinion about EV bias one way or the other. If you nudge exposure compensation after locking, most implementations let that change go through and shift your locked exposure by exactly that amount, silently. AE lock is not a vault door that rejects all further exposure input. It is a snapshot of the algorithm's state that a separate control can still walk right past.

This is precisely why the sibling volume in this shelf, "The Unlock-Set-EV-Relock Dance," exists as its own book. If you need to bias exposure deliberately (say, because the automatic meter reads a scene as brighter than you want it recorded), the correct sequence is not "lock, then tweak compensation." It is unlock, set the compensation you want, let the algorithm re-converge with that bias folded in, then lock again. Treating exposure compensation as something you can freely adjust after the lock is engaged, expecting the lock to somehow constrain it, misunderstands what the lock is for. The lock freezes an output. It was never built to gate an independent input.

White balance carries its own version of this trap, and it runs in the opposite direction from what people expect. Camera2 has no direct "set white balance to N kelvin" control at all. `CONTROL_AWB_MODE` is restricted to a fixed list of presets, INCANDESCENT, FLUORESCENT, WARM_FLUORESCENT, DAYLIGHT, CLOUDY_DAYLIGHT, TWILIGHT, SHADE, each approximating a specific reference illuminant rather than exposing a tunable temperature (Android Developers, CameraMetadata reference). So a "fixed Kelvin" strategy on Android is always an approximation via the nearest preset, and that is a genuinely different strategy from AWB lock, which freezes whatever value the auto-algorithm last converged on, a value that may not correspond to any named preset at all. One approach commits to a known, repeatable target before you shoot. The other commits to whatever the algorithm guessed, which is why the sibling volume "Metering Off a Mid-Tone Wall and Locking It" matters for exposure and why an equivalent discipline matters for color: feed the algorithm a deliberate, representative target before locking anything, rather than trusting it to have guessed well on its own.

Put together, the practical model for a solo orbit capture is this. AE lock and AWB lock each freeze one algorithm's output, and only that output, at the moment you engage them. Neither lock reaches backward to constrain other controls, like exposure compensation, that live at a different layer of the pipeline. And neither lock is a substitute for giving the algorithm something sensible to converge on in the first place. Lock the wrong instant, or lock a value the algorithm was still adjusting, and every photo downstream inherits that error, because SIFT-based matching in COLMAP has no exposure correction step of its own; it just tries to match features across images with whatever brightness and color differences you handed it.

For the engineer building the capture tool, the actionable takeaway is to treat AE lock and AWB lock as two independent booleans, not one "exposure locked" flag, and to expose exposure compensation as a control that must be set before the lock, not one that's safe to touch after. If your tool's UI implies that locking exposure freezes the whole capture pipeline in place, it is teaching the operator something the API does not actually guarantee, and the room-by-room capture will pay for that gap in silently mismatched brightness between frames that no amount of post-processing color correction fully undoes.
