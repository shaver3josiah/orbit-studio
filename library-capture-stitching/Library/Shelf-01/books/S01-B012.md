# Doorways, Thresholds, and Keeping Continuity Between Rooms

What happens the moment you step through a doorway with the camera still rolling?

That single question decides whether your two rooms become one connected 3D scene or two disconnected islands that COLMAP registers separately, if it registers them at all. Everything else in a room-by-room capture, careful overlap, locked exposure, steady standoff, is protecting a chain of correspondences from frame to frame. A doorway is the one place in the building where that chain is easiest to break, because it is the one place where the scene content, the lighting, and your own walking motion all change at once.

## Why the threshold is the weak point

COLMAP's own tutorial guidance is blunt about what it needs: high visual overlap between frames, every surface seen in at least three images, and genuinely different viewpoints rather than repeated shots from one spot. Under the hood, if you're using sequential matching, the matcher only compares each frame against a window of neighbors, ten images to either side by default, so image 214 only gets matched against roughly 204 through 224 unless loop detection kicks in separately. That window is what makes a walkthrough capture tractable at all: you are not asking every frame to be compared against every other frame, you are asking each frame to hand off cleanly to its neighbors. A doorway is exactly where that handoff is most likely to fail, because it is where "neighbor" stops meaning "similar room" and starts meaning "different room entirely."

Two structural things make doorways risky. First, a doorway is often where an operator's motion stops being smooth translation and becomes something closer to rotation in place, pausing to look through the frame, pivoting to check the room ahead, stepping through without images in between. Structure-from-motion depends on real parallax between viewpoints; pure rotation without translation is a documented degenerate case where the geometry between two views is not uniquely determined by point correspondences alone, and reliable triangulation needs a real baseline, not just abundant matched features. Standing in a doorway and swiveling produces exactly the kind of low-baseline sequence that leaves depth unresolved even when the images look fine to the eye.

Second, a doorway is a texture bottleneck. Door frames, flat trim, and blank hallway walls are close to the texture-poor surfaces that COLMAP's own documentation flags as weak ground for its default SIFT-based matching, and the same failure mode shows up in the broader SfM literature on low-texture and repetitive surfaces: keypoints become sparse or unstable, matches skew toward outliers, and that instability can propagate into the pose estimates for everything nearby. A hallway of identical doors compounds this in a second way that belongs to loop closure rather than local matching, and that risk is covered in full in "Loop Closure in the Field: Returning to Where You Started," but the short version matters here too: visually similar, repeated architecture is a documented hard case for recognizing whether you're really back where you started, or just somewhere that looks like it.

## What to actually shoot at the threshold

The fix is not a special doorway technique so much as refusing to let the doorway interrupt the technique you're already using. Concretely:

**Keep walking, don't pivot.** Cross the threshold as part of continuous forward translation, at the same standoff distance and pace as the rest of the room. The geodetic photogrammetry guidance on standoff distance treats a stable, consistent distance while walking a path as the same discipline as maintaining overlap, not a separate concern, and a doorway is the worst place to let either slip. If you need to look into the next room to plan your route, do that before you start shooting the transition, not while the shutter is firing.

**Shoot the frame itself, not just past it.** Take at least one frame with the doorway and its immediate jambs fully in view from the room you're leaving, and at least one more just after you've crossed, with the same doorway visible from the other side. This gives COLMAP a shared, identifiable structure, the door frame, the threshold strip, the trim, that appears in images from both rooms. Given COLMAP's stated minimum of three images per registered point, treat two as a floor and three or four as the real target for anything at the threshold: a stray blurred or rotated frame at exactly the point where connectivity matters most is the single most expensive frame to lose in the whole capture.

**Slow the frame spacing through the gap.** Pix4D's overlap guidance already pushes well past its general 75% frontal / 60% side baseline for harder scenarios, interior reconstruction specifically is called out as needing overlap around 90%, precisely because interiors are tight, texture-repetitive, and full of exactly this kind of geometric pinch point. A doorway is a pinch point inside a pinch point. If your normal cadence through a room is one frame every arm's length, tighten that to a shorter interval for the two or three steps spanning the threshold, so no single frame has to carry the entire connection between the outgoing room and the incoming one.

**Overlap the doorway from both rooms' sequences, not just once.** Because COLMAP's sequential matcher works off a fixed neighbor window, the frames straddling the doorway are the literal hinge in your image order: everything before the threshold belongs conceptually to room A, everything after to room B, and the matcher only bridges them because consecutive frame numbers happen to share visual content. Make sure that content overlap is real and generous, not assumed. This is also the point where the routing decisions from "Routing a Room-by-Room Walk Without Backtracking" pay off or don't: if your walk order already threads doorways in a sensible sequence rather than jumping around the floor plan, the sequential matcher's neighbor window naturally lines up with the building's real connectivity, and you are not fighting your own frame order on top of everything else.

**Treat the threshold as a lighting seam, not just a geometric one.** Doorways are frequently where illumination changes hardest, daylight from one room's window against tungsten in the next, or a bright hallway against a dim closet. A fixed white-balance setting matters more here than almost anywhere else in the shoot: auto white balance is well documented to drift frame to frame under mixed or changing light, since it re-estimates color temperature independently on every shot, and that drift is exactly what shows up as a visible seam right at the doorway if you let it run. Locking exposure and white balance before you start the room, and choosing one deliberate compromise value if the two rooms' lighting genuinely disagree, is the specific transition strategy that "Mixed Lighting Zones: Transition Strategy Between Rooms" develops at length; the doorway is simply where that decision gets tested hardest, because it's the one place two different lighting conditions sit in the same frame.

## What it means for the reader

A doorway does not need a separate capture method. It needs you to protect the same three things you protect everywhere else, translation instead of rotation, sufficient overlap, and locked exposure and white balance, at the exact moment those three things are most likely to slip. Walk through instead of pivoting through. Shoot the frame and its jambs from both sides, generously. Tighten your spacing for the handful of steps that span the gap. And treat any mismatch you feel between the two rooms' light as a decision to make deliberately, not a problem to fix later. Get the threshold right and the rest of the building's frames connect the way COLMAP's matching actually depends on: not room by room, but as one continuous sequence of neighbors that never had to jump a gap.
