# EV Compensation for Windows, Skylights, and Dark Corners

How much should you nudge exposure compensation before you relock, when the room you're standing in has a window blazing on one wall and a closet swallowing light on the other?

This is the question every solo operator hits within the first ten minutes of a real building. The unlock-set-relock rhythm described in *The Unlock-Set-EV-Relock Dance* assumes you're choosing one exposure value per zone, but it doesn't tell you which direction to push that value or by how much. That's a judgment call, made room by room, and it's the difference between a capture set COLMAP can register cleanly and one where half the frames are unusable.

## The problem exposure compensation is solving

On a phone shooting through Camera2 or CameraX, exposure compensation is a simple lever: an index multiplied by a step size, added on top of whatever the auto-exposure algorithm already metered, with positive values brightening the frame and negative values dimming it. CameraX exposes this through `setExposureCompensationIndex()` on `CameraControl`, and the supported range is queried from the device's own `CameraInfo.exposureState`, so the ceiling and floor vary by phone. That's the mechanism. The judgment is what value to dial in before you lock AE and start capturing a room.

The underlying reason you need this lever at all is that camera light meters, however sophisticated, are still calibrated around a mid-tone assumption: the scene is expected to average out to roughly 18% gray reflectance, the middle zone in Ansel Adams's zone system. A room that doesn't average to mid-gray fools the meter in a predictable direction. A window-lit room, dominated by a bright rectangle of daylight, reads brighter than mid-gray, so the meter pulls exposure down to compensate, and the rest of the room goes dark. A dim corner or closet, dominated by shadow, reads darker than mid-gray, so the meter pushes exposure up, and the window (if one is in frame at all) blows out. In both cases the meter is doing exactly what it's built to do. It just isn't built for your room.

Exposure compensation exists to correct for that mismatch before you lock. The question is which way to turn the dial, and that depends entirely on which part of the room you're actually trying to preserve.

## Reading the room before you touch the dial

Three lighting conditions come up constantly in real interiors, and each one asks for a different move.

**Windows and glass doors.** A window in frame is the single most common cause of blown highlights in an indoor capture set. Once a bright window pushes far enough right on the histogram, that detail is gone for good and unrecoverable in post, which is exactly the failure mode *Clipping and Banding: Reading a Histogram in the Field* is built to help you catch before you walk away from a room. The fix, before relocking, is to dial exposure compensation down. You are trading a slightly darker room for a window that still has sky and mullions in it instead of a pure white hole. If the room itself goes a little underexposed as a result, that's the acceptable side of the trade, because COLMAP's own tutorial guidance explicitly warns against capturing under high dynamic range conditions in the first place, and a blown window is the textbook case of blowing that guidance.

**Skylights and overhead glazing.** Treat these as a more extreme version of the window problem, not a different one. A skylight puts the brightest patch of the scene directly in frame from below, often filling a meaningful fraction of the image when you're capturing the ceiling plane or an upper corner. The same negative EV nudge applies, usually more aggressively than for a wall window, because the skylight's contrast against the surrounding ceiling is typically higher than a window's contrast against a wall. If the room has both a skylight and deep shadow elsewhere, you're looking at a dynamic range problem the sensor can't fully solve in one frame, and the honest move is to bias exposure toward protecting the skylight and accept that the shadowed area will need its own pass.

**Unlit corners and closets.** This is the mirror image of the window problem, and the instinct to compensate for it is exactly backwards from what you'd do for a bright window. Here you dial exposure compensation up, deliberately overexposing relative to what a well-lit part of the same room would need, because a dark corner with too little tonal data risks two separate failures. Pix4D's own capture guidance calls out that soft, consistent lighting is what photogrammetry work wants, and names direct, harsh contrast as the thing to avoid; a starved dark corner is the low-light cousin of that same problem. First, feature detectors depend on visible texture and edges to find keypoints at all, and a dark corner with insufficient tonal separation gives SIFT (COLMAP's default) very little to grab onto, degrading match quality in exactly the way documented for weak-texture, low-light surfaces. Second, an underexposed region compressed into few tonal steps is a banding risk the moment anyone tries to brighten it in post, since banding is a direct symptom of insufficient tonal data in a gradient region.

## Why you can't just split the difference

The tempting move is to average it out, dial in some small positive or negative nudge and call the room done in one pass. Resist that. A single frame's dynamic range is a fixed resource, and every stop you spend protecting a window is a stop you don't have for the shadow, and vice versa. When a room has genuinely incompatible zones, the better answer isn't a compromise exposure, it's two exposures: relock for the window-facing wall, capture that wall's coverage, then walk to the dark corner, relock again with positive compensation, and capture that corner's coverage separately. This is more shots per room, but it's cheap insurance against a bundle where a third of the frames are unusable in the exact spot you needed geometry most.

This is also where the compensation decision inside one room connects to the bigger transition problem covered in *Mixed Lighting Zones: Transition Strategy Between Rooms*: a hallway with a skylight at one end and a windowless bathroom at the other isn't one lighting problem, it's several, laid out in sequence, and the same room-by-room EV judgment applies at every transition, not just when you cross a doorway.

One honest caveat: none of the sources behind this shelf give a numeric EV offset to dial in for a given lux ratio between a window and a shadowed corner. That number depends on your sensor, your phone's metering behavior, and the specific room, so what follows is a rule of thumb, not a formula: you're reading the histogram, judging which zone you can't afford to lose, and pushing compensation toward protecting it.

## What this means in practice

Before you relock AE and AWB and start moving through a room, ask which zone of that room you can least afford to lose. If it's a window or skylight, dial compensation negative and let the room's shadows go a stop darker than feels natural. If it's a dark corner with real geometry in it, dial compensation positive and accept a slightly hot look everywhere else. If both zones matter and neither can survive the other's ideal exposure, split the room into two capture passes rather than compromising on one. The dial itself is simple. Knowing which way to turn it, before the room disappears behind you, is the actual skill.
