# From Building to Bundle: The Shape of the Orbit Capture Pipeline

What actually happens between walking into a building with a phone and getting a walkable 3D scene out the other end? Not the theory of structure-from-motion, not the math of Gaussian splatting, but the shape of the work itself: what gets captured, in what order, how it gets cut up, and what gets handed off. If you can't describe that shape in a few sentences, you'll make bad decisions at every step, because you won't know what a decision made in room three costs you when you're assembling the bundle in room twelve.

Here is the shape, stated plainly, before any of the reasoning behind it: you walk a building room by room. In each room you capture a set of overlapping images, either as discrete phone stills with locked exposure or as 360 footage from something like an Insta360 X4 Air that later gets stitched from dual-fisheye into equirectangular JPGs. Each room's captures get cropped and organized into their own folder. Every room's folder, plus a manifest describing the whole shoot, gets assembled into a single bundle. That bundle, and only that bundle, is what crosses the handoff to the reconstruction side, where COLMAP turns images into camera poses and a sparse point cloud, and a gsplat-style trainer turns that into a Gaussian-splat scene. Four stages: capture, crop, manifest, handoff. Each one exists because of a specific failure the previous stage would otherwise cause.

**Why room by room, not one continuous sweep.** The solo-operator constraint (the subject of *One Person, One Building: The Solo Operator Constraint*) means there's no second person managing the shoot while you move, no fixed rig sweeping a predetermined path. You are the rig, the operator, and the quality-control pass, all at once. Breaking the building into rooms is what makes that survivable: a room is a unit small enough to fully cover in a few minutes, and a unit you can mentally check off. COLMAP's own tutorial guidance is to capture images with high visual overlap, make sure every surface is seen in at least three images, and shoot from genuinely different viewpoints rather than rotating in place from one spot. A room gives you a natural boundary for applying that discipline deliberately, instead of trying to hold "have I covered everything" in your head across an entire building at once.

The room boundary also matters for what comes later. COLMAP's sparse reconstruction phase (feature detection, matching, incremental structure-from-motion) benefits from images that overlap richly with their true spatial neighbors. If your mental model of the shoot is "one big pile of photos from the building," you have no way to reason about which images should overlap with which. If your mental model is "twelve rooms, each internally overlapping," you've already done a large part of the matching problem's work for it, because you know the seams: the doorways and hallways where one room's coverage needs to hand off cleanly to the next.

**Why per-room crops, not raw dumps.** Once a room is captured, its images need to become a discrete, addressable unit rather than an undifferentiated stream. This is where the pipeline's second stage lives: crops per room, organized so that later stages can reason about "this is the kitchen" as a first-class concept instead of a set of filenames you have to infer from timestamps. This matters for the same reason room-by-room capture matters: it turns an implicit structure (the operator's memory of the walk) into an explicit one (folders on disk) before that memory has a chance to degrade or get reconstructed incorrectly by someone else.

It also matters because rooms are not created equal from a reconstruction standpoint. COLMAP's own FAQ and tutorial are explicit that texture-poor surfaces are a known weak point, and separate research on SfM matching failure confirms that hand-crafted keypoint detectors like SIFT (COLMAP's default) degrade seriously on weak-texture or repetitive surfaces, becoming the bottleneck for the whole pipeline in that region. A blank hallway or a repetitive tiled bathroom is a harder room than a cluttered kitchen. Keeping crops organized per room means you can look at a folder and immediately know which rooms are the risky ones, rather than discovering the problem only after a building-wide reconstruction fails somewhere in the middle and you have to reverse-engineer which images caused it.

**Why a manifest, and not just a folder of folders.** A pile of per-room folders is progress, but it's still missing the thing that turns a shoot into a dataset: an explicit record of what was captured, in what order, and how the pieces relate. That record is the manifest, and building it correctly, along with the ZIP_STORED packing convention used to bundle the crops without wasting CPU time re-compressing already-entropy-coded JPEGs, is the full subject of *Assembling and Packing the Bundle: manifest.json and ZIP_STORED Crops*. What matters at the level of pipeline shape is simpler: the manifest is the thing that makes the bundle self-describing. Anyone (or anything) downstream that receives the bundle should be able to answer "what is this, and is it complete" without having opened a single image.

**Why the bundle is the unit of handoff, not the individual room folders.** This is the part of the shape that's easiest to get wrong if you've been thinking room by room the whole time: the reconstruction side does not want rooms. It wants a bundle, one coherent thing with a defined contract, described in full in *The Bundle Contract: What Downstream Expects From You*. The reason is straightforward once you look at what COLMAP and a Gaussian-splat trainer actually need. COLMAP's tutorial describes the pipeline as two broad phases, sparse reconstruction and dense reconstruction, and the reference 3D Gaussian Splatting implementation expects a specific directory layout on the other side of that: an images folder and a sparse/0 folder containing the camera and point data COLMAP produces. Getting from your rooms to that layout is COLMAP's job, not yours, and it can only do that job if it receives the whole set of overlapping images at once, because its incremental structure-from-motion process is precisely about finding the overlap and connectivity between images that you, as the operator, experienced sequentially but which the algorithm needs to see all together. Hand off a bundle, not a stream of per-room deliveries, and you give COLMAP the entire graph of overlap to work with from the start.

There is one place this shape is honestly underspecified: how tight the per-room overlap should be for particularly difficult rooms, texture-poor or highly repetitive ones, versus a general building-wide baseline. The available guidance (from Pix4D and CIPA) documents that specific difficult scenarios, like forests or snow, warrant tighter overlap than a general baseline, which supports adjusting overlap per-room by analogy, but no source gives a specific indoor number for a texture-poor room. Treat that as a judgment call informed by the pattern, not a number to look up.

What this means for you as the operator or the tool-builder: the four stages are not bureaucracy layered onto a simple task, they're the direct answer to four separate ways a solo, phone-in-hand capture effort can produce a dataset that COLMAP and a splat trainer cannot use. Capture by room because you're the only one keeping track. Crop by room because reconstruction quality varies by room and you need to be able to see that variance. Build a manifest because a bundle without a description of itself is not really a dataset. And treat the bundle, not the room, as the thing you hand off, because that's the unit the reconstruction pipeline was built to consume. Once you hold that shape clearly, the individual techniques in the rest of this shelf, exposure locking, overlap targets, blur detection, manifest schemas, all slot into a structure you already understand rather than arriving as a list of disconnected rules.
