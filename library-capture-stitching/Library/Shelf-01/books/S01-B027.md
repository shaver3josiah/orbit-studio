# Reflective and Transparent Surfaces: Windows, Mirrors, Glass Doors

What happens when the room you are capturing contains a surface that isn't really there?

A mirror shows you a second room that doesn't exist. A window shows you the parking lot outside, floating impossibly behind the wall. A glass shower door shows you a distorted, doubled version of the bathroom you already photographed. To a human eye these are obviously tricks. To a structure-from-motion pipeline they are just more pixels, and pixels are exactly what COLMAP uses to figure out where your camera was standing and what the room looks like. Feed it a lie and it will faithfully reconstruct the lie.

This book is about that specific failure mode and what a solo operator can actually do about it during capture, not in post.

## Why glass and mirrors are different from a blank wall

The sibling volume "Texture-Poor Walls and Why Blank Surfaces Fail to Register" covers surfaces that give SIFT nothing to grab onto. Reflective and transparent surfaces are the opposite problem, and arguably worse: they give SIFT plenty of sharp, distinctive-looking features, and every one of them is wrong.

A mirror reflects real geometry from the room, so the "features" COLMAP detects on its surface are photometrically strong and often quite stable-looking across a couple of nearby frames. But they don't correspond to the mirror's own surface at all. They correspond to whatever is being reflected, at a depth that has nothing to do with where the glass physically sits. When COLMAP's incremental SfM tries to triangulate those points, it's trying to solve for a 3D location using rays that don't converge on anything real. The notes on COLMAP's own tutorial and FAQ are explicit that the software's official documentation offers no guidance for this case at all: reflective and transparent surfaces are, in the maintainers' own words, a documented gap in the official docs themselves. You are on your own here, which is exactly why this book exists.

Windows and glass doors add a second failure on top of the first: the surface is not just showing you the wrong depth, it's showing you two scenes superimposed, the reflection and the transmitted view straight through the glass, at different intensities depending on your angle and the lighting on each side. That layered, angle-dependent appearance is precisely why the same pane of glass can look like a solid gray wall from one standoff position and a transparent window from another, a few feet to the left.

## Where the damage actually lands

There are two distinct places this goes wrong, and it's worth separating them because the fixes differ.

**Sparse reconstruction and pose.** If a mirror or window occupies a meaningful fraction of the frame, some of the feature matches SIFT proposes across your overlapping shots will be matches against reflected content rather than real, stationary geometry in the room. COLMAP's incremental pipeline builds camera poses from these correspondences. Enough contaminated matches in a region and you get an unstable or outright wrong pose estimate for the frames that included the glass, per the general triangulation and matching behavior described in the arXiv survey on SfM degeneracy in these notes. That survey's core point is broader than mirrors specifically: triangulation depends on having real translational parallax between genuinely corresponding 3D points, and a reflected point is not a real 3D point at all, so no amount of camera movement gives you a reliable depth for it.

**Dense reconstruction and the splat itself.** Even where the sparse pose survives, dense multi-view stereo and the eventual Gaussian-splat training will try to build actual surface geometry where the glass is. A mirror typically ends up as either a hole (no consistent depth estimated at all, so nothing gets filled in) or a phantom extension of the room, a false doorway into a space that shouldn't exist. A window frequently reconstructs as a blurry, semi-transparent smear where indoor and outdoor content blend into one incoherent patch of Gaussians. Neither is subtle once you walk the finished splat.

## What actually reduces the damage

There is no fix in COLMAP's own settings that solves this, because the software isn't aware the glass exists as a category of surface. The mitigation has to happen at capture time, and it comes down to standoff distance, angle, and how much you let the glass dominate the frame.

**Increase standoff, don't decrease it.** The instinct with a hard-to-capture surface is often to move closer and shoot more of it, the way you might compensate for a texture-poor wall by tightening overlap. That instinct is wrong here. Getting closer to a mirror or window increases the fraction of the frame it occupies and increases the specular intensity of the reflection, making the fake content stronger relative to the real room around it. The companion volume "Standoff Distance and the Geometry of a Room" treats standoff as a deliberate, varied parameter rather than one fixed number, following the three-tier far/mid/close pattern described in the Geodetic Systems photogrammetry notes, and glass surfaces are a case where you deliberately choose the far end of that range and keep the surface small in the frame rather than filling it.

**Change your angle across frames, not just your position.** A specular reflection is strongly angle-dependent: the same mirror looks almost blinding from one line of sight and comparatively dim and washed-out from another a few degrees off. Because your capture loop already moves you around the room from multiple headings, no single reflection angle dominates every frame that sees the glass, which limits how many frames get contaminated by the same false content. Shooting the glass from a steep oblique angle rather than straight-on, where practical, further weakens the specular return, similar in spirit to the Brewster's-angle positioning described for suppressing glare in the cross-polarization notes on photogrammetric capture.

**Use cross-polarization if you're capturing something that matters enough to justify it.** The NASA technical report and the archaeological capture guides in the research notes describe an established technique: a polarizing filter on the camera lens, oriented roughly 90 degrees relative to a polarizer on the light source, that suppresses specular reflections at the sensor before they're ever recorded. A single lens-mounted polarizer positioned near Brewster's angle relative to the glass does something similar without needing a polarized light. This is heavier gear than a phone-only Orbit Capture workflow assumes, and it will not be practical for a routine walkthrough. But if a shoot has a mirrored wall or storefront glass that's central to the space, a clip-on circular polarizer on the phone lens, rotated while framing the shot, is a low-effort way to knock down the worst of the specular content before it ever reaches COLMAP.

**Accept the hole, don't fight it with more coverage.** For a routine solo capture, the most reliable strategy is often the least clever one: shoot the mirror or window at a distance and angle that keeps it small and weak in the frame, and let the reconstruction leave a soft or slightly wrong patch there rather than trying to force a correct one. Piling on more images of the glass, the way you'd add overlap for a texture-poor wall, does not help here, because more images of a lie just means more consistent-looking wrong matches for COLMAP to trust. The stairwells volume, "Stairwells, Hallways, and Other Awkward Standoff Geometries," makes a related point about tight, awkward spaces: sometimes the right move is choosing a capture path that minimizes exposure to the hard geometry rather than trying to out-shoot it.

## What this means for the walkthrough

A splat with a slightly wrong mirror or a hazy window is still a usable, walkable scene, and it's an honest one: the wrongness is confined to a small, recognizable patch rather than smeared across the whole reconstruction. The failure mode to actually worry about is a large mirrored wall or floor-to-ceiling glass storefront treated as if it were an ordinary surface, shot up close and head-on the way you'd shoot a textured brick wall. That's the setup that corrupts poses beyond the glass itself, because the false depth estimates and bad matches don't stay neatly contained to the frames that show the mirror. Keep standoff generous, vary your angle, keep the glass small in frame, and treat any large reflective or transparent surface as a place to walk past deliberately rather than a feature to document thoroughly.
