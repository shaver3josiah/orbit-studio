# Stairwells, Hallways, and Other Awkward Standoff Geometries

What do you do when the room won't let you stand far enough back?

The 0.3-1 meter standoff rule assumes you have somewhere to retreat to. A living room, a lobby, an open floor plan: you can always take one more step back and widen your field of view. A stairwell doesn't give you that option. Neither does a coat closet, a service corridor, or a hallway barely wider than your shoulders. The walls are close, the ceiling is close, and the only way to move is forward or backward along a single axis. This book is about what breaks when standoff distance stops being a choice, and what to do about it.

**Why narrow spaces are a different problem, not just a smaller one**

Structure-from-motion depends on translation between viewpoints, not just rotation. Reliable triangulation of 3D points fundamentally requires sufficient parallax, and pure rotation without translation is a well-known degenerate configuration for two-view geometry: the geometry isn't uniquely determined by point correspondences when two views differ only by rotation. In a wide room you get translation for free just by walking your capture loop. In a stairwell or a narrow hallway, the temptation is to plant your feet and pivot, because there's nowhere to step sideways. That's exactly the motion that starves the reconstruction of the parallax it needs (arXiv survey on SfM degeneracy). If you've read Standoff Distance and the Geometry of a Room, this is the same principle turned up to an emergency: in a normal room, standing still and rotating is a bad habit; in a stairwell, it's the default behavior the space pushes you toward, which is why it needs calling out on its own.

The fix isn't a different standoff number. It's accepting that your standoff will be forced tight and compensating with motion instead. Walk the actual length of the hallway or the run of the stairs even when it feels redundant. Every step forward, even a half-step, buys you the translation that a pivot cannot.

**Overlap has to go up when standoff goes down**

Pix4D's best-practice guidance sets a general baseline of at least 75% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap, but explicitly raises that baseline for specific scenarios. Corridor mapping is one of the named cases: at least 85% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap. Interior reconstruction generally is called out as needing overlap around 90%, with terrestrial (ground-based) imagery recommended over other capture methods for interiors. That's not a coincidence. When you're close to a wall, the same forward step covers a bigger share of your field of view than it would in an open room, because the wall fills more of the frame at short range. Each frame captures less new information relative to the last one, so you need more frames to keep the same coverage guarantee.

Practically, this means slowing down in stairwells and hallways more than intuition suggests. If your usual capture cadence gives you comfortable overlap in a bedroom, it likely will not in a 90cm-wide hallway at the same walking pace, because the effective overlap has dropped even though your steps haven't changed length. Shorten your step interval, or increase your shot frequency, until the frame-to-frame change feels almost too small to bother with. In a corridor, "too much overlap" is closer to correct than "just enough" would be in a bigger room.

**Repetitive geometry makes the tight space worse**

Stairwells and hallways don't just constrain your standoff. They tend to be visually repetitive: identical treads, identical risers, evenly spaced doors, matching handrails. This matters because loop closure, the process of recognizing that a current view matches a previously visited location so accumulated drift can be corrected, depends on visual distinctiveness. Indoor environments with high scene similarity, such as repeated layouts or near-identical units, are explicitly documented as a harder case for loop closure, because visual similarity between genuinely different locations increases the risk of matching the wrong instance (PLOS ONE indoor SLAM study). A stairwell landing on floor 3 can look enough like the landing on floor 5 that a matching algorithm confuses them.

COLMAP's own sequential matcher is relevant here. It matches each image against a window of neighbors (a default overlap of 10 consecutive images) and periodically runs loop detection by retrieving visually similar images from a vocabulary tree, matching every Nth image against whatever the tree judges to be its closest visual neighbors. In a long uniform hallway or a stairwell with many identical flights, that similarity search has real ammunition for a false match: two doors on different floors, or two treads on different flights, can present nearly identical local geometry to a matcher that only sees texture and shape, not floor number. The tutorial's own guidance to shoot from genuinely different viewpoints rather than rotating in place from one spot exists for this reason too: repeated views from the same handful of spots make the aliasing problem worse, not better.

There's no dedicated guidance in the source material for solving false loop closure in a solo phone-capture workflow specifically; the loop-closure literature here comes from robotics and SLAM research, not photogrammetry practice, and that gap is worth naming plainly rather than papering over. What the SLAM literature does support is the general shape of the fix: break the visual sameness. Capture continuously along the whole run rather than in disconnected bursts, so consecutive-frame matching (which doesn't depend on distinctiveness) carries more of the reconstruction load than long-range loop matching does. Where you can, include a distinguishing feature in frame, a numbered door, a light fixture, a stain on a particular tread, so that even a generic vocabulary-tree comparison has something non-generic to grab onto.

**Where reflective and awkward-angle problems compound**

Hallways often have exactly the surfaces covered in Reflective and Transparent Surfaces: Windows, Mirrors, Glass Doors, glass fire doors, framed art under glass, polished handrails, and stairwells frequently have the vertical surfaces covered in Vertical Coverage: Ceilings, Floors, and the Frames People Skip, since a stairwell is essentially a vertical shaft where the ceiling of one flight is the floor of the next. None of that goes away because the space is narrow. If anything it gets worse, because a tight standoff means you're often shooting a reflective surface at a steep, close angle rather than the more forgiving oblique angle a spacious room would allow.

**What this means for the operator**

Awkward standoff geometry isn't solved by finding a way to back up that doesn't exist. It's solved by treating the constraint as a signal to change three things at once: walk the space fully instead of pivoting from fixed points, raise your overlap target toward the 85-90% range these tight interior spaces call for, and deliberately break visual repetition so the matcher isn't left guessing which identical-looking tread or door it's actually looking at. Get those three right and a stairwell stops being a room your rule doesn't fit. It becomes a room with a different, but still learnable, rule of its own.
