# Low-Light Rooms Without Supplemental Lighting

What do you do when the room you have to capture is a windowless storage closet, a basement mechanical room, or an interior hallway with the lights off and no permission to turn them on or bring in your own? You cannot meter it the way you meter a living room with a lamp and a window. You cannot expect the phone's auto-exposure to find anything to lock onto. And you still need a bundle of JPGs that COLMAP can register and gsplat can train into something walkable. This book is about the specific adjustments that make that possible: standing back further than feels natural, pushing exposure compensation harder than feels safe, and accepting that the frames will be noisier than you would like.

Start with why the normal approach breaks down. Orbit Capture depends on locked exposure and white balance across a sequence of overlapping stills, precisely because structure-from-motion wants the same surface to look the same from frame to frame. The Android camera stack gives you that lock through CONTROL_AE_LOCK and CONTROL_AWB_LOCK, which freeze auto-exposure and auto-white-balance to whatever values they most recently calculated. In a normal room, you point the camera at something representative, let AE and AWB settle, lock them, and shoot the sequence. In a dark room, that first step fails before it starts. There is nothing bright enough for the auto-exposure algorithm to settle on with confidence, and locking it too early just freezes a bad guess across every frame that follows.

The fix is to stop trusting the algorithm's guess and substitute your own. Exposure compensation exists for exactly this: a deliberate offset applied on top of whatever the AE algorithm proposes, typically within a device-reported range of roughly minus three to plus three EV steps. In a dark room, you push that dial hard toward positive, well past what would look correct in a bright one, then lock AE and AWB at that pushed value before you start the sequence. The goal is not a pleasant-looking exposure. It is an exposure that preserves enough tonal separation on the room's surfaces that a feature detector can find something to grab onto. That decision, how far to push and how to judge you have gone far enough without wrecking a highlight, is the subject of the sibling volume EV Compensation for Windows, Skylights, and Dark Corners, and it deserves its own full treatment rather than a paragraph here. What matters for this book is the direction of the tradeoff: brighter and noisier beats correct and empty.

That leads to the texture problem, which is really the whole reason low light rooms are hard for this pipeline. COLMAP's own tutorial guidance is blunt about avoiding completely texture-less surfaces, because a feature detector needs something with visible structure to latch onto across multiple viewpoints. A dark room does not just lack photons, it actively hides the texture that is physically there. Wood grain, drywall texture, the seams in a concrete floor, all of it gets crushed into a narrow, indistinguishable band of dark pixels when the room is underexposed. Documented work on SIFT (COLMAP's default detector) shows it degrading seriously on weak-texture, repetitive, or low-light surfaces specifically because it becomes hard to detect repeatable keypoints across viewpoints, and that becomes the bottleneck for the whole reconstruction. Underexposing a dark room does not just make the photos look bad. It quietly deletes the very texture SfM needs, before the feature detector ever runs. Pushing EV compensation hard is not a cosmetic choice, it is what keeps that texture visible enough to survive into keypoints.

The cost of pushing exposure that far is noise, and there is no way around it without adding light. Raising exposure without adding photons means the sensor's automatic gain does more of the work, and gain amplifies sensor noise along with the signal. This is the "accepted noise tradeoff" in the book's scope, and it is worth being explicit that it is a tradeoff and not a problem to be solved away. A noisy but texture-preserving frame gives SIFT something to match. A clean but crushed-black frame gives it nothing. Between those two failure modes, noise is the one COLMAP's pipeline can tolerate, because feature matching operates on local contrast and gradient structure, not on a clean noise floor. It is worth flagging one operational wrinkle here: automated blur checks that use the variance of the Laplacian to reject soft frames can be fooled the other direction by heavy sensor noise, since noise itself inflates high-frequency variance and can register as false sharpness. If your capture tool runs that kind of blur gate, a dark-room capture session is exactly the condition where its threshold needs to be treated with more suspicion, not less.

Standoff distance is the other lever, and it works for a more mechanical reason. Photogrammetry guidance on working distance treats standoff as something you vary deliberately across a shoot rather than holding to one fixed number, with different passes at different distances for different levels of detail. In a low-light room the practical argument for standing further back is about controlling how much of the frame's exposure budget goes toward capturing a wide, stable view instead of a tight, shake-prone one. A longer standoff means the phone's own small movements, the ones that are unavoidable in a handheld solo capture, represent a smaller fraction of the frame's field of view, and a shot held further from its subject is less likely to have that motion read as blur at the pixel level the feature detector cares about. This reasoning follows from the same discipline that governs standoff and overlap consistency generally: a stable, deliberately chosen distance held constant through the room, rather than one improvised shot to shot. It is worth being honest that the research behind this shelf did not turn up a source addressing indoor low-light standoff as its own named guideline; the recommendation here is inference from adjacent photogrammetry and camera-shake literature, not a directly quoted number, and a capture operator should treat "stand back further in the dark" as a sound default rather than a precisely calibrated rule.

One more consequence of underexposed, noisy captures is worth naming even though this book will not resolve it: a dark room often forces you to increase overlap beyond what a well-lit room would need, because a marginal keypoint match in a noisy, low-contrast frame is less reliable than the same match in a clean one, and more overlapping views give the matcher more chances to confirm a correspondence rather than accept a spurious one. This is the same logic that shows up in outdoor photogrammetry guidance, where scenarios with inherently weaker or more repetitive texture (forests, agricultural fields, snow) get pushed to higher overlap targets than the general baseline. Nothing in the research behind this shelf gives an indoor-specific number to replace that baseline with, so treat "more overlap in the dark" as the same kind of informed default as the standoff recommendation, not a hard rule.

Put together, a dark room without supplemental lighting asks you to break two habits that serve you well everywhere else. You do not trust the camera's auto-exposure, you override it hard toward brighter than looks natural, then lock it. And you do not treat noise as a defect to be minimized at all costs, you treat it as the acceptable cost of keeping texture alive for the feature detector. Once a room is captured this way, the transition into a normally lit adjacent room becomes its own problem, covered in Mixed Lighting Zones: Transition Strategy Between Rooms, and the exposure judgment call itself, how to read the histogram and know when you have pushed far enough, is covered in full in Clipping and Banding: Reading a Histogram in the Field. Neither substitutes for the core habit this book asks you to build: in the dark, exposure discipline and standoff discipline matter more than they do anywhere else in the building, precisely because there is no light to fall back on when they slip.
