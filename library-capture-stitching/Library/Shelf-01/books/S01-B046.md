# Multi-Room Buildings with Repeating Geometry: Avoiding False Loop Closures

Walk a hotel corridor and open five doors in a row. The rooms are, for all practical purposes, the same room. Same bed position, same window trim, same bathroom tile, same light fixture bolted to the same spot on the ceiling. That is good news for the hotel's construction budget and bad news for your capture pipeline. A reconstruction algorithm that is supposed to recognize "I have been here before" has no reliable way to tell room 214 from room 219 if the two look almost identical from the camera's point of view. It will happily decide it has looped back to a room it already has, when in fact it has walked into a twin down the hall. The question this book answers is why that happens and what a solo operator actually does about it before it wrecks a scan.

## What a loop closure is actually for

Structure-from-motion pipelines like COLMAP build a scene incrementally, adding one image's estimated pose after another. Small errors in each pose estimate accumulate as you go, the way a hand-drawn map drifts if you never check it against a known landmark. Loop closure is the check: when the pipeline (or a broader SLAM system) recognizes that the current view matches somewhere it has already mapped, it uses that match to correct the accumulated drift across everything in between. The concept comes out of visual SLAM research, not photogrammetry specifically, but the mechanism is the same one underlying vocabulary-tree loop detection in COLMAP: compare current visual data against historical map data, recognize a revisited location, and use that match to fix the trajectory. The companion volume Loop Closure in the Field: Returning to Where You Started covers the healthy version of this, where you deliberately walk back to your starting point so the pipeline has a real seam to stitch.

The problem in a repeating-geometry building is that the recognition step is doing pattern matching on visual appearance, and visual appearance is exactly what got standardized across the rooms. The published literature on indoor SLAM is blunt about this: environments with high scene similarity, explicitly including repeated room layouts and identical office or hotel-room interiors, are documented as a harder case for loop closure, because the visual similarity between genuinely different locations increases the risk of the algorithm matching the wrong instance. That is not a hypothetical edge case. It is a named, studied failure mode in the field this pipeline borrows from.

## Why the underlying features cooperate with the mistake

It helps to understand what a feature matcher is actually looking at. COLMAP's default detector, SIFT, and comparable hand-crafted keypoint methods work by finding distinctive, repeatable local patterns, corners, edges, and reproducing them across viewpoints. That works well when a scene has enough unique texture to give each location its own visual fingerprint. Weak-texture, repetitive-pattern surfaces are documented to seriously degrade this kind of matching, because it becomes hard to detect repeatable keypoints that are actually distinguishable across different locations rather than merely similar-looking. A hallway of identical doors, a floor of identical cubicles, a row of identical hotel rooms: these are the textbook conditions under which correspondence-based matching starts confusing "looks the same" with "is the same." The matcher is not broken. It is doing exactly what it was built to do, matching similar patterns, in an environment engineered by an architect to be full of similar patterns.

This is a distinct problem from the ordinary drift that loop closure normally fixes. Ordinary drift is a real seam in a genuinely unique scene, corrected by a real match. A false loop closure is a fabricated seam between two different physical spaces that happen to share a visual signature. Fixing the first makes the model more accurate. Fixing the second, if the pipeline actually acts on it, warps two separate rooms into the same location, which is a far worse failure than the drift it was supposed to prevent.

## Where this bites in a COLMAP-plus-gsplat pipeline

Two mechanisms in the pipeline are worth naming specifically, because they are where a repeating floor plan turns into a wrong answer rather than a warning.

The first is sequential matching's own loop detection. COLMAP's sequential matcher, built for ordered walks through a space, periodically checks the current image against a set of visually similar images pulled from a pre-trained vocabulary tree, at intervals controlled by a loop-detection-period setting. That vocabulary tree ranks images purely by visual similarity of local features. It has no floor plan, no room number, no notion that "similar-looking" and "same location" are different claims in a building with repeated layouts. If room 214 and room 219 are furnished identically, the vocabulary tree can rank an image from 219 as a strong match against an image from 214, and the matcher will propose a correspondence between two frames that were never actually looking at the same wall.

The second is the geo-registration and alignment step, which COLMAP documents as needing at least three images of a given object or point to register it reliably. That threshold assumes the images genuinely observe the same point. If a false match feeds a room-219 image into the correspondence set for a room-214 point, the three-image minimum can be technically satisfied while the underlying assumption, that all three images are looking at the same physical spot, is false. The pipeline does not know the difference between "three real observations of one point" and "three observations split across two points that happen to look alike." It just counts to three and proceeds.

## What actually distinguishes twin rooms, if the camera can catch it

The fix is not a magic setting. It is recognizing that repeated layouts share furniture and finish but rarely share every incidental detail, and steering your capture and your matching strategy toward the details that do differ. Room numbers on doors and door frames, window views to the outside (which differ by which side of the building a room sits on), wall art, cable runs, thermostat placement, scuffs and wear patterns unique to that specific room, and any built-in signage are all real, physical differences between otherwise-identical rooms. A capture pass that reliably includes the doorway, the room number plaque, and at least one window in every room gives the matcher unique anchors to work with instead of leaving it to adjudicate between two nearly indistinguishable interiors.

This same logic should shape your route through the building, which is the subject of Routing a Room-by-Room Walk Without Backtracking: a walk that moves through repeated units in a consistent, ordered sequence, always entering through the door and always capturing the corridor context on the way in and out, gives sequential matching a strong temporal signal to lean on alongside the weak visual one. Sequential matching already privileges nearby-in-sequence images, images captured close together in time are checked against each other first. A disciplined, non-backtracking route makes that temporal ordering trustworthy, so the pipeline has less reason to reach for a distant vocabulary-tree match in the first place.

It also argues for the adaptive-overlap thinking laid out in Adaptive Overlap: When to Shoot Tighter Than 70-80%. General guidance sits around 70 to 80 percent frontal overlap, but scenario-specific work consistently pushes that number up for harder visual conditions, forests, snow, dense vegetation, all get boosted past baseline in published capture guidance. A repeating floor plan is exactly the kind of harder condition that argument extends to, even though no source in this field specifically names "repeating room layouts" as its own overlap tier. Tighter overlap through each individual room means more of that room's unique detail, its scuffs, its specific window, its exact furniture placement, ends up captured redundantly enough to give the matcher something distinctive to hold onto, rather than a handful of wide, generic frames that could describe any room on the floor.

## What this means for the walk

None of this shows up as an error message. A false loop closure does not crash the reconstruction, it quietly bends it, and the failure is often only visible once you are walking the finished splat and notice a hallway that folds back on itself or a room that inherited the wrong window view. The defense is upstream of the algorithm: capture doorways and room numbers as a habit, route the building so your sequence is orderly rather than looping back through similar spaces out of order, and treat repeated-layout rooms as a texture-poor condition worth shooting tighter than you would a unique space. The pipeline cannot tell two identical rooms apart on its own. Your job on the walk is to make sure they were never actually identical in what the camera saw.
