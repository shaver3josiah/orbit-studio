# Where Reconstruction Quality Is Decided: A Map of Shelf 01's Capture Craft

Shelf 01 owns one piece of territory and defends it stubbornly: everything that happens before a single byte reaches COLMAP or a gsplat trainer. That sounds like a modest claim until you sit with its implication, which is the argument this whole shelf keeps returning to in different forms. Reconstruction quality is not decided in software. It is decided in the building, by a person walking rooms with a phone, making a few dozen small decisions about distance, exposure, pacing, and coverage that no downstream algorithm can undo if they go wrong. A blown-out window, a room shot from one spot by rotating in place, a hallway walked too fast for the overlap it needed: none of these show up as error messages. They show up two hours later as a warped splat, a rejected registration, or a hole in a wall that was never actually there, and by then the operator is home and the building is someone else's problem to get back into.

For a solo operator building or using an Orbit Capture-style tool, that is the whole stakes of this shelf. There is no second person on this job watching a monitor for clipped highlights, no one to notice a missed corner, no crew member to say "we already got that wall." Every judgment call a film crew would split across two or three people has to be made by one person, in real time, room by room, and made correctly the first time because there usually isn't a second visit. Shelf 01 exists to convert that impossible-sounding constraint into a repeatable, learnable discipline: a small number of physical properties (overlap, parallax, exposure consistency, sharpness, coverage) that a photoset either has or doesn't, and a set of habits, checks, and on-device tools that let a single person guarantee all of them without a crew.

## The foundational cluster: naming the four things that make a photoset usable

The shelf opens by defining its terms before it gets practical, and it's worth reading these books as a set rather than picking one. **What Makes a Photoset Reconstructable** lays out the core claim directly: overlap, parallax, exposure consistency, and sharpness are the four properties a structure-from-motion pipeline actually needs, and a photoset can look complete to the eye while quietly failing any one of them. **The Parallax Requirement: Why Pure Rotation Kills a Splat** takes the property people most often confuse with overlap and shows why it is a separate, non-negotiable requirement: two-view geometry needs a real translational baseline, and a camera that only rotates in place produces a mathematically degenerate case no amount of matched features can rescue. **Overlap as the Currency of Structure-from-Motion** and **Standoff Distance and the Geometry of a Room** fill in the other two load-bearing numbers, the 70-80% overlap target and the 0.3 meter minimum standoff, and explain why they're linked rather than independent dials.

Sitting alongside these is the cluster's other job: framing the whole discipline around the solo operator. **One Person, One Building: The Solo Operator Constraint** is the clearest statement of why this shelf reads differently from generic photogrammetry guidance built for crews, and **From Building to Bundle: The Shape of the Orbit Capture Pipeline** gives the end-to-end shape everything else fits into. **Exposure Consistency as a First-Class Requirement**, **Reading a Room Before You Shoot It**, **What Can Go Wrong: A Field Taxonomy of Bad Captures**, and **The Bundle Contract: What Downstream Expects From You** round out this tier, and they earn the word foundational honestly: nothing in the applied or implementation clusters makes sense until these ideas are load-bearing in the reader's own head.

## The applied cluster: turning numbers into field habits

The second tier takes those abstract requirements and converts them into things a person can actually do while standing in a room with a clock running. **Routing a Room-by-Room Walk Without Backtracking** and **Doorways, Thresholds, and Keeping Continuity Between Rooms** solve the path problem: how to move through a building so overlap stays continuous and nothing gets shot twice out of confusion. **The 70-80% Overlap Target in Practice: Counting Steps, Not Percentages** is maybe the shelf's single most useful trick, converting an abstract percentage into a step-and-shoot cadence an operator can hold in their head without a calculator, and **Working the 1-3 Minute Room: Pacing a Capture Session** turns that cadence into a repeatable rhythm against a real time budget.

Exposure and white balance get their own tight sub-cluster here: **Metering Off a Mid-Tone Wall and Locking It**, **AE Lock: What It Freezes and What It Doesn't**, **The Unlock-Set-EV-Relock Dance**, **EV Compensation for Windows, Skylights, and Dark Corners**, **Clipping and Banding: Reading a Histogram in the Field**, **AWB Lock vs. a True Fixed-Kelvin White Balance**, and **Carrying One White Balance Across Differently-Lit Rooms**. Read together, these seven books are a complete field method for a problem that has no forgiveness once the walk is done: once a room's exposure is locked wrong, every frame in it inherits the mistake. Two code volumes sit in this tier as well, **Estimating Capture Blur Risk with a Laplacian-Variance Score** and **A Field Overlap Estimator: Comparing Consecutive Frames**, which start turning the eyeball checks into scripts.

## The implementation cluster: building the tool that enforces the discipline

The third tier is where the shelf stops describing what a good operator does and starts building the on-device app that makes good behavior the default rather than a matter of willpower. On the Kotlin and Camera2 side, **Camera2 Capture Session Basics for a Locked-Exposure App** lays the plumbing every later implementation book assumes, then **Implementing AE Lock and a Fixed Metering Region in Camera2**, **Coding the Unlock-Set-EV-Relock Sequence in Kotlin**, and **Reading and Applying AWB Lock State via Camera2** wire the exposure and white-balance discipline from the applied cluster directly into the camera API. **Capturing Frame Metadata: ISO, Shutter, EV, Timestamp**, **On-Device Laplacian-Variance Blur Scoring in Kotlin**, **Flagging and Triggering a Reshoot Mid-Session**, and **Building the Room-by-Room Session State Machine** build the rest of the capture app around that foundation, and **Writing Per-Room Crops to Disk with Consistent Naming** and **Assembling and Packing the Bundle: manifest.json and ZIP_STORED Crops** close the loop by producing the actual artifact, a `bundle.zip` with a manifest and crops, that Shelf 02 will consume. This last book is a good example of the shelf's practical register: it explains not just how to zip a bundle but why `ZIP_STORED` beats `ZIP_DEFLATED` here specifically, because JPEGs are already entropy-coded and burning CPU to compress them again buys nothing.

This tier also carries the shelf's harder essays on failure geometry: **Texture-Poor Walls and Why Blank Surfaces Fail to Register**, **Reflective and Transparent Surfaces: Windows, Mirrors, Glass Doors**, **Stairwells, Hallways, and Other Awkward Standoff Geometries**, **Mixed Lighting Zones: Transition Strategy Between Rooms**, **Loop Closure in the Field: Returning to Where You Started**, **Vertical Coverage: Ceilings, Floors, and the Frames People Skip**, and the trio on knowing when to stop: **The Cost of a Missed Room: Why Reshoots Are Cheaper On-Site**, **Deciding On-Site Whether a Room Needs a Reshoot**, and **Troubleshooting a Session That Feels Wrong Before You Leave**, plus **Handoff Discipline: What the Operator Owes the Pipeline**.

## The advanced cluster: the edge cases and the automation that closes the loop

The final tier handles the situations that break the general rules and the tooling that stops asking the operator to make judgment calls at all. **Multi-Room Buildings with Repeating Geometry: Avoiding False Loop Closures** is the sharpest essay on this shelf: it explains why a hotel corridor of identical rooms can trick a loop-closure algorithm into matching the wrong instance entirely, a failure that doesn't crash anything, it just quietly bends the model. **Low-Light Rooms Without Supplemental Lighting** and **Adaptive Overlap: When to Shoot Tighter Than 70-80%** cover the other edge conditions, dark rooms and texture-poor or repetitive geometry, where the general-case numbers from the foundational tier need deliberate adjustment. The two closing code volumes, **Building a Pre-Upload Capture Health Check on Device** and **Automating Reshoot Triggers from Blur and Overlap Scores Together**, fuse the blur scorer, coverage tracker, and exposure metadata built earlier in the shelf into a single pass or fail gate, moving the shelf's whole argument from "here is what a good operator does" to "here is a tool that checks it for you before you leave the room."

## A reading path

Start with the four foundational essays on reconstructability and parallax, **What Makes a Photoset Reconstructable** and **The Parallax Requirement**, then read **One Person, One Building** and **From Building to Bundle** to get the shelf's operating premise. That's the load-bearing core; everything else assumes it. From there, move into the applied cluster in the order the exposure sub-series is written, metering, AE lock, the unlock-set-relock dance, EV compensation, histogram reading, since each book depends on the one before it. Read the overlap-and-pacing books (**The 70-80% Overlap Target in Practice**, **Working the 1-3 Minute Room**) alongside them rather than after, because pacing and exposure decisions happen in the same ten seconds on site.

The implementation cluster is best read by whichever half of the job you're doing. An operator who just wants field discipline can skim it for the reasoning and skip the Kotlin. An engineer building the capture app should read it start to finish in order, since the Camera2 books build directly on each other. The failure-geometry essays in that tier (texture-poor walls, reflective surfaces, awkward standoff geometries, mixed lighting) are reference material: dip into whichever one matches the building in front of you rather than reading them cover to cover.

The advanced cluster rewards being read last, after the general rules are solid, because every book in it is explicitly about when to break those rules. Treat **What Can Go Wrong: A Field Taxonomy of Bad Captures** as a companion reference throughout, not a one-time read: it's the closest thing this shelf has to an index of symptoms, worth returning to whenever a scan feels wrong and you're not sure why.

## Carrying this forward to Shelf 02

Shelf 02, Stitching It Together: From Frames to a 3D Scene, picks up exactly where this shelf's bundle handoff ends. Everything Shelf 01 teaches about overlap, parallax, exposure, and coverage is not background color for that shelf, it is the input contract its matching, pose recovery, and gsplat training assume was satisfied. A reader arriving at Shelf 02 should carry forward the manifest and bundle shape from **Assembling and Packing the Bundle** and **The Bundle Contract: What Downstream Expects From You**, the loop-closure discipline from **Loop Closure in the Field** and its repeating-geometry edge case, and above all the working knowledge that every diagnostic Shelf 02 runs, a failed registration, a warped point cloud, a bad loop closure, traces back to a capture decision made on this shelf. Shelf 02 will tell you what went wrong in the language of algorithms. Shelf 01 is what tells you, before you ever leave the building, whether it was going to go wrong at all.
