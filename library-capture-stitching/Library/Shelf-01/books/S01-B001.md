# What Makes a Photoset Reconstructable

Before you open COLMAP, before you touch gsplat, before you even think about training a splat, there is a single question that decides whether any of that software can help you: is this photoset reconstructable? Not "are the photos pretty." Not "did I get every room." Reconstructable means a structure-from-motion pipeline can look at your JPGs and recover, with confidence, where the camera was standing for each one and where every point in the scene sits in 3D space. If it can't do that, nothing downstream matters. A gorgeous, well-lit, perfectly framed set of photos that fails this test produces a broken point cloud, a splat full of floating debris, or an outright registration failure.

The good news is that reconstructability is not mysterious. It comes down to four properties, and every capture failure you will ever debug traces back to one of them being missing. Overlap. Parallax. Exposure consistency. Sharpness. Get all four and the pipeline has what it needs. Lose any one and you are asking software to solve a problem it fundamentally cannot solve, no matter how many flags you tune.

**Overlap** is the most basic requirement, and it is also the one COLMAP's own documentation is blunt about. The tutorial's capture guidance is explicit: shoot with high visual overlap, and make sure every object or surface appears in at least three images, with more being better. That "at least three" is not a soft suggestion, either. COLMAP's FAQ states it as a hard minimum for geo-registration and alignment estimation, quoting the requirement directly: "Make sure that each object is seen in at least 3 images." Two images of a wall corner is not enough for the pipeline to triangulate it reliably. Three overlapping views is the floor.

Overlap is also the subject of its own book on this shelf, Overlap as the Currency of Structure-from-Motion, because it deserves a full treatment of exactly how much is enough. For now, the headline numbers are worth knowing. Pix4D's photogrammetry guidance recommends at least 75% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap as a general baseline, and pushes that higher for harder scenes: 90% for interior reconstruction specifically. Interiors get singled out because they combine two problems at once, repetitive geometry (walls, corners, identical doorways) and tight quarters that limit how far the camera can move between shots. The CIPA 3x3 Rules for architectural photogrammetry, an older but still-cited standard, put a floor of 60% overlap between successive photographs, rising to at least one-third for convergent, angled pairs. Different traditions, same conclusion: reconstructable means dense overlap, not photos of "the whole room" taken from four corners.

**Parallax** is the property people confuse with overlap, and confusing them is the single most common way a capture dies without the operator realizing it until the splat comes out warped or refuses to solve at all. Overlap asks whether two images share the same content. Parallax asks whether the camera actually moved between those two images, so that shared content is seen from meaningfully different positions. You can produce enormous overlap by standing in one spot and rotating in place, taking a full panorama of a room from a single point. Every frame overlaps its neighbors heavily. And it is nearly useless for reconstruction, because pure rotation without translation is a documented degenerate case in two-view geometry. When the camera only rotates, the fundamental matrix that relates two views isn't uniquely determined by the point correspondences, and triangulation, the actual math that turns matched pixels into a 3D point, requires a baseline, a real displacement between camera positions, to resolve depth at all. Abundant, well-matched features do not save you if there is no baseline behind them.

This is exactly why COLMAP's own tutorial warns against it directly, telling operators not to shoot from the same location, and to capture from genuinely different viewpoints rather than rotating in place. A companion volume on this shelf, The Parallax Requirement: Why Pure Rotation Kills a Splat, goes deep on this specific failure mode, because for a solo operator walking a building it is the easiest mistake to make without noticing. Standing in a doorway and swinging your phone across a room feels like thorough coverage. It is thorough coverage of the wrong variable. What the pipeline needs is you walking, genuinely changing position, so that the same wall corner is triangulated from two meaningfully separated vantage points.

**Exposure consistency** is where the phone-camera version of this workflow runs into a hazard that a dedicated photogrammetry rig doesn't face as badly: auto-everything. A modern phone camera left on automatic will re-meter every single frame, adjusting exposure and white balance independently shot to shot. Two photos of the identical wall, taken seconds apart, can come out different brightness and different color temperature purely because the auto-exposure and auto-white-balance algorithms recalculated. For SIFT-style feature matching, the algorithm COLMAP uses by default, that inconsistency degrades the very correspondences the whole pipeline depends on, and it is compounded by the fact that Gaussian splatting's reference training pipeline itself expects images captured with reasonably stable exposure, since the optimizer is fitting radiance to pixel color, not correcting for per-frame brightness drift on its own.

The fix lives in the camera API, not in post-processing. Android's Camera2 interface exposes CONTROL_AE_LOCK and CONTROL_AWB_LOCK specifically so an application can freeze auto-exposure and auto-white-balance to their last calculated values and stop them from drifting frame to frame, described in the platform documentation as locking to "its latest calculated values." The practical workflow is: let the camera meter and white-balance once on a representative part of the scene, lock both, then shoot the rest of that room's photoset with those locked values held constant. Photography convention backs this up outside of Android's own docs too. Fixed white balance is the standard recommendation whenever a technique depends on color consistency across many frames, with panoramas and blended-exposure sequences named as the textbook examples, precisely because auto white balance is known to drift under mixed or indoor lighting even when nothing else in the scene changes. Consistent exposure is not a nicety here. It is a precondition, in the same category as overlap and parallax, for the same reason: the pipeline is comparing pixels across images and assuming they represent the same underlying surface color and brightness.

**Sharpness** is the simplest of the four to explain and the easiest to check for automatically. A blurry frame, whether from camera shake or from a rushed walking pace, has fewer distinct edges for a feature detector to lock onto, and the features it does find are noisier and less repeatable across views. The standard cheap check for this is a single line of OpenCV: run a Laplacian filter over a grayscale image and take the variance of the result. Sharp images produce high variance because they have abundant edges and rapid intensity changes; blurry images produce low variance because they don't. It is not a universal threshold, the original write-up on this technique is explicit that any cutoff score is subjective to image content and needs tuning per dataset, but the direction of the signal is reliable enough to flag obviously soft frames for a re-shoot before they ever reach COLMAP. On the capture side, the classic reciprocal rule for handheld shooting, keep shutter speed at least the reciprocal of the focal length to avoid camera-shake blur, still applies to a phone walking a room, even though "focal length" for a phone's fixed wide lens is a smaller and more forgiving number than it is for an interchangeable-lens camera.

None of these four requirements is exotic, and none of them individually is hard to satisfy. What makes them worth naming together as a single test is that a photoset can look complete to the eye while silently failing one of them, and the failure only becomes visible once you have already spent the walk-through, already fed the bundle into COLMAP, and gotten back a sparse point cloud full of holes or a rejected registration. Checking overlap, parallax, exposure, and sharpness before you leave the building is cheaper than debugging a broken reconstruction after you get home. A separate volume on this shelf, What Can Go Wrong: A Field Taxonomy of Bad Captures, catalogs the specific symptoms each kind of failure produces once it reaches the pipeline, but the fix always starts here, at the four properties that made the photoset reconstructable or not in the first place.
