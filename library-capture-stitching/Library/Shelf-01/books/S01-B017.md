# The Unlock-Set-EV-Relock Dance

You are three rooms into a scan. The hallway you just walked was bright, sun coming in sideways through a window, and you locked exposure to keep every frame consistent. Now you're stepping into a windowless bathroom, and the locked settings from the hallway will render it as a black hole. Do you unlock and let auto-exposure fix it, and if so, how do you get back to a locked, stable state without breaking the very thing exposure lock was protecting?

That's the problem this book covers: how to change exposure bias in the middle of a capture without losing the consistency the lock was supposed to guarantee.

## Why you can't just leave it locked, and why you can't just leave it unlocked

The sibling volume *AE Lock: What It Freezes and What It Doesn't* covers the mechanics of CONTROL_AE_LOCK in detail, but the short version matters here: once you lock auto-exposure, the camera freezes it "to its latest calculated values" and holds there regardless of what the lens now sees. That's exactly what you want walking through a hallway with mixed light, because Gaussian-splat training assumes photometric consistency across the input set. The reference gsplat implementation (Kerbl et al., "3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering") builds its scene from a COLMAP sparse reconstruction, and COLMAP's own tutorial guidance is explicit that captures should use "similar illumination avoiding high dynamic range scenes." A lock is how a solo operator with one phone and no lighting rig approximates that discipline in a real building.

But a single global exposure lock breaks down the moment the building doesn't cooperate with the assumption. A gray-carpeted hallway and a dark storage closet are not one exposure away from each other, they're stops away. If you hold the hallway's exposure walking into the closet, you get crushed blacks with no recoverable shadow detail. If you had instead locked to the closet's exposure and carried it back into the hallway, you'd blow the highlights. Neither failure is subtle, and neither is fixable by "boosting shadows in post," because the underlying scene texture (the thing your feature matcher needs) was never captured. Locked-wrong is worse than unlocked, because unlocked at least degrades gracefully frame by frame, while locked-wrong looks identical to your correctly-exposed frames until you actually inspect the pixels.

So the lock has to be defeatable, deliberately, at the exact boundary between two lighting zones, and it has to come back afterward. That three-step motion is the dance: unlock, set exposure compensation, relock.

## What actually happens in each step

Unlocking is simple: set CONTROL_AE_LOCK back to false (or in CameraX terms, stop treating the lock as active) and let the auto-exposure algorithm start re-metering the new scene. This is the moment of vulnerability. The algorithm needs a frame or two to converge, and if you fire your capture shutter during that window you get a genuinely wrong exposure, not a locked-but-stale one.

Setting exposure compensation is where you inject your own judgment on top of what the algorithm converges to. On Android, CameraX exposes this through `Camera.CameraControl.setExposureCompensationIndex()`, where the actual EV shift equals the index times a step size, positive brightening and negative dimming, with the supported range and step queried from `CameraInfo.exposureState`. This is a different control from the lock itself; the Android CaptureRequest reference (and the corresponding javadoc mirror for `CONTROL_AE_LOCK`) cross-links exposure compensation as a separately configured field, which confirms that locking and biasing are two independent levers, not one setting doing double duty. That separation is exactly why the dance works: you don't have to fight the lock to bias the exposure, you unlock, bias, then lock again on the biased result.

Why bias at all instead of just trusting auto-exposure's new reading? Because auto-exposure targets an average, typically calibrated around the 18% gray assumption that underlies all TTL metering (the same assumption that makes gray-card metering useful, as covered in the sibling volume on EV compensation for tricky rooms). A skylight-lit room or a room with one dark corner and one bright window will get an auto-exposure reading that's a compromise, not a correct exposure for the surface you actually care about capturing texture on. Exposure compensation is how you push that compromise toward "expose to the right" without clipping, per the standard histogram-reading guidance: push brightness up until you're near the highlight edge but not clipped, because clipped highlights are unrecoverable and posterized gradients come from underexposing a smooth region. The sibling volume on EV compensation across windows, skylights, and dark corners covers the room-specific judgment calls; this book is about the mechanical sequence that makes those judgment calls actionable mid-scan.

Relocking is the step operators skip when they're in a hurry, and it's the one that matters most. Once you've dialed in a compensation value that looks right on the new room, you re-engage CONTROL_AE_LOCK so every subsequent frame in that room holds the same exposure. Skipping this and shooting the whole new room in auto-exposure mode reintroduces the original problem: auto-exposure will keep re-metering as you walk, and a slight shift in framing (more window in one frame, more wall in the next) will nudge every frame's brightness independently. That's the frame-to-frame drift that breaks photometric consistency, the exact thing the whole lock discipline exists to prevent.

## Why the sequence has to be atomic, not just correct

The three steps have to happen as one deliberate action at a room boundary, not as ongoing fiddling. If you unlock, adjust, relock, and then adjust again mid-room because the new exposure still looks slightly off, you've created a second discontinuity inside what should be one consistent lighting zone. COLMAP's incremental structure-from-motion doesn't know about your rooms, it only sees a stream of images and tries to match overlapping features between them. An exposure jump in the middle of a supposedly uniform room shows up to the matcher exactly like an exposure jump between rooms, except now it's unexplained by geometry. The fix is discipline, not tooling: commit to a compensation value before you relock, and only run the dance again at the next real lighting boundary.

There's also a timing detail worth being honest about, and it comes straight from the gap in the underlying camera documentation rather than folklore: because the camera pipeline processes requests in flight, "the settings that end up locked don't necessarily match the settings in the most recent CaptureResult received." In practice this means you should not fire your capture shot on the exact frame you set the lock, you should let one or two frames pass so the pipeline has actually caught up to the locked state before you trust it. This is a small margin, but it's the difference between a lock that's cosmetically set and one that's actually holding the values you think it's holding.

None of this requires exotic hardware or a lighting kit. It requires a phone camera API that exposes lock and compensation as separate controls, and an operator who treats "changing rooms" as a scripted three-beat action rather than an improvised fumble with sliders. If you're the engineer building the capture tool rather than the one walking the building, the practical implementation of exactly this sequence, with real Kotlin code against CameraX, is covered in *Coding the Unlock-Set-EV-Relock Sequence in Kotlin*. What this book gives you is the reasoning for why the sequence exists and why all three steps, in that order, atomically, are non-negotiable: skip the unlock and you carry the wrong exposure into a new room; skip the compensation and you inherit auto-exposure's mediocre compromise; skip the relock and you invite drift back into a zone that needs to hold still.
