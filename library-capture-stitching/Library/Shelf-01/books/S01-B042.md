# Handoff Discipline: What the Operator Owes the Pipeline

When is a bundle actually done? Not "done" in the sense of "I walked every room," but done in the sense that a reconstruction pipeline can pick it up and produce a walkable scene without the operator needing to be in the room to explain what they meant. That's the question this book answers: what is the minimum bar a bundle must clear before it's fair to hand it off, and why does each item on that bar exist.

The instinct of a new operator is to treat capture as the whole job and handoff as an afterthought, a matter of dragging a folder of JPGs somewhere. That instinct is wrong, and it's wrong in a specific, costly way. COLMAP's own tutorial documentation is blunt about what it needs from its input: images with high visual overlap, every surface seen in at least three views, no completely texture-less stretches, consistent illumination, and genuinely different viewpoints rather than repeated shots from one spot. None of that is enforced by the camera. It's enforced by the operator, in the field, and if it isn't there, the pipeline doesn't fail loudly. It fails quietly, in the form of a hole in the mesh, a smeared wall, or a reconstruction that drifts and never recovers, discovered by an engineer hours later staring at a broken point cloud with no way to ask the walkthrough what happened.

So handoff discipline is really a contract. The operator owes the pipeline three things: coverage, an exposure log, and naming. Get those three right and the reconstruction side can do its job without guessing. Get any one of them wrong and you've shipped ambiguity downstream, where it's much more expensive to resolve.

## Coverage: the three-times rule and why it isn't the whole story

COLMAP's FAQ states a hard minimum plainly: each object or point needs to be seen in at least three images to be geo-registered reliably. That's not a suggestion, it's closer to a mathematical floor, the same way a single microphone can't triangulate a sound's origin. Three is the minimum for the math to work at all, not the number that makes a good reconstruction.

The industry numbers for "good" run considerably higher than three. Pix4D's best-practices guidance sets a general baseline of at least 75% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap, and then raises that baseline for harder scenarios: forests and dense vegetation need 85%, snow and sand need 85% frontal and 70% side, urban and interior work is called out specifically as needing around 90% overlap, with terrestrial (ground-based) capture preferred over other methods for interiors. The older CIPA 3x3 Rules for architectural photogrammetry set a lower floor, 60% between successive photographs and a third for angled pairs, though the secondary literature summarizing CIPA is careful to note those numbers are dated relative to modern capture technology and shouldn't be treated as gospel for an indoor phone workflow today.

Put those together and the practical rule for an Orbit Capture operator is: assume the interior-scenario number, not the general one. Ninety percent overlap in practice means walking slower and shooting more than feels necessary, especially in hallways and corners where a texture-poor wall can eat several frames' worth of the algorithm's patience. The research notes for this shelf are honest about a limit here: there is no single authoritative source stating a specific tighter overlap percentage for texture-poor or repetitive-geometry rooms as its own named guideline. What exists is an inference from the pattern Pix4D and CIPA both show, that overlap requirements rise for harder scenes, applied by analogy to indoor capture rather than quoted as a stated figure. Say that plainly rather than pretending there's a citable number: when a room looks hard (blank walls, repeating tile, low light), shoot tighter than the baseline, because the pattern across every documented hard-scene case points that direction even where no single indoor number exists.

Coverage also has a failure mode that isn't about overlap percentage at all: pure rotation. Structure-from-motion depends on translational baseline between views, the camera actually moving through space, not just turning in place. A camera that pans from one spot without walking gives the algorithm angle but no parallax, and depth can't be reliably resolved without parallax no matter how many features match. COLMAP's tutorial says this directly: don't take images from the same location. An operator who spins in the center of a room to "get the walls" has produced overlap without translation, and the bundle will show it.

## The exposure log: consistency the camera won't give you for free

Coverage gets a room seen. Exposure discipline gets it seen the same way twice, which matters because reconstruction pipelines assume a scene's appearance is stable across frames, not just its geometry.

Auto-exposure and auto-white-balance are both designed to re-optimize per frame, which is exactly wrong for a bundle meant to be stitched together. Android's own CaptureRequest documentation describes CONTROL_AE_LOCK and CONTROL_AWB_LOCK as freezing the algorithm's last computed values in place, and recommends locking both, or setting both off explicitly, specifically to get consistent behavior across devices rather than leaving it to chance. Fstoppers' guidance on white balance makes the practical case: auto white balance is fine for one shot under stable light, but it visibly drifts frame-to-frame under mixed or indoor lighting, and any technique depending on cross-frame color consistency, panoramas, focus stacks, blended sequences, needs a fixed Kelvin or fixed preset instead. A roomful of JPGs where every fifth frame has a slightly different color cast isn't a coverage problem, it's an exposure-discipline problem, and it looks like a coverage problem to whoever inherits the bundle without the log to explain it.

The classic metering trap compounds this. A camera's light meter is calibrated around the assumption that a scene averages to 18% gray reflectance, so a bright room or a dark one will fool the meter into the wrong exposure unless the operator corrects for it, the same reason a photographer might meter off a gray card rather than trust the scene's overall brightness. And once exposure is set, "expose to the right," pushing brightness as far as possible without clipping highlights, is worth understanding for the same reason clipped highlights are unrecoverable in post: a stitching or reconstruction pipeline can't invent detail that was blown out at capture, no matter how good the software downstream is.

None of these decisions are visible in the JPGs themselves. A file doesn't say "AE was locked at frame 40" or "I set white balance for the daylight window and accepted the tungsten lamp would read warm." That's what an exposure log is for: a short, honest record of what settings were locked, what compromise was made, and where. It's the cheapest insurance in the whole workflow, a few lines of text that save an engineer from reverse-engineering the operator's intent from pixel evidence alone.

## Naming: the part that looks trivial and isn't

Coverage and exposure are about physics and optics. Naming is about bookkeeping, and it's tempting to treat it as the least important item on the list. It isn't, because a bundle without a clear, ordered naming scheme forces the reconstruction side to reconstruct not just the scene but the operator's path through it, guessing at sequence and room boundaries from timestamps or file hashes. Sequential matching in COLMAP, one of the two practical matching strategies alongside exhaustive matching, depends on frame order meaning something: it matches each image against its near neighbors in the sequence, plus periodic loop-detection passes against visually similar earlier frames. If the files aren't named in a way that preserves capture order, sequential matching degrades into a coin flip. The companion volume Assembling and Packing the Bundle: manifest.json and ZIP_STORED Crops covers the concrete mechanics of building a manifest and packing a bundle correctly; naming discipline is what makes that manifest meaningful rather than decorative.

## What this means for the operator

The three obligations, coverage, an exposure log, and disciplined naming, aren't separate chores. They're the minimum honest description of what happened in the room, handed to someone who wasn't there. A bundle that clears this bar isn't guaranteed to reconstruct perfectly, texture-poor walls and reflective surfaces remain genuinely hard problems the tooling can't fully solve yet, but it guarantees the reconstruction side is working with the truth rather than reconstructing the operator's intentions from ambiguous evidence. The full downstream expectations this bar feeds into are laid out in The Bundle Contract: What Downstream Expects From You, and the signs that a session already violated this discipline while you're still standing in the room are the subject of Troubleshooting a Session That Feels Wrong Before You Leave. Meeting the bar before you leave the building is what makes handoff a handoff, rather than a problem you've quietly passed to someone else.
