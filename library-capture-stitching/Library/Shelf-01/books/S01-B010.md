# The Bundle Contract: What Downstream Expects From You

What does the reconstruction pipeline actually need from you, the operator, at the end of a shoot?

Not a good feeling about the walkthrough. Not a memory of which rooms felt well-covered. It needs a folder: a `manifest.json` file and a `crops/` directory full of JPGs, sitting next to each other, referencing each other correctly. That folder is the entire interface between the part of this process a human does with a phone and the part a machine does with COLMAP and a gsplat trainer. Everything upstream of it is craft. Everything downstream of it is code. The bundle is where the two meet, and it is worth treating as a contract rather than a byproduct.

## Why a contract, and not just "the output"

Call it a contract because it has to hold regardless of who or what produced it. The operator walking a building room by room with a phone, locking exposure and shooting overlapping stills, doesn't hand their work directly to COLMAP. They hand it to a bundle. The bundle is what COLMAP's feature matching and incremental structure-from-motion actually open. If the operator's discipline in the field, framing, overlap, steady standoff distance, doesn't survive translation into that folder correctly, none of it matters. The reconstruction only knows what the bundle tells it.

This is the same idea explored from the capture side in *From Building to Bundle: The Shape of the Orbit Capture Pipeline*, and from the packaging side in *Assembling and Packing the Bundle: manifest.json and ZIP_STORED Crops*. This book sits between those two: it's about why the bundle shape exists at all, and why getting it wrong is not a minor bug but a broken handoff.

## What's actually in the folder

Two things, and they need to agree with each other.

`manifest.json` is the index. It's the machine-readable record of what was captured: which files exist, in what order, and whatever metadata the pipeline needs to make sense of them, camera identifiers, sequence position, whatever fields the capture tool decided to track. `crops/*.jpg` is the payload: the actual still images, cropped and prepared, sitting where the manifest says they sit.

Neither one is useful alone. A manifest with no matching files is a list of promises nobody kept. A pile of JPGs with no manifest is a pile of JPGs, no different from a phone's camera roll, with no guarantee they were captured in an order or with settings a matching pipeline can rely on. The contract is the pairing: every entry in the manifest resolves to a real file in `crops/`, and every file in `crops/` is accounted for in the manifest. That mutual consistency is the whole point. It's not two artifacts, it's one artifact with two parts.

## Why JPGs, and why the format choice belongs to the contract

It's worth noticing that the bundle isn't raw sensor data and isn't a proprietary intermediate format. It's JPGs, an ordinary, portable, already-compressed image format. That choice matters downstream in a way that's easy to overlook: JPEG is already entropy-coded, so there's nothing left to gain by re-compressing it. The Python standard library's own `zipfile` documentation is explicit about this: `ZIP_DEFLATED` requires the zlib codec and spends real CPU time compressing, while `ZIP_STORED` is the module's default, uncompressed method, and the `compresslevel` parameter has no effect at all when `ZIP_STORED` is used. For a bundle of JPEG crops, running them through a compression pass is pure waste. That's exactly the reasoning behind packing the bundle with `ZIP_STORED`, a decision covered in depth in *Assembling and Packing the Bundle*. The point here is narrower: the contract's file format decisions aren't arbitrary, they're chosen because the payload is already in its final, densest form by the time it reaches the bundle.

## What downstream does with what it's handed

Once the bundle is complete and consistent, COLMAP takes it from there: feature detection and matching across the images, then incremental structure-from-motion to recover camera poses and a sparse point cloud. That sparse reconstruction is not a side artifact of the process, it's the required input for what comes after. The reference implementation behind 3D Gaussian Splatting is explicit about this: it expects a COLMAP-style directory of images plus `cameras.bin`, `images.bin`, and `points3D.bin`, and the original Gaussian Splatting paper describes its own method as initializing directly from the sparse point cloud that a structure-from-motion pipeline produces. Nothing about training a walkable Gaussian-splat scene starts from raw, uncalibrated photos. It starts from the output of a process that itself starts from the bundle.

That chain is why the bundle's correctness compounds. An error in the manifest, a missing crop, a mismatched index, doesn't just produce a slightly worse reconstruction. It can silently degrade or outright break camera pose estimation for that portion of the scene, and that failure then propagates into whatever gets trained on top of it. The bundle sits at the narrowest point in the pipeline. Everything before it is redundant and forgiving (miss a stretch of wall and you can usually recapture it), and everything after it treats the bundle as ground truth it has no way to double-check.

## What this means for the operator

The instinct, when you're the one walking the building with a phone, is to think of the job as "capturing the space." That's true, but it's not the deliverable. The deliverable is a bundle that correctly represents what you captured. A capture session can be geometrically excellent, tight overlap, steady standoff, consistent exposure, and still produce a broken bundle if the manifest and the crops fall out of sync, if files get dropped, reordered, or renamed somewhere in the handoff from phone to processing tool.

This is the discipline addressed directly in *Handoff Discipline: What the Operator Owes the Pipeline*: the operator's job doesn't end when the last room is shot. It ends when the bundle that represents that shoot is verified consistent, before it crosses into the part of the pipeline that will trust it without question. Treating the bundle as a contract, rather than an incidental output, is what makes that verification feel necessary rather than optional. Downstream isn't going to notice your careful lighting or your steady walking pace. It's only going to notice whether the manifest and the crops agree.
