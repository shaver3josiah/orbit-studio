# Standoff Distance and the Geometry of a Room

How far back should you stand from a wall before you press the shutter?

It sounds like a trivial question, the kind of thing you'd guess at and move on from. But standoff distance, the gap between the camera and whatever it's looking at, is one of the few capture decisions that touches every stage of the pipeline downstream: feature detection, matching, triangulation, and eventually the density and stability of the splat itself. Get it wrong and no amount of clever COLMAP flags or gsplat tuning will fully recover what the geometry never gave you a chance to see.

The rule this book defends is simple to state: never go closer than about 0.3 meters to a subject, and treat roughly 1 meter as your working default for most interior surfaces. The rest of the book is about why that range exists and what it's actually protecting you from.

## The problem is baseline, not proximity

It's tempting to think standoff distance is about avoiding a blurry or distorted close-up. That's part of it, but the deeper issue is what structure-from-motion needs to work at all. COLMAP, like every SfM pipeline, recovers 3D structure by triangulating the same point seen from at least two camera positions that are genuinely separated in space. That separation is called baseline, and it is not the same thing as taking two photos. If you stand in one spot and pivot the camera, you get two images but no baseline, and the two-view geometry becomes mathematically underdetermined: the fundamental matrix can't be pinned down from point correspondences alone, and depth simply cannot be resolved no matter how many features match. This is the same failure mode covered in more detail in "The Parallax Requirement: Why Pure Rotation Kills a Splat," and it's worth understanding here because standoff distance is the other half of that same coin. Some modern wide-baseline SfM pipelines even deliberately delay full initialization until enough parallax has accumulated between frames, because triangulating too early on a thin baseline produces depth estimates that are technically computed but not trustworthy.

Standing very close to a subject shrinks your effective baseline options. When you're 20 centimeters from a wall, even a modest step sideways swings your viewing angle on that wall dramatically, which sounds like it should help parallax, but in practice it also shrinks your field of view down to a small, texture-thin patch and increases the odds that consecutive frames no longer share enough of the same surface to match reliably. You end up trading one problem (no baseline) for another (no overlap). Distance to subject is what lets you hold both good overlap and a workable baseline at the same time, which is the entire point of the standoff rule.

## Why 0.3 meters is a floor, not a target

Camera optics themselves impose a hard limit before geometry even gets involved. Industrial photogrammetry rigs like the V-STARS system are specified to hold acceptable focus only within a bounded working-distance range, and on the near side that bound sits around 1 meter, according to Geodetic Systems' guidance on photogrammetry basics. Consumer phone cameras are more forgiving at short range than dedicated survey cameras, but the underlying physics doesn't disappear: get close enough and you leave the lens's comfortable focus range, softness creeps in, and every downstream feature detector inherits that softness as noisy, unstable keypoints. 0.3 meters is a practical floor for a phone held in a hand, walking a room. Below it you're gambling on focus far more than the pipeline can tolerate.

There's a second, more geometric reason to respect that floor. Getting too close to a flat surface, a wall, a cabinet face, a section of floor, makes the surface fill the frame edge to edge with almost no perspective change across it. A feature detector like SIFT depends on having enough distinct, repeatable texture to lock onto, and COLMAP's own documentation is explicit that SIFT degrades on weak-texture or repetitive surfaces. A blank wall photographed from 20 centimeters away is functionally the same problem as a blank wall photographed from any distance, except worse, because you've also eliminated any chance of catching adjacent objects, corners, or trim that would otherwise anchor the match.

## Why roughly 1 meter is the working default

If 0.3 meters is the floor you don't cross, 1 meter is closer to where you actually want to live for most of an interior walk. Geodetic Systems' own capture pattern for a single subject describes three deliberate distance tiers: a wide loop around 3 meters out for overall coverage, a mid loop around 1.5 meters, and a close pass around 50 centimeters reserved specifically for detail work. That's not an accident of convenience, it's a working method: distance is a parameter you deliberately vary depending on what you're trying to resolve, not a single number you lock in and forget.

For a room walk specifically, 1 meter tends to be the sweet spot because it balances two things that pull in opposite directions. Move farther back and you get more of the room in frame, which is good for coverage, but you also flatten the parallax between consecutive shots and lose fine detail on trim, fixtures, and edges. Move closer and detail improves but coverage per frame shrinks, overlap becomes harder to maintain, and you edge toward the optical and texture problems described above. A meter of standoff, adjusted a little wider in generous rooms and a little tighter in cramped ones, is what let Geodetic Systems' mid-range loop do the bulk of the reconstruction work while the close pass simply fills in detail afterward.

Interior spaces also have their own overlap demands that make distance discipline matter more, not less. Pix4D's best-practices guidance calls out interior reconstruction specifically as needing around 90 percent overlap between frames, well above the general baseline of 75 percent frontal and 60 percent side overlap recommended for most subjects. That kind of overlap is only achievable if your standoff distance stays consistent step to step. Geodetic Systems makes this connection directly: holding a stable standoff distance while walking a path is treated as necessary, alongside a stable heading, specifically to maintain the overlap percentage the reconstruction depends on. Standoff consistency and overlap consistency are, in their framing, the same discipline wearing two names. If you drift from 1.5 meters to 60 centimeters halfway across a room because a couch got in your way, your overlap percentage silently changes with it, even if you never noticed a difference through the viewfinder.

## What this looks like on an actual walk

In practice this means a room walk isn't a single lap at a single distance. You're constantly negotiating the room's own geometry, and this is exactly where standoff distance and room shape start to interact: a hallway forces you close to both walls at once, a stairwell adds a vertical dimension to the same problem, and neither behaves like an open living room. That interaction between standoff rules and awkward interior shapes is its own subject, covered in "Stairwells, Hallways, and Other Awkward Standoff Geometries," and the routing choices that determine when you approach a wall versus circle a room's center are covered in "Routing a Room-by-Room Walk Without Backtracking." This book's job is narrower: to establish that the distance itself, independent of the path you take to maintain it, is a variable with real consequences, not an afterthought you can fix by shooting more frames.

It's also worth being honest about where the guidance thins out. There isn't a single authoritative source specifically prescribing an indoor-room overlap or standoff number tighter than the general 75 to 90 percent range; the evidence for tightening up in texture-poor or repetitive indoor spaces is drawn by analogy from scenario-specific increases documented for other conditions, like forests, snow, and agricultural fields, rather than from a study of interior rooms as their own case. Treat the 0.3 meter floor and 1 meter default as a synthesis of documented optical limits and general overlap practice, not as a number lifted verbatim from a single indoor-specific standard.

## What it means for the reader

Standoff distance is not a stylistic preference, it's the variable that decides whether your baseline, your overlap, and your feature texture all stay usable at the same time. Stay above 0.3 meters and you keep the optics and the texture on your side. Aim for something near 1 meter as your default lap distance and you keep the overlap percentage a room-scale reconstruction actually needs, while still leaving room for closer detail passes where they matter. The number is small enough to remember and forgiving enough to eyeball without a laser rangefinder, which is exactly what a solo operator walking a building alone needs it to be.
