# Loop Closure in the Field: Returning to Where You Started

Why does the last thing you shoot in a room matter as much as the first?

Because every step you take between them costs you a little bit of truth. COLMAP builds its understanding of a scene incrementally: it registers one image, then the next, then the next, each new camera pose estimated relative to the ones before it. Every estimate carries a small error. Those errors do not cancel out. They accumulate, the same way a hand-drawn floor plan drifts crooked if you only ever measure each wall relative to the last one you drew. Robotics researchers call this drift, and the standard fix in visual SLAM is loop closure: recognize that you have arrived somewhere you have already been, and use that recognition to correct the whole chain of estimates that got you there. The core idea, as one survey of indoor SLAM systems puts it, is comparing current visual data against historical map data to recognize a revisited location, then using that match to correct the accumulated drift in the trajectory. Orbit Capture is not a SLAM robot, but the operator walking a building with a phone or an Insta360 is building the same kind of chain, one photo at a time, and the same fix applies.

Picture a rectangular room. You start at the door, shoot the near wall, work your way around to the far wall, continue around to the wall opposite where you started, and finish back near the door. If you stop there, satisfied, you have handed COLMAP an open chain: a sequence of camera poses that starts at point A and ends near point A but has no way of knowing that the two are the same place. COLMAP's incremental reconstruction has no ground truth to check itself against. It only has what the images tell it, image by image. If your walk drifted a few degrees off true as you rounded each corner, that drift is baked into every pose after it, and the far side of the room ends up slightly rotated or offset relative to where it actually sits. The reconstruction will often still look plausible. It just won't be correct.

Now picture the same walk, except at the end you deliberately step back to your starting vantage point and shoot it again, framing the same wall, the same doorway, the same fixed furniture you photographed at the very beginning. That final set of frames overlaps heavily with your very first set. When COLMAP's matching stage compares them, it finds strong correspondences between images that are far apart in your shooting sequence but close together in actual space. That mismatch, many shared features but a long chain of intermediate poses, is the signal COLMAP needs. It can now solve for the loop as a constraint: the accumulated error across the whole sequence gets distributed back through the chain instead of being left to pile up silently at the far end. This is the practical mechanism the sequential matcher's loop detection is built around: at a set interval it retrieves the most visually similar images from a pre-trained vocabulary tree and matches against them, specifically to catch exactly this kind of revisit (per the COLMAP tutorial and the deepwiki documentation of the sequential matching strategy). You don't need to invoke that vocabulary tree by hand to benefit from the underlying principle. Even without it, extra overlapping frames between the end and the start of a loop hand the matcher an obvious, strong connection it would otherwise never see, because in a normal walk-through sequence, frame 80 is never compared against frame 3 unless something in your shot list makes that comparison worth making.

This is why "return to your starting point" belongs on every operator's shot list as a discrete, deliberate step, not an afterthought of walking back to the door to leave the room. The revisit needs to look like a real observation of the same place from close to the same vantage point and orientation you used the first time, with clear overlap against your opening frames. A glancing shot of the corner as you exit doesn't do this job. Framing the same section of wall, the same fixture, the same visual landmarks you led with at the start does. COLMAP's own guidance backs this up in general terms even outside the loop-closure case: every object or surface needs to be seen in at least three images as a hard minimum for it to be reliably triangulated at all, and more overlap is generally better up to a point of diminishing returns. A deliberate loop-closing revisit is simply that same overlap discipline applied at the scale of the whole room instead of the scale of a single wall.

It is worth being honest about a limit here. The loop-closure literature this reasoning draws from comes from robotics and SLAM research, not from photogrammetry capture guides written for a solo phone operator, so the framing above is an adaptation of that research to this workflow rather than a direct quote from a source built for it. The mechanism, revisit a place, get a strong cross-link, correct the chain, is well established. The exact best practice for how far back to step, how many frames to shoot, and how tight the revisit needs to be in a household or office setting is something this book infers from COLMAP's general overlap guidance rather than a number you can look up.

There is a companion danger worth flagging without solving here: buildings with repeating layouts, identical hallways, matching rooms on different floors, can trick a matcher into thinking it has found a loop when it has actually matched two different but similar-looking places. That risk, and how to avoid it, is the subject of its own volume, Multi-Room Buildings with Repeating Geometry: Avoiding False Loop Closures. What this book is about is the simpler, more common case: one room, one loop, one deliberate return.

Loop closure also does not stand alone. It only works if the frames leading up to it were captured with the continuity a matcher can chain together in the first place, which is the discipline covered in Doorways, Thresholds, and Keeping Continuity Between Rooms, and it only works if the operator has actually handed the pipeline a complete, well-organized bundle to run on, which is the handoff responsibility covered in Handoff Discipline: What the Operator Owes the Pipeline. Loop closure is the last stitch in that fabric, not a substitute for the rest of it.

For the operator in the field, the practical takeaway is small and easy to carry into every room: don't just start shooting and stop when you run out of walls. Plan the return. Know before you start where your closing shot will stand and what it will frame, and make sure it echoes your opening shot closely enough that COLMAP cannot miss the connection. That one deliberate step, walking back to where you began and shooting it again on purpose, is what turns a long chain of accumulating small errors into a closed loop that corrects itself.
