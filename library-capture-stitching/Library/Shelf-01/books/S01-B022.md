# Recognizing Motion Blur Before You Leave the Room

How do you know, standing in a room with a phone in your hand, whether the shot you just took is good enough to build with, or whether you are about to walk out the door and doom yourself to a reshoot three days later? That is the whole question this book answers. Not how to prevent blur, not how to score it after the fact on a laptop, but how to look at the small rectangle of glass in your hand, right now, and make the call.

This matters because of where you are in the workflow. Orbit Capture is a solo operation. You walk a room, you shoot overlapping stills, and then you move to the next room, and the next, until the building is done. There is no second operator checking your frames, no tethered monitor, no assistant flagging problems. The preview on your phone screen is the only quality gate you get, and it is a gate you pass through once, while you are still standing in the room with the light the way it was and the furniture where it was. Once you leave, that exact configuration is gone. A reshoot is not "come back and take a photo." It is come back, hope the light matches, hope nothing moved, and burn another visit.

So the practical skill is this: learn to distinguish blur that matters from blur that does not, using nothing but your eyes and a phone screen, before you commit to the next room.

### What blur actually costs you

It helps to know why blur is fatal to this pipeline rather than just unattractive. Orbit Capture feeds its JPGs into COLMAP for structure-from-motion, and COLMAP's own tutorial documentation is explicit about what it needs from source images: high visual overlap, each surface visible in at least three images, and stable illumination across the set. Blur does not just make one photo ugly. It quietly breaks the feature-matching step that the entire reconstruction depends on. A blurred frame has fewer distinct edges for the matcher to lock onto, and the edges it does find are less precisely located, which degrades the camera pose estimate built from that frame. If enough frames in a room are soft, the sparse reconstruction for that room either comes out warped or fails to register at all. And Gaussian splatting inherits every one of these sins, because the official 3D Gaussian Splatting method (Kerbl et al., 2023) does not train from raw photos at all. It trains from the sparse point cloud that COLMAP's structure-from-motion produces. Garbage poses in, garbage splat out. There is no downstream stage that fixes a blurry input photo. The correction has to happen before you leave the room, because it cannot happen after.

### Reading the preview: what you're actually looking for

A phone screen is small, often glare-washed, and easy to lie to yourself about. The trick is to stop looking at the whole frame and start looking at specific places in it.

First, find an edge with genuine contrast: a door frame against a wall, a light switch plate, the corner where two walls meet, text on a sign or a spine of a book. Blur shows up as edges losing their crispness, turning into a soft gradient instead of a clean transition. This is the same principle that underlies the standard computational blur-detection technique, the variance of the Laplacian, described in the widely used PyImageSearch method: a sharp image has abundant edges and rapid intensity changes, a blurry one does not, because the Laplacian operator responds to those rapid changes and a soft image simply produces less of a response. You are doing by eye what that operator does by math. Find the highest-contrast edge in the frame and ask whether it looks drawn with a sharp pencil or smudged with a thumb.

Second, zoom in with your fingers on the phone's own preview, not just squint at the thumbnail. Motion blur at thumbnail size is often invisible and only reveals itself at something close to full resolution, because the softening it produces is a matter of a few pixels smearing across each other, which a small on-screen image simply cannot resolve for you. If your capture app lets you tap to zoom the last frame taken, use that every time you have any doubt.

Third, know what kind of blur you are looking at, because the two most common kinds have different causes and different fixes on the spot. Camera shake is blur from your own hand or body moving during the exposure, and it is the failure mode the photography world's reciprocal rule exists to prevent: keep your shutter speed at least as fast as 1 over your focal length, so a phone shooting at an equivalent wide angle needs a comparatively fast shutter to avoid shake, and image stabilization, where present, buys you several stops of slack rather than a full exemption. Subject motion blur is different: something in the scene moved, a ceiling fan, a curtain in a draft, a pet, and no amount of hand-steadiness fixes it, because the sensor is faithfully recording something that changed position during the exposure window regardless of how still you held the phone. If you see a soft trailing edge, ask yourself which one it is. Camera shake means slow down your walk, brace against something solid, or pause half a second longer before tapping the shutter. Subject motion means wait for the fan to stop, or reframe to exclude it, because bracing yourself will not help.

Fourth, remember that a locked exposure changes what blur looks like. Orbit Capture deliberately locks auto-exposure and white balance across a room's capture set, per Android's own CaptureRequest documentation on CONTROL_AE_LOCK and CONTROL_AWB_LOCK, specifically so that every frame in the set shares consistent brightness and color, which COLMAP's tutorial guidance also calls for. But a locked exposure means the shutter speed is fixed too, set once for the room's light level and then held. If you locked exposure in a bright spot and are now walking toward a darker corner of the same room, the same shutter speed that was safely fast a moment ago may now be borderline, because the underlying exposure algorithm is no longer adjusting per frame to compensate. This is worth a specific, deliberate check when you cross into a noticeably dimmer part of a room you have already locked exposure for.

### The decision, and when it stops being yours to make alone

Spot-checking on a screen is fast triage, not a lab measurement. It answers the immediate question: does this one frame look sharp enough to move on. It does not replace the more rigorous, numeric version of the same judgment, which the companion volume Estimating Capture Blur Risk with a Laplacian-Variance Score works through in detail, running the same Laplacian principle as software against a whole batch rather than a single glance. And a soft frame here or there is only one entry in a much larger list of things that can go wrong on a capture, cataloged fully in What Can Go Wrong: A Field Taxonomy of Bad Captures. If you are unsure whether what you are seeing crosses the line from "acceptable, reshoot not warranted" into "this room needs to be shot again," the field decision framework in Deciding On-Site Whether a Room Needs a Reshoot is the next book to reach for.

But the eye-on-screen check taught here is the first line of defense, and it is the cheapest one you will ever get. It costs you three seconds and a thumb-zoom, performed while you are still standing in the room, with the light and the furniture and the fan all exactly as they were when you shot the frame. Everything after this point costs more: a return trip, a mismatched light, a room that has been rearranged. Train the habit of checking one sharp edge, at zoom, before every step out the door, and most of the reshoots this workflow would otherwise generate simply never happen.
