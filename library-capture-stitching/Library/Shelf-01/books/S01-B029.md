# Mixed Lighting Zones: Transition Strategy Between Rooms

What do you actually do, as the operator, in the three seconds when you step out of a sunlit living room and into a windowless hallway closet?

That moment is where a lot of otherwise careful scans fall apart. Not at the doorway itself, which "Doorways, Thresholds, and Keeping Continuity Between Rooms" already covers as a geometric problem: enough overlapping frames on both sides of the frame so COLMAP has something to hook the two rooms together on. This book is about a different failure that happens at the same moment, one that's photometric rather than geometric. The camera's automatic systems, which have been quietly doing their job for the whole daylight room, hit a lighting cliff and try to "fix" it. If you let them, you get a scan that's geometrically fine and visually broken: a hallway that looks like it was shot on a different camera, in a different building.

## Why the transition is the dangerous moment, not the rooms themselves

Within one room, exposure drift is usually gradual. Auto-exposure and auto-white-balance track slow changes in what the lens sees and mostly stay coherent frame to frame. The problem in mixed lighting zones isn't gradual, it's a step function. A room lit by a window and a hallway lit by nothing but a ceiling fixture, or nothing at all, sit on opposite ends of both the exposure scale and the color temperature scale at the same time. Daylight runs cool and bright. Indoor tungsten sits around 3000K, and a genuinely windowless space might have no consistent light source at all beyond whatever fixture is on. Fstoppers' guidance on white balance consistency across a capture set is explicit about this: auto white balance "is documented to visibly drift frame-to-frame under mixed or colored indoor lighting, since it re-estimates a scene's color temperature independently on every shot." That's exactly the mechanism that turns a doorway crossing into a color mismatch, because the moment you cross the threshold, the camera has a brand new scene to re-estimate against, and it will take it.

If your workflow already carries one fixed white balance across the whole building, as covered in "Carrying One White Balance Across Differently-Lit Rooms," you've already closed half of this gap before you ever reach the transition. The other half, exposure, is the part specific to this moment: the sudden jump from bright to dark (or dark to bright) as you cross from a daylight room into a windowless one.

## What actually goes wrong if you do nothing

Leave auto-exposure and auto-white-balance running freely and here's the sequence: you're in the living room, AE has settled on some shutter/ISO combination that suits the window light, AWB has converged on something reasonably neutral for daylight. You step into the closet. The scene brightness the sensor sees drops sharply. AE reacts, usually by raising ISO or slowing shutter, sometimes both. AWB reacts too, re-estimating for whatever the ambient light in the windowless space actually is: tungsten if there's a bulb, something worse if there isn't.

Two consequences follow, and Gaussian splat training does not forgive either of them. First, exposure inconsistency across the doorway means the last few frames in the living room and the first few frames in the closet, frames that COLMAP needs to match on shared features to connect the two rooms, may not look like they belong to the same photographic session. Second, and more insidiously, even if COLMAP still manages the geometric match, the color and brightness discontinuity gets baked into the Gaussian splat itself. There's no note in these research notes claiming a specific numeric penalty for this in 3D Gaussian Splatting, and that's a fair gap to name rather than paper over, but the training reference for 3DGS ("3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering") is explicit that the whole method starts from a COLMAP-style sparse reconstruction and its calibrated camera poses. A shaky handoff at the reconstruction stage is a shaky foundation for everything trained on top of it.

## The strategy: lock before you cross, not after

The fix is a discipline about sequencing, not a piece of gear. You do not want to be adjusting exposure or white balance while standing in the doorway. You want the camera already locked to a decision by the time you get there.

On Android's Camera2 API, this is literally two flags: `CONTROL_AE_LOCK` and `CONTROL_AWB_LOCK`. Per the Android reference, `CONTROL_AE_LOCK` determines "whether auto-exposure is currently locked to its latest calculated values," and `CONTROL_AWB_LOCK` works the same way for white balance, freezing it so it "will not change color balance settings until" unlocked. Camera2's own guidance is worth taking seriously here: because AWB and AF behavior when AE is off is device-dependent, the documented practice is to explicitly lock (or turn off) AWB and AF as well, specifically to get consistent behavior across different phones. If you're on CameraX rather than raw Camera2, the equivalent knob is `Camera.CameraControl.setExposureCompensationIndex()` for exposure, though CameraX's own configuration docs are candid that it trades granular control for ease of use, and anything requiring a genuinely custom capture template still routes back down to Camera2.

The practical sequence for a mixed-lighting transition looks like this. Before you approach the doorway, decide which room's exposure and white balance you're going to carry through the crossing. This is a real decision, not a default: you're choosing which side of the threshold gets photometric priority. Meter for that room using a mid-tone reference if you have one (the 18% gray card convention that TTL metering itself is calibrated around, tracing back to Ansel Adams' Zone V), lock AE and AWB, then walk the transition and into the far side of the new room before you consider unlocking anything. Only once you're a few frames deep into the windowless room, with a clean run of exposure-locked overlap frames bridging the doorway already banked, do you re-meter and re-lock for the new room's actual lighting.

This is the same discipline "EV Compensation for Windows, Skylights, and Dark Corners" applies to bright and dark pockets within a single room, just executed at building scale. There the problem is a blown-out window or a black corner while everything else in frame is fine. Here the problem is the entire scene changing under you. But the underlying tool, exposure compensation dialed deliberately rather than left to drift, and the underlying test, watch the histogram for clipping and use the "expose to the right" logic Cambridge in Colour describes for pushing exposure without pinning highlights to white, are the same in both cases.

One honest limitation: none of the sources behind this book address Gaussian-splat training's tolerance for a hard photometric seam quantitatively. The advice above is a capture-side discipline built on solid ground (Camera2's own lock semantics, established white-balance-consistency practice, and the fact that 3DGS trains from COLMAP's calibrated output), not a guarantee backed by a benchmark number.

## What this means for the operator

Treat every daylight-to-windowless crossing as a decision point, not a place to let the camera improvise. Lock exposure and white balance to a deliberate choice before you reach the threshold, carry that lock through the doorway and a few frames past it, and only then re-meter for the new room. Do that consistently and the seam between rooms stops being a place where your scan visibly changes cameras, and starts being just another wall.
