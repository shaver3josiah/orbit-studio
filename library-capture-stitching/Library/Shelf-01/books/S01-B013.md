# The 70-80% Overlap Target in Practice: Counting Steps, Not Percentages

How do you hit a 70-80% overlap target when you have no way to measure overlap while you're standing in the room?

That's the practical problem this book solves. Every guide an operator reads, including the sibling volume Overlap as the Currency of Structure-from-Motion, states the target as a percentage. Pix4D's best-practices guidance calls for at least 75% frontal overlap and at least 60% side overlap as a general baseline, with interior reconstruction singled out as needing something closer to 90% given how repetitive and texture-poor indoor surfaces tend to be. The older CIPA 3x3 Rules for architectural photogrammetry put a floor of at least 60% between two spatially successive photographs. These are real numbers, backed by real institutions. They are also useless in the moment, because nobody standing in a hallway with a phone can look at two frames and read off "73%." Overlap is a property you can compute after the fact by comparing images, which is exactly the subject of the sibling volume A Field Overlap Estimator: Comparing Consecutive Frames. On site, in real time, you need something you can count instead of measure.

The fix is to stop thinking in percentages and start thinking in steps. A percentage is a ratio between two images; a step is a unit of motion your body already understands. If you can convert "80% overlap" into "one small step between shots," you've turned an abstract target into a cadence you can hold in your head while you're also watching your footing, your lighting, and the clock.

Here is the reasoning that gets you there. Overlap collapses toward zero exactly when you cover too much new ground between frames relative to how much of the frame's original content survives. The two things that eat overlap fastest are walking too far before you shoot again, and rotating instead of translating. COLMAP's own tutorial is blunt about the second failure mode: it explicitly warns against capturing images from the same location by rotating in place, and instead calls for shooting from genuinely different viewpoints. That's a translation requirement, not a rotation requirement, and it matters for cadence because it means your "step" has to be a step, an actual change in camera position, not a pivot of your body. A rotate-in-place capture can look like good coverage in a photo review and still starve the reconstruction of the parallax it needs.

That parallax point isn't cosmetic. Structure-from-motion depends on triangulating 3D points from two or more viewpoints, and that triangulation requires real translational baseline between the camera positions, not just different pixels on screen. Pure rotation is a documented degenerate case: when two views differ only by rotation, the geometry that would normally pin down a 3D point becomes mathematically underdetermined, and depth can't be reliably resolved no matter how many features you matched. So "take a small step" isn't a rule of thumb bolted onto a percentage target. It's the physical action that produces the parallax the percentage target is a proxy for. A high-overlap image pair with no translation is not a good pair.

With that established, here's the cadence rule in practice: shoot, take one modest step (a half-stride, not a full stride, along the wall or through the room), shoot again. That single-step interval is a reasonable default for interior spaces at typical room distances, because it keeps successive frames close enough together that most of the frame survives from one shot to the next while still giving the camera genuine lateral movement to triangulate against. It errs toward the tighter end of the target range on purpose. Indoor scenes are exactly the case Pix4D's guidance flags for going above the general baseline, and they're also the case where a texture-poor wall or a repeated hallway pattern can silently degrade a feature matcher's ability to find correspondences at all, a failure mode covered in more depth by other volumes on this shelf. When you don't know whether the wall ahead is going to be plaster-blank or richly textured, the safe default is to shoot a little more often than the loose end of the range, not a little less.

There's a second number worth internalizing alongside the step cadence, and it comes straight from COLMAP's own documentation rather than from inference: every object or surface needs to appear in at least three images, and more is better. This is a hard floor, not a suggestion. Fewer than three views of a given surface region and COLMAP may not have enough constraints to register it reliably at all. A one-step cadence naturally clears this floor for most surfaces in a room, because a wall you pass while stepping steadily along it will appear in several consecutive frames as you go. But it's worth holding as an explicit check for the awkward geometry every real room has: a corner, a doorway, a low ceiling nook. If you can only picture one or two shots landing on a given patch of wall as you walk your planned path, that patch needs an extra shot, not a shortcut.

It's also worth being honest about what the cadence rule doesn't give you. There is no single authoritative source, in the material behind this book, that specifies an indoor-specific overlap percentage the way Pix4D specifies numbers for forests or snow or agricultural fields. The 90% interior figure and the one-step cadence derived from it are a reasoned extension of scenario-based guidance that exists for other environments, not a quoted indoor standard. Treat the step cadence as a strong, physically grounded default, and treat any specific percentage claim about indoor capture with the understanding that it's an analogy, not a citation.

Distance from the subject changes what "one step" means, and this is where the cadence has to flex rather than stay rigid. Standoff distance and overlap consistency are, in effect, the same discipline: holding a stable distance from the walls as you walk is what keeps a fixed step interval producing a fixed overlap fraction. Get closer to a wall and a half-stride covers proportionally more of the frame, so the same step count now overshoots your overlap target; back away and the opposite happens. The practical adjustment is to shorten your step near a wall and lengthen it in an open room, always steering by the same felt sense of "did most of what I just saw stay in frame," rather than by a fixed physical stride length that only works at one distance.

None of this is a plan you write down and follow room by room. It's a rhythm: step, shoot, step, shoot, tightened near featureless walls, held to at least three views per surface, translating rather than rotating, and re-tuned by feel whenever you move closer to or farther from what you're capturing. The full version of that rhythm, including how it interacts with how much time you actually have in a given space, is the subject of Working the 1-3 Minute Room: Pacing a Capture Session. What this book gives you is the piece that has to be automatic before pacing matters at all: a step cadence you can hold in your head that reliably produces the overlap a percentage target only describes after the fact.

For the operator on site, this is the whole payoff. You can't measure overlap while you're capturing it. You can count steps. Get the step cadence right and the percentage takes care of itself.
