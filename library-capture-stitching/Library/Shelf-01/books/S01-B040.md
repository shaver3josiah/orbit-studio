# The Cost of a Missed Room: Why Reshoots Are Cheaper On-Site

What does it actually cost to fix a bad capture after you have already left the building?

The honest answer is: far more than the thirty extra seconds it would have taken to fix it on the spot. That gap, between the cost of catching a problem while you are still standing in the room and the cost of catching it after you have driven home, unloaded the gear, and started processing, is the entire argument of this book. A reshoot taken on-site is a 1-3 minute interruption to your walk-through. A reshoot discovered later is a second trip, a second access request, a second block of time carved out of someone else's calendar, and in the worst case a room that has already changed, been rearranged, or become unavailable. The math is not close.

## Why the gap is invisible in the moment

The reason operators skip the on-site check is not laziness. It is that nothing about a bad capture looks bad while you are taking it. A phone screen at arm's length does not show you that your last three frames were motion-blurred, or that the auto white balance quietly drifted warmer walking from the hallway into a room lit by a different lamp, or that you rotated in place at one spot instead of genuinely moving through the space. COLMAP's own tutorial is blunt about that last failure mode: capture from genuinely different viewpoints, and do not take images from the same location. A phone held up and swiveled at one spot violates that instruction in a way that is completely invisible on a 6-inch screen but fully visible three days later when the reconstruction cannot resolve depth in that room.

That failure has a specific technical cause worth naming, because it explains why the damage is silent until it isn't. Structure-from-motion depends on translational baseline, actual movement of the camera center, between views. Pure rotation without translation is a documented degenerate case for two-view geometry: the geometry that relates two views is not uniquely determined when the views differ only by rotation, and reliable triangulation of 3D points requires sufficient parallax regardless of how many features you matched. You can have a perfectly sharp, perfectly exposed set of images that still fails to reconstruct, because nobody actually walked.

Motion blur is the same story from a different angle. A blurry frame does not announce itself on a phone screen the way it would on a full-resolution monitor, and by the time you are looking at the actual pixels during processing, the light, the furniture, or the room itself may no longer be arranged the way it was. The well-known reciprocal rule for handheld shooting (minimum shutter speed at least 1 over the focal length, adjusted for crop factor on non-full-frame sensors) exists precisely because camera shake is invisible until you check for it directly, and stabilization only buys back some of that margin, not all of it.

## What a missed room actually costs

Break down what "catching it later" really means. First there is the diagnosis cost: you do not just discover a room reconstructed badly, you have to figure out why, usually by rereading which images belong to that room and staring at overlap and blur, work that has to happen after the fact instead of in ten seconds against the wall in front of you. Then there is the logistics cost: getting back into a commercial space, a client's home, a construction site, or a venue is rarely instant. It means rescheduling, re-requesting access, sometimes waiting days. Then there is the highest cost of all, the one that cannot be recovered by any amount of scheduling flexibility: the room itself might not be the same room anymore. Furniture gets moved, staging gets struck, construction proceeds, lighting fixtures get swapped, a rented venue empties out. A missed capture window is sometimes not a delay, it is a total loss of that exact state of the room.

Compare that against what an on-site reshoot actually costs. You are already there. The gear is already out. The lighting has not changed. Redoing a 1-3 minute room, the pacing this library covers in Working the 1-3 Minute Room: Pacing a Capture Session, costs you 1-3 minutes plus whatever tiny interruption it makes to your rhythm walking the building. That is the entire bill. There is no rescheduling, no second commute, no risk that the room will look different when you come back, because you never left.

## Why this argument survives every excuse

The natural objection is that you cannot always tell, in the moment, whether a room needs a reshoot. That is true, and it is exactly the problem the sibling volume Deciding On-Site Whether a Room Needs a Reshoot exists to solve: giving you fast, checkable signals (blur, overlap, lighting consistency, genuine movement through the space) you can apply before you walk out the door, rather than trusting your gut or your phone's tiny preview. Blur is not something you have to guess at either. The classic variance-of-Laplacian check, a single line of OpenCV code that scores an image's sharpness by looking at the variance of its edge response, turns "does this look sharp" from a subjective judgment call into a number you can threshold against. It is not a universal cutoff (the original authors are explicit that the right threshold depends on the content of the scene), but a rough, fast, on-site check is worth infinitely more than a perfect check performed three days too late.

The other natural objection is that stopping to double-check every room slows you down, and that speed is the whole point of a solo capture workflow. But this gets the tradeoff backwards. The interruption cost of a spot-check, or of a 1-3 minute reshoot when the check fails, is bounded and small. The interruption cost of discovering the gap in post-processing is unbounded: it can be an afternoon, a week, or in the worst case, permanent, if the room has already changed. A capture practice that trades a small guaranteed cost now for protection against a large uncertain cost later is not being slow. It is being cheap in the way that actually matters.

There is a related failure mode worth a plain caveat here: rooms with repeated layouts (a hallway of identical hotel rooms, a row of matching offices) create a genuine risk of the reconstruction pipeline confusing one room for another during loop closure, a problem documented in visual SLAM research on high-similarity indoor scenes. The research on solo-operator phone capture specifically is thin on this exact failure mode, so treat that adaptation as reasoning by analogy from the robotics literature rather than a directly measured number. If your building has repeating rooms, that is itself a reason to weight the on-site check more heavily, not less, since the failure is harder to catch later and easier to catch by simply noticing you are standing in a room that looks like the last one. If something already feels off while you are still inside, the checklist in Troubleshooting a Session That Feels Wrong Before You Leave is built for exactly that moment.

## What this means for you

Treat every room as closed only when you have actually confirmed it, not when you have merely walked through it. The confirmation costs you almost nothing while you are there. It costs you everything, sometimes literally the room itself, once you are gone. Build the habit of the last-second check before every doorway, because the fee for skipping it is never charged on the spot. It is charged later, with interest, at a time and place you do not get to choose.
