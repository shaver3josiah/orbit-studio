# Working the 1-3 Minute Room: Pacing a Capture Session

What do you actually do with your body and your phone during the ninety seconds you spend in a bedroom you'll never walk into again?

That's the real question behind the 1-3 minute budget. It isn't a countdown timer stapled to the job for its own sake. It's what's left over once you subtract the two things a solo capture operator cannot skip: getting enough overlapping coverage that COLMAP can register the room, and getting enough contiguous images that the exposure and color stay stable across camera pipeline updates the phone applies mid-scene. Everything else in the room, the crown molding, the wall art, the paint, has to fit inside that arithmetic. The room doesn't care how photogenic it is. It gets the time the coverage math says it gets.

## The rhythm, not the clock

Treat the room as four beats: read, shoot, check, move on. Not four equal slices of a stopwatch, four decisions made in sequence, fast.

**Read.** Before you raise the phone, look at the room the way COLMAP will have to. Where's the texture and where's it missing? A wall of trim and furniture gives the feature matcher plenty to lock onto. A blank painted wall or a hallway ceiling doesn't. COLMAP's own tutorial documentation is explicit that you should avoid completely texture-less surfaces and shoot from genuinely different viewpoints rather than rotating in place from one spot ("Capture images with high visual overlap," "Do not take images from the same location," per the COLMAP 3.6 Tutorial). That's the read: which walls need extra passes, which corners are dead space you can walk past quickly. You're deciding your path through the room before you've taken a single frame.

**Shoot.** This is where the budget actually gets spent, and it's spent on overlap, not on artistry. Pix4D's acquisition guidance sets a general baseline of at least 75% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap, and calls out interiors specifically as needing high overlap, around 90%, with terrestrial (walking, ground-based) imagery strongly preferred for interior work over any other capture method. That's a stricter number than the outdoor baseline, and it's the number that should be running in your head as you move through a room, not a percentage but a felt sense of how far you can step before the last frame stops being useful. The companion volume in this shelf, *The 70-80% Overlap Target in Practice: Counting Steps, Not Percentages*, turns that percentage into something you can actually execute while walking, which is the tool you want in hand during this beat.

The other thing happening during the shoot beat is that your exposure and white balance need to hold still. If you locked auto-exposure and auto-white-balance before entering the room, as the Android CaptureRequest reference documents, AE lock freezes exposure to its latest calculated values and AWB lock freezes color the same way, until you explicitly unlock them. That stability is what lets a run of frames belong to the same room instead of drifting in brightness or color cast from shot to shot. The pacing exists partly to protect that lock: the moment you start second-guessing exposure mid-room and start toggling settings, you've broken the one thing the lock was there to preserve.

**Check.** Before you leave, look at what you just captured. Not admire it, audit it. Two failure modes matter here, and both are cheap to catch in the room and expensive to catch back at the workstation. First, blur: a fast, computable check is the variance of the Laplacian on a grayscale frame, a single line of code, and a sharp frame reads as high variance while a blurry one reads low (PyImageSearch's blur-detection method, using `cv2.Laplacian(image, cv2.CV_64F).var()`). The author of that method is upfront that the exact threshold for "too blurry" is subjective to the content of the image and has to be tuned, not treated as universal, but the underlying signal, a live low-variance reading, is exactly the kind of thing a capture tool can flag for you in real time instead of you noticing it two weeks later during reconstruction. Second, coverage gaps: did you actually get every surface into at least three images? COLMAP's FAQ states this as a hard floor for registration, "make sure that each object is seen in at least 3 images," and that number climbs as texture gets weaker. The sibling volume *Live Coverage Tracking: Am I Done With This Room?* is the tool built specifically for answering this in the moment rather than guessing.

**Move on.** If the check comes back clean, you leave. The temptation to circle back for one more angle "just in case" is the single biggest threat to the budget, because it's a decision made out of anxiety rather than data. If your check step is honest, and it's telling you the room is covered and sharp, additional images past that point aren't neutral. COLMAP's tutorial notes that excess images beyond what's needed can slow processing without improving reconstruction quality, so the return on one more redundant shot isn't zero, it's mildly negative once you're past the threshold. Trust the check, and go.

## Why the walking matters as much as the shooting

There's a physical constraint sitting underneath all four beats: you have to keep moving with a stable standoff distance while you shoot, because that's what maintains the overlap the whole pipeline depends on. Photogrammetry guidance on working distance treats a consistent, deliberate standoff and a stable overlap percentage as the same underlying discipline, not two separate skills. In practice this means the pacing of a room isn't really about how many seconds you spend in it. It's about whether your walking speed and your shutter cadence are matched to each other. Walk too fast for your shot interval and you blow past the overlap floor; you'll find out at reconstruction time, when the SfM solver can't bridge two frames that don't share enough common geometry, or worse, when a stretch of nearly-pure sideways motion with too little forward translation leaves the solver with no real parallax to triangulate from, a known degenerate case in structure-from-motion regardless of how many features matched. Walk too slow and you're burning your minute-budget on redundant frames the check step will just tell you to discard anyway.

This is also why the 1-3 minute room only works for a solo operator who owns the whole loop, reading the room, shooting it, checking it, deciding to leave, without handing any of those decisions to someone else or to a second camera running independently. *One Person, One Building: The Solo Operator Constraint* is the volume that makes the case for why that single-loop ownership is a feature of the method, not a limitation you're working around. The pacing described here is what that constraint looks like in practice, room by room.

One honest limitation: none of the sources behind this shelf specify a literal "1 to 3 minutes per room" figure as a named guideline. That number is this library's own operational synthesis of the overlap requirements, coverage minimums, and stability constraints documented above, not a claim any cited source makes directly. Treat it as the practical output of the math in this book, not a citation of its own.

## What this means for you

The room doesn't get a fixed slice of time. It gets exactly as long as it takes to read its texture, walk it at a pace that holds 75-90% overlap depending on how forgiving its surfaces are, confirm every part of it landed in at least three sharp frames, and leave the instant that check comes back clean. Do that honestly, room after room, and the minute-or-three budget isn't a constraint you're fighting. It's just what the arithmetic of coverage looks like when you stop second-guessing it.
