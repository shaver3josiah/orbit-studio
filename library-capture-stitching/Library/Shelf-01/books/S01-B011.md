# Routing a Room-by-Room Walk Without Backtracking

If you're capturing a building alone, with one camera and one pass, what path through the rooms keeps every frame connected to the next without forcing you to retrace your steps?

This is a routing problem before it's a photography problem. A building is a graph: rooms are nodes, doorways are edges, and your walk is a single traversal of that graph. The constraint that makes it hard is the one named in "One Person, One Building: The Solo Operator Constraint" — there's no second operator to close a loop from the other end, and no do-over pass once you've packed up. Whatever order you walk the rooms in has to produce a chain of images where each one overlaps its neighbors, from the first room to the last, in one continuous take.

## Why backtracking breaks the chain

COLMAP's own tutorial is explicit about what a good capture set looks like: high visual overlap between images, every surface seen from at least three viewpoints, and — this is the part that matters for routing — genuinely different vantage points rather than repeated passes from the same spot ("Do not take images from the same location"). A room-by-room walk that doubles back through a room you already shot isn't neutral. It's a second, near-identical pass over territory you've already covered, and it changes what the matcher sees.

Sequential matching, which is the natural mode for a continuous walk-and-shoot capture, works by comparing each image against a window of nearby frames in the capture order — by default the ten images before and after it, per the documented `overlap` parameter in COLMAP's sequential matcher. That's a good model for a walk that moves steadily forward through the building. It's a bad model for a walk that loops back on itself, because now the "nearby in sequence" images and the "nearby in physical space" images stop being the same set. You end up relying on loop detection — periodic matches against a vocabulary tree of visually similar frames — to stitch the revisit back to the earlier pass. Loop detection is a real mechanism and it works, but it is not something a routing plan should need in the first place, and indoor buildings are precisely where it is weakest. A hallway that looks like the hallway two doors down, or a bedroom with the same layout as the one across the hall, is exactly the kind of repeated visual pattern that makes loop closure unreliable: as the SLAM literature on high-scene-similarity indoor environments points out, visual similarity between genuinely different locations raises the risk of matching the wrong instance rather than the right one. A capture plan that depends on the algorithm correctly untangling two similar bedrooms after a backtrack is a plan with a known failure mode built in.

There's a second reason to avoid it that has nothing to do with matching and everything to do with time and effort. COLMAP's tutorial also notes that excess images past the point of adequate coverage have diminishing, even negative, returns — more overlap doesn't keep buying you accuracy, and past some point it just adds processing time. A backtrack is exactly this: extra frames of a room you already fully covered, adding runtime to the reconstruction without adding new geometry.

## Treating the building as a graph to walk once

The practical fix is to plan the route before you start shooting, the same way you'd plan a hallway before wiring it. Model the building as rooms connected by doorways, and look for a path that visits every room exactly once, entering and leaving each room through a different door where the floor plan allows it. This is the same shape of problem as a Hamiltonian path — find one ordering of the rooms that touches each exactly once — except you get to use your own judgment about the graph instead of running an algorithm on it, because most buildings have few enough rooms that the good routes are visible on a floor plan.

A few shapes of building make this straightforward. A shotgun house or a simple hallway-and-rooms layout is already a path graph: walk it start to end, no choices to make. An open loop — a ring of connected rooms around a central core, like a wing that returns to its own entrance — is walkable end to end without repeating a room, provided you don't feel obligated to close the loop back to your starting point; you simply stop at the last room and treat the fact that it's physically adjacent to your first room as irrelevant to the shoot. It's the branching layouts that force a decision: a central hallway with rooms peeling off on both sides is a tree, not a path, and a tree has no route that visits every node exactly once without retracing an edge, because you have to walk back down the hallway to reach the rooms on the other side.

For a branching layout, the honest move is to accept that the hallway gets walked more than once, but treat the hallway itself, not the rooms, as the place absorbing the repeat. Walk into each side room, shoot it, walk back out through the same doorway, continue down the hall, and treat every hallway pass as additional overlap on a corridor rather than a redundant pass on a room. Corridor mapping is exactly the scenario Pix4D calls out as needing high frontal overlap in its best-practices guidance — at least 85% frontal overlap for corridor-style capture, well above the general baseline — which lines up with treating the hallway as the one place in the building where you expect and plan for repeated coverage. The rooms stay single-pass; the connective tissue absorbs the repetition, and it absorbs it cheaply because a hallway is usually short, visually simple, and unlikely to be confused with anything else in the building the way two similar bedrooms are.

## Doorways carry the chain

None of this works if the doorway transitions themselves lose overlap. The routing plan only produces a continuous chain of images if you're still overlapping frames as you step from one room into the next, which is the subject "Doorways, Thresholds, and Keeping Continuity Between Rooms" covers directly — a route plan tells you which door to walk through and in what order, but it's the doorway technique that keeps the images on either side of that door actually connected. Get the order right and lose the doorway overlap, and you've built a set of well-shot, disconnected rooms. And the order and the overlap both depend on holding a repeatable standoff distance and heading as you move, which is the discipline "Standoff Distance and the Geometry of a Room" treats as the physical half of this problem — a route is only a plan on paper until your walking speed, distance from the walls, and camera angle actually deliver the overlap the plan assumes.

There's a structural reason to care about routing beyond just avoiding backtracking, too. Two-view geometry needs real translation between viewpoints to work at all: a sequence of frames connected mostly by rotation, with too little actual movement of the camera through space, is a known degenerate case where triangulation can't reliably resolve depth even with good feature matches. A sensible room-by-room walk, moving steadily forward through a real floor plan, naturally gives you that translation. Standing in the middle of a room and pivoting to shoot it from one spot does not, and no amount of clever routing between rooms fixes a capture technique that stays put within them.

## What this means for the walk

Before you pick up the camera, sketch the floor plan and find the one order that touches every room without doubling back. If the building is a simple path or a loop, that order exists and is usually obvious. If it branches, accept the hallway as the place that gets walked twice and keep every room single-pass. The goal isn't elegance for its own sake. It's giving the matcher a capture sequence where "nearby in the shot order" and "nearby in the room" stay the same thing for the entire walk, so the reconstruction never has to guess which of two similar-looking rooms you're actually standing in.
