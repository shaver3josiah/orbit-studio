# What Can Go Wrong: A Field Taxonomy of Bad Captures

Why does a capture that looked complete on the phone screen come back from COLMAP as a scattered handful of disconnected camera poses, or a splat full of ghosting and holes? The honest answer is that reconstruction failure is rarely one big mistake. It is almost always one of a small number of recurring failure modes, each with its own signature, and each visible in the raw photos if you know what to look for before you leave the building. This book is the map of that territory: a field taxonomy of the ways a capture goes wrong, so you can recognize the symptom on site instead of discovering it three hours later when the pipeline stalls.

Four failure modes account for most bad captures: blur, blank walls, exposure drift, and missed coverage. They are worth treating as a single family because they share a root cause. COLMAP's structure-from-motion stage depends on finding the same physical point in multiple photos and triangulating it. Every one of these four failures breaks that matching step in a different way, at a different stage of the pipeline, which is why they produce different-looking failures downstream even though they start from the same weakness.

**Blur** attacks the feature-detection step directly. SIFT, COLMAP's default feature detector, needs sharp local contrast to find repeatable keypoints (COLMAP 3.6 FAQ). A blurred photo does not just lose a few keypoints, it can lose the keypoints that would have connected that frame to its neighbors, effectively dropping the image from the reconstruction. The two causes of blur are different problems wearing the same coat. Camera shake comes from the operator's own hand or walking motion during the exposure, and the reciprocal rule gives a rough guardrail: minimum shutter speed should be at least 1 over the focal length, so roughly 1/50s at a 50mm-equivalent focal length (Photography Life / SLR Lounge). Subject motion blur, from something in the scene moving during the exposure, is a different failure with the same visual result, and the reciprocal rule does nothing for it. Modern stabilization can buy 4 to 7 stops of headroom against camera shake specifically, which matters for a solo phone operator walking rather than standing still. The companion volume "Recognizing Motion Blur Before You Leave the Room" goes deep on catching this on site, before the capture ends, rather than after.

**Blank walls and texture-poor surfaces** attack the same feature-detection step from a different angle. A blank white wall does not lack sharpness, it lacks anything for the detector to lock onto in the first place. SIFT and other hand-crafted keypoint detectors are documented to degrade seriously on weak-texture, repetitive-pattern, or low-light surfaces, because it becomes hard to find repeatable keypoints across viewpoints at all (mural-digitization study, npj Heritage Science). On these surfaces you get too few matches, or a high proportion of outlier matches, and either one can propagate into unstable camera poses or outright registration failure for that part of the scene. COLMAP's own tutorial names this directly: avoid completely texture-less surfaces like blank white walls as a first-order capture rule, not an edge case. When the surface itself cannot be avoided, COLMAP recommends increasing input resolution and expanding the patch window for dense reconstruction specifically to compensate for weak texture, though this is a dense-stage mitigation, not a fix for sparse matching. "Texture-Poor Walls and Why Blank Surfaces Fail to Register" is the dedicated volume on this failure mode; treat it as the deep dive this book only summarizes.

**Exposure and white-balance drift** attacks matching in a subtler way: it does not remove keypoints, it makes the same physical point look like two different points across frames. Auto-exposure and auto-white-balance are designed to keep each individual frame looking good, which is exactly the wrong goal for a photoset that needs to look consistent across dozens of frames. Auto white balance re-estimates color temperature independently on every shot, and is documented to visibly drift frame-to-frame under mixed or indoor lighting even when nothing else in the scene changed (Fstoppers). The fix is locking rather than disabling: Android's CONTROL_AE_LOCK and CONTROL_AWB_LOCK freeze the algorithm's last-computed values, which is why a locked-exposure workflow means finding good exposure once, early in the room, then holding it rather than never metering at all. A caution worth carrying: because the camera pipeline processes requests in flight, the settings that end up locked don't necessarily match the settings shown in the most recent preview frame you saw before locking (Android CaptureRequest reference), so verify the lock took effect rather than trusting the moment you pressed the button. Pix4D's best-practice guidance for photogrammetry generally names direct sunlight specifically as a source of harsh shadows and overexposure, and calls out maintaining consistent camera settings throughout a session as an explicit recommendation, not an optional nicety.

**Missed coverage** is the failure mode that has nothing to do with image quality at all. Every individual frame can be sharp, textured, and consistently exposed, and the reconstruction can still fail if the geometry of the walk itself was wrong. COLMAP's own guidance is blunt about the minimum: every object or surface needs to be seen in at least three images, and more is better, with a hard floor of three images required for geo-registration and alignment estimation (COLMAP FAQ, COLMAP tutorial). Below that floor there simply is not enough evidence to triangulate a stable position. Pix4D's baseline recommendation for general capture is at least 75% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap, and interior reconstruction specifically is called out as needing overlap around 90%, well above the general baseline, with terrestrial (ground-level) capture strongly preferred over other methods indoors. The older CIPA 3x3 architectural photogrammetry rules put the floor lower, at 60% for successive photographs, which is a reminder that these numbers come from different eras and different subject matter rather than one universal law; a modern indoor phone capture should lean toward the higher, interior-specific numbers rather than the older architectural baseline. Missed coverage also has a structural cousin worth naming here even though it belongs to a different book: rotating in place from one spot rather than walking to genuinely different viewpoints. COLMAP's tutorial states this as a direct instruction, do not take images from the same location, because pure rotation without translation is a known degenerate case for two-view geometry: the geometry math that resolves depth from parallax has nothing to work with when there is no baseline between camera positions. "What Makes a Photoset Reconstructable" covers the full logic of why coverage, overlap, and viewpoint diversity combine into a single requirement rather than three separate checklist items.

What all four failure modes share is timing. Every one of them is far cheaper to catch while you are still standing in the room than after the photos have been uploaded to a pipeline that will spend an hour computing before it tells you anything went wrong. Blur shows up on a zoomed playback check. A blank wall is visible to the naked eye the moment you frame the shot. Exposure drift is checkable by glancing at two frames from opposite ends of the room and asking if they look like the same lighting. Missed coverage is a matter of discipline in the walk pattern, not a defect you can spot in any single frame after the fact. None of these are hard to check. What is hard is remembering to check them, room after room, capture after capture, before the convenience of "that's probably fine" takes over. Building that habit, not any single clever fix, is what turns a taxonomy of failures into a capture that actually reconstructs.
