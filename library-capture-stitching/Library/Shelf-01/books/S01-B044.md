# Vertical Coverage: Ceilings, Floors, and the Frames People Skip

Walk a room with a phone or a 360 rig and watch where the lens actually points. It swings left and right, drifts forward, pivots at doorways. It almost never tilts up to the ceiling or down to the floor for more than a courtesy glance. Why does an operator who is careful about everything else, overlap, exposure, pacing, still leave the top and bottom of a room thin on coverage?

The answer is habit, not judgment. Ceilings and floors don't look like "the room" the way walls and furniture do. A ceiling is often a blank plane with a light fixture; a floor is a repeating pattern underfoot that the operator is standing on, not looking at. Neither one triggers the instinct that says "this needs a photo." But a Gaussian-splat reconstruction doesn't know what a room is supposed to contain. It only knows what it was shown. If you never point the camera up, there is no data up there, and the model has to guess or leave a hole. If you've read Live Coverage Tracking: Am I Done With This Room?, you already know that "done" is a coverage judgment made in the field, not after the fact, and vertical coverage is exactly the kind of gap that judgment tends to skip.

The cost is not abstract. Reconstruction depends on structure-from-motion first, and COLMAP's own documentation is explicit that every surface needs to appear in at least three images to be reliably registered, with more overlap being better up to a point of diminishing returns (COLMAP 3.6 Documentation — Frequently Asked Questions; COLMAP 3.6 Documentation — Tutorial). A ceiling shot from a single upward glance, once, from one spot in the room, does not meet that bar. It might get one or two frames of weak, oblique coverage and nothing else. When the sparse reconstruction can't triangulate points up there, gsplat training has nothing to build on, and the ceiling either turns to fog in the final splat or disappears into the void entirely.

Pix4D's best-practices guidance backs this up from the other direction. For large vertical objects and structures, Pix4D specifically calls for 90% overlap between images captured at the same height and at least 60% overlap between images captured at different heights (Pix4D — Best practices for image acquisition and photogrammetry). That is a direct statement that height itself is a coverage axis, not an afterthought bolted onto horizontal walking. The same source calls interior reconstruction a case that needs unusually high overlap, around 90%, and recommends terrestrial (ground-based) capture over other methods for it. Ceilings and floors are the "different height" band of an interior capture, and if the operator's overlap discipline only ever operates on the horizontal plane, the vertical band never gets its own pass.

There's a second, sharper reason this matters, and it's not just about overlap percentage. It's about how the camera moves.

Structure-from-motion needs translation, not just rotation, to recover depth. Pure camera rotation with no change in position is a textbook degenerate case: the geometry that lets two views triangulate a 3D point breaks down when the views differ only by rotation, because there isn't enough parallax to resolve depth even with plenty of well-matched features (structure-from-motion degeneracy under pure rotation and insufficient baseline, multiple arXiv surveys). This is exactly what happens when an operator, standing in the center of a room, tilts the phone up to grab a ceiling shot and tilts it back down. The camera rotated. It didn't translate. Any single frame captured that way, disconnected from a walked path, is starved of the baseline that triangulation needs. It doesn't matter how sharp or well-exposed that one frame is; geometrically it's close to useless on its own.

The fix follows directly from the diagnosis. Vertical coverage has to be walked, the same way horizontal coverage is walked, not grabbed as a single tilt-and-shoot from a fixed spot. That means moving through the room while the camera angle is held up toward the ceiling for a pass, and moving through again (or through the same path) with the angle held down toward the floor, so that each ceiling or floor patch is seen from multiple positions with real translation between them, not just multiple angles from one position. It's slower. It roughly doubles the number of vertical-facing frames an operator has to plan for per room. But it turns the ceiling and floor from a single degenerate glance into an ordinary, well-triangulated part of the scene, subject to the same three-images-minimum rule as everything else.

Ceilings and floors also tend to be the least textured surfaces in a room, which compounds the problem instead of excusing it. Flat painted ceilings and plain carpet or tile are close to the textureless surfaces that COLMAP's tutorial explicitly warns against, and separate research on SIFT-based matching confirms that weak-texture, repetitive surfaces degrade keypoint matching because the algorithm can't find enough repeatable, distinctive points across viewpoints (SfM feature matching failure on texture-poor and repetitive surfaces, npj Heritage Science mural-digitization study). A textured wall with furniture and edges gives SIFT plenty to grab even with average overlap. A blank ceiling gives it almost nothing, which is precisely why it needs more images per unit area, walked properly, rather than fewer. This is the same texture problem covered from a different angle in What Can Go Wrong: A Field Taxonomy of Bad Captures, and it is worth naming directly here: a low-texture surface with thin, rotation-only coverage is close to the worst combination an operator can hand the pipeline. Either problem alone is survivable. Together, they are how ceilings turn into holes.

None of this requires new equipment or a new pipeline. It requires a mental adjustment to the walk plan: treat "up" and "down" as two more sides of the room, not decorative extras. On a stills-based capture, that means adding a deliberate ceiling pass and a deliberate floor pass to the route, walked with the same step-and-shoot rhythm as the horizontal sweep, not a single tilted frame taken in passing. On a 360 rig capturing dual-fisheye footage for equirectangular stitching, the geometry is more forgiving since every frame already contains the full sphere, ceiling included, but the same translation requirement still applies: the rig has to move through the space for the ceiling and floor regions of each frame to triangulate against neighboring frames, not just sit and spin. The lens seeing the ceiling is not the same as the pipeline having enough of the ceiling, from enough positions, to reconstruct it.

The habit worth building is simple to state and easy to forget under time pressure: before leaving a room, ask whether the ceiling and floor got their own walked pass, at their own overlap standard, independent of whatever the walls received. If the answer is no, the capture isn't done, no matter how thorough the horizontal sweep felt. A splat with a perfect floor plan and a foggy ceiling reads as unfinished the moment anyone looks up, and the fix at that point is a return trip, not a patch.
