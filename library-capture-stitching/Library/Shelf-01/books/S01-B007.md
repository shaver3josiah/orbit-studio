# Exposure Consistency as a First-Class Requirement

Why does a scan with generous overlap and a steady walking pace still fall apart in COLMAP? The operator did the thing every tutorial tells them to do: cross the frame slowly, keep 70-80% of each shot repeated in the next, avoid blank walls. And yet the sparse reconstruction comes back sparse in the wrong sense, thin, broken into islands, missing whole hallways. The usual suspects get blamed first: not enough overlap, bad lens, cheap phone sensor. Often the real cause is simpler and less visible on the phone screen while shooting: the exposure moved.

Feature matching, the algorithmic core that COLMAP's structure-from-motion pipeline depends on, is not comparing shapes. It is comparing local pixel-intensity patterns. SIFT, COLMAP's default feature detector, builds a descriptor for each keypoint out of gradients in a small neighborhood of pixel brightness values. Two images of the same wall corner, from two nearby viewpoints, are supposed to produce nearly identical descriptors at that corner so the matcher can say "same point, seen twice." That correspondence is what triangulation runs on. If the brightness of that wall corner is meaningfully different between the two shots because the camera's auto-exposure re-metered mid-walk, the descriptors drift apart. Enough drift and the matcher either fails to pair the point at all or, worse, pairs it wrongly. COLMAP's own tutorial guidance makes the assumption explicit rather than incidental: it tells operators to capture under similar illumination and to avoid high-dynamic-range scenes, in the same breath as it tells them to maximize overlap and avoid texture-less surfaces. Exposure discipline sits on the same list as overlap discipline, not below it.

This is easy to miss because overlap is visible in a way exposure isn't. A phone's live preview shows you framing instantly. It does not show you, in any obvious way, that the auto-exposure algorithm just recalculated because you panned past a window. Auto white balance is worse in this respect: it recalculates a scene's color temperature independently on every shot, which is fine for one photo of one scene and actively harmful across a sequence meant to describe the same scene from many angles. A capture set with drifting AWB is not simply an inconvenience to color-correct later; the color drift is itself a change in the raw pixel values the matcher reads, which means every frame-to-frame handoff pays a small tax against the very consistency SIFT assumes.

Android's camera2 API names this problem directly in the semantics of its exposure lock. CONTROL_AE_LOCK freezes auto-exposure to its latest calculated values, and CONTROL_AWB_LOCK does the analogous thing for white balance, locking color computation until explicitly released. The two locks are documented as behaving independently, and Android's own guidance for anyone turning AE off is blunt: either set AWB and autofocus to OFF as well, or lock them explicitly first, specifically because leaving them on auto produces device-dependent, inconsistent results otherwise. That single piece of documentation is the technical spine of the argument this book is making: exposure consistency across a scan is not a nice-to-have refinement, it is a precondition the feature-matching stage assumes and does not check for you. Nothing in COLMAP inspects your capture for exposure drift and warns you. It just quietly produces fewer, worse, or wrong matches, and the failure surfaces three steps later as a broken reconstruction with no obvious cause.

Consider what "locked" buys and what it does not. AE_LOCK freezes the exposure calculation, but Android's own reference notes a subtlety worth taking seriously: because the camera pipeline processes requests in flight, the settings that actually end up locked are not guaranteed to be the exact settings reported in the most recent capture result you saw before locking. In practice this means the moment you choose to lock matters. Lock too early, before the algorithm has settled on a reading that represents the room rather than the doorway you were just standing in, and you carry a bad reading through the whole scan. This is exactly the territory covered by the companion volume AE Lock: What It Freezes and What It Doesn't, which goes into what the lock actually pins down versus what still moves underneath it. The practical discipline that solves the "lock too early" problem is covered in Metering Off a Mid-Tone Wall and Locking It: meter deliberately off something representative of the room's overall brightness before you commit the lock, rather than locking on whatever the algorithm happened to be reading when you tapped the button.

The stakes of getting this wrong are asymmetric in a useful way. A slightly under-exposed but consistent scan is recoverable; every frame is dim, but every frame is dim the same way, so the intensity relationships a matcher relies on between neighboring views survive intact. A scan where exposure drifts from frame to frame is not recoverable by the same logic, because the problem isn't the average brightness, it's the inconsistency between adjacent views specifically. This is also why the failure is not just about clipping or crushed shadows in any one frame, though those matter too and are their own subject in Clipping and Banding: Reading a Histogram in the Field. It is entirely possible for a scan to contain no clipped highlights and no obviously wrong exposures in isolation, and still fail on exposure consistency grounds, because the metric that matters for SfM is not "is this one photo well exposed" but "does this photo agree with its neighbors on how bright the same surface should look."

There is a second, quieter cost of exposure drift that matters for the specific texture-poor interiors an Orbit Capture operator spends most of their time in. Research on SfM failure modes on weak-texture and repetitive surfaces documents that hand-crafted keypoint detectors like SIFT already degrade on low-texture walls, producing sparse or unreliable matches because there simply aren't enough distinctive gradients to key off. A blank hallway wall is already a hard case for the matcher. Add exposure drift on top of an already texture-starved surface and you have removed the one thing the matcher had left to work with: a stable set of subtle brightness gradients that at least stayed put between frames. On a richly textured brick wall, minor exposure drift might get absorbed by the sheer number of candidate keypoints. On a painted drywall corridor, it often can't be, because there was so little signal to begin with that any additional noise from inconsistent brightness pushes the match rate below what triangulation needs.

None of this demands exotic gear. It demands a decision made once per room, before the first shot, rather than trusting the camera to keep making that decision correctly on your behalf as you walk. Lock exposure. Lock white balance. Meter off something that represents the room rather than whatever the lens happened to be pointed at in the first half-second. Then walk the loop and let the overlap and the coverage do their job, knowing that the brightness values under that overlap are the same values a matcher will actually see as "the same point" rather than "a point that got a little brighter." Overlap gives the matcher candidates to compare. Exposure consistency is what makes the comparison mean anything.
