# Deciding On-Site Whether a Room Needs a Reshoot

How do you know, standing in the room with your phone still warm from shooting, whether what you just captured is enough? Not "does it look fine on the little screen" but "will COLMAP actually register this room, and will the splat that comes out of it hold together." That question has an answer you can compute in the time it takes to walk out the door, and this book is about the three numbers that answer it.

The wrong way to make this call is to eyeball the thumbnails and move on. Thumbnails lie. A photo can look crisp at phone-screen size and still be soft enough to starve SIFT of the keypoints it needs, and a set of photos can each look individually great while still failing to overlap enough for COLMAP to chain them into a single reconstruction. You don't find out until hours later when the pipeline chokes on that room, and by then you're not in the room anymore. That's the whole argument for the sibling volume "The Cost of a Missed Room: Why Reshoots Are Cheaper On-Site" — a decision made now costs you two minutes; the same decision made after the drive home costs you the return trip. This book is the mechanism that makes the on-site decision cheap enough to actually run every time: a small rule that takes a blur score, an overlap estimate, and a one-glance visual read, and turns them into a single yes-or-no.

## The three inputs, and why none of them alone is enough

**Blur score.** The standard cheap test for image sharpness is the variance of the Laplacian: convert the frame to grayscale, convolve with a Laplacian kernel, take the variance of the response. Sharp images have abundant edges and produce high variance; blurry images produce low variance because the edges have been smeared away. PyImageSearch's widely-cited implementation is literally one line, `cv2.Laplacian(image, cv2.CV_64F).var()`, computed in 64-bit float rather than 8-bit because Laplacian responses can go negative and an unsigned integer type would clip them. Their worked example used a threshold of 100 to call something blurry, with blurry images in their test set scoring 64-83 and sharp ones scoring 200 and up — but the source is explicit that this number is "quite subjective to the contents of the image" and has to be tuned to your own material, not copied as gospel. That tuning is exactly the subject of this shelf's "Recognizing Motion Blur Before You Leave the Room," and the number your capture tool computes per frame should already reflect a threshold you've calibrated against your own gear and rooms, not the number from someone else's blog post.

Blur score alone tells you about one frame. It says nothing about whether that frame connects to its neighbors.

**Overlap estimate.** COLMAP's own tutorial states its capture requirement plainly: shoot with high visual overlap, make sure every surface is seen in at least three images, and don't just rotate in place from one spot — move to genuinely different viewpoints. Three images is the documented hard floor for the FAQ's geo-registration/alignment estimation, but "the minimum that technically works" and "the minimum you want to ship" are different numbers, and the photogrammetry industry has settled on something much higher for anything that isn't a throwaway test. Pix4D's best-practice guidance calls for at least 75% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap as a general baseline, and specifically flags interior reconstruction as needing high overlap around 90%, with ground-based (terrestrial) imagery strongly preferred for interiors over other capture methods. The CIPA 3x3 Rules, the older architectural-photogrammetry standard, put the floor lower — at least 60% between successive photographs, one-third for angled pairs — though the secondary literature is candid that those numbers predate modern laser scanning and phone-based capture and shouldn't be treated as gospel for this workflow. What all of these numbers agree on is the shape of the requirement: overlap isn't binary, it's a percentage, and low-overlap frames are the single most common reason a room fails to register even when every individual photo is tack-sharp.

An overlap estimate alone tells you about coverage. It says nothing about whether the surfaces you covered actually have anything on them for a feature detector to find.

**Visual read.** This is the one number you can't compute, and it's not a weakness of the rule, it's the reason the rule needs a human in the loop at all. SIFT, COLMAP's default feature detector, is documented to degrade badly on weak-texture, repetitive, or low-light surfaces — a blank white wall, a hallway of identical doors, a mirror. On those surfaces there simply aren't enough repeatable keypoints to match across viewpoints, and that failure mode doesn't show up in a blur score (the wall is perfectly in focus) or an overlap estimate (you covered it at 90%). It shows up only when you look at the actual content of the frame and recognize "this is a texture-poor surface, or this is a plausible false loop-closure" — the same visual-aliasing problem documented in indoor SLAM research, where rooms with repeated layouts get matched to the wrong instance of themselves. No formula catches that. Only a person looking at the room does.

## The rule: combine, don't cascade

The mistake is treating these three signals as a checklist to run in sequence and stop at the first failure. That produces false confidence: a room can pass a blur check and an overlap check and still be a bad reconstruction candidate because it's a hallway of identical white doors. The rule that actually holds up is a combination, evaluated together before you leave:

- If blur score is below your calibrated threshold on more than a small fraction of frames in the room, reshoot those frames regardless of what overlap says.
- If your overlap estimate falls meaningfully short of the interior target — think Pix4D's ~90% guidance as the ceiling to aim for, with CIPA's 60% floor as the point below which you should assume trouble — reshoot the gap, even if every frame you did capture is sharp.
- If the visual read flags texture-poor, reflective, repetitive, or low-light surfaces — the case COLMAP's own FAQ admits it has no explicit guidance for — treat that as an override that can force a reshoot even when both numbers pass, because neither number was built to detect it.

None of the three thresholds is a hard science. The blur number needs tuning to your device and lighting. The overlap number is a target drawn from analogous domains (aerial, architectural, industrial) rather than a number written for solo phone-based interior capture specifically, which the research notes for this shelf are honest about not finding. And the visual read is inherently a judgment call. But a rule built from three imperfect signals, checked together, catches far more real failures than any one of them checked alone, and it costs you a glance at three numbers before you walk out the door.

## What this means when you're actually in the room

The mechanical version of this book is one screen in your capture tool showing three things at once as you finish a room: a per-frame blur pass/fail count, a running overlap estimate against your target, and a prompt to actually look at what you shot. If all three are clean, move on. If any one of them isn't, that's the trigger this shelf's "Flagging and Triggering a Reshoot Mid-Session" covers: how the tool surfaces the flag and hands you the choice, not a silent log entry you discover later. The discipline this book is arguing for isn't complicated. It's just refusing to let one green light stand in for three.
