# Carrying One White Balance Across Differently-Lit Rooms

What color temperature do you set before you start walking?

That question sounds trivial until you actually stand in the hallway of a real building with a capture rig in hand. The living room has a west-facing window pouring in daylight. The kitchen has tungsten pendant lights. The office down the hall has fluorescent tubes overhead. Each of these light sources has a genuinely different color, and your camera has exactly one white balance setting active at any given moment. The question this book answers is simple to state and awkward to live with: should you hold that one setting fixed for the entire building, and if so, which setting do you pick?

The instinct to leave the camera on auto white balance (AWB) and let it adapt room by room feels reasonable. It is also the wrong move for this workflow, and the reasoning is worth spelling out because it is not about which look is prettier. It is about what the reconstruction pipeline downstream actually needs.

## Why one building needs one number

Photography guidance on this point is blunt: AWB is fine for a single scene under stable light, but it is documented to visibly drift frame to frame under mixed or colored indoor lighting, because the algorithm re-estimates color temperature independently on every single shot (Fstoppers, "White balance: Kelvin presets vs. Auto White Balance for consistency across a capture set"). Two photos of the same wall, taken seconds apart, can come back with slightly different color casts if AWB decided the scene looked slightly different each time. For a portrait sequence that is a rounding error. For a structure-from-motion pipeline that treats overlapping photos as evidence about the same physical surface, a shifting color cast on the same wall is not cosmetic. It is a mismatch. The same source that flags AWB's frame-to-frame drift also names the exact class of technique that suffers from it: panoramas, focus stacks, and blended-exposure sequences, all cases where color consistency across many frames is the entire point (Fstoppers). An Orbit Capture walkthrough is precisely this kind of sequence, just spread across an entire building instead of one tripod position.

This is the throughline that connects to a sibling volume in this shelf, AWB Lock vs. a True Fixed-Kelvin White Balance, which draws the harder distinction: locking AWB freezes whatever value the algorithm last converged on, which may not correspond to any deliberately chosen target, while a true fixed-Kelvin (or fixed-preset) setting pins a specific, known value you chose on purpose. Both approaches hold a single number steady across the room. Only one of them tells you what that number actually is.

## The building has no single correct answer

Here is the uncomfortable part. Indoor tungsten lighting sits around 3000K, and the human eye automatically compensates for the resulting orange cast without you noticing, but a camera sensor records that cast faithfully (Fstoppers). Daylight through a window runs cooler, fluorescent tubes cooler still and often greenish. There is no color temperature that is "correct" for all three rooms simultaneously, because they are not the same color of light. Camera2 on Android makes this concrete at the API level: CONTROL_AWB_MODE is limited to a fixed list of presets, among them INCANDESCENT (approximating CIE standard illuminant A) and FLUORESCENT (approximating CIE standard illuminant F2), each one tuned to a specific reference illuminant rather than an arbitrary Kelvin value (Android Developers, CameraMetadata reference). There is no "set to 4300K exactly" control in the standard API. You are choosing among named buckets, and every bucket is a compromise the moment you leave the room it was designed for.

Photography practice for the single-mixed-light-source case gives a useful analogy, even though a whole building is a bigger version of the same problem: when a room has daylight from a window and a tungsten lamp both contributing, the recommended practice is to set white balance for the dominant, key light source and accept a single global color-temperature compromise for the rest, rather than trying to correct multiple sources with one in-camera setting (Fstoppers). Scale that logic up from one room to a floor plan. You are not going to find a Kelvin value that renders the fluorescent office and the daylight-lit living room as equally neutral. You are choosing which room's light gets to be "correct" and letting the others drift, on purpose, in a known and consistent direction.

## What you actually get for holding it fixed

The payoff for this discipline is not a nicer-looking photo set. It is a texture atlas the reconstruction pipeline can trust. If you let AWB run free, a wall photographed near the end of the tungsten-lit kitchen and the same wall photographed a few frames earlier in warmer daylight spill can come back as two different colors, even though nothing about the physical wall changed. The dense reconstruction and the Gaussian-splat training that follows do not know the color shift is fictional. They treat it as scene detail. You get texture seams, color banding at the boundary where a mismatched patch meets its neighbor, and in bad cases a wall that looks like it changes hue as you walk past it in the final splat, purely because the capture, not the wall, changed hue mid-shoot. This is the exact failure mode addressed at length in another sibling volume, White Balance Drift and False Color Matches Across Rooms, and holding one fixed white balance for the whole walkthrough is the first and cheapest defense against it.

The tradeoff is that every room except your reference room will carry a visible, uniform color cast in the raw capture. The tungsten kitchen will look a little too warm if you locked to daylight; the daylight living room will look a little cool and blue if you locked to tungsten. That is not a defect to fix mid-shoot. It is the price of consistency, and it is a price worth paying, because a uniform cast is something color grading can correct evenly in post, on the whole model at once, while a shifting cast baked frame by frame is something no amount of post-processing fully undoes once it has already confused the matcher.

None of this removes the need for a deliberate strategy at the literal doorway between rooms, where the light actually changes. That handoff moment, and how to manage it without breaking the walk, is its own problem and its own book: Mixed Lighting Zones: Transition Strategy Between Rooms. What this volume establishes is the baseline decision that transition strategy has to work within: pick one white balance target before you start walking, based on the room or light source that matters most for the shoot, and do not let the camera renegotiate it as you move from room to room.

## What this means for the operator

Before you cross the threshold into the building, decide which light wins. If most of the square footage is daylight-lit, lock to daylight and let the tungsten rooms run warm. If the building is dominated by fluorescent-lit interior offices, lock to that instead and let the daylight room run cool. Whichever you choose, set it once, hold it for the entire walkthrough, and treat every other room's resulting cast as an expected, uniform offset rather than a problem to chase room by room. The alternative, an algorithm quietly renegotiating white balance every few seconds as you walk, produces exactly the kind of inconsistency a structure-from-motion pipeline is least equipped to forgive.
