# Adaptive Overlap: When to Shoot Tighter Than 70-80%

How much closer together should your shots be when the room is working against you?

The 70-80% overlap target from *Overlap as the Currency of Structure-from-Motion* is a baseline, not a law. It assumes an average room: enough texture on the walls for SIFT to lock onto distinct keypoints, enough geometric variety that no two corners look alike, enough light that features stay visible frame to frame. Most rooms meet that bar. Some don't, and shooting them at the baseline overlap is how a capture session turns into a broken reconstruction three hours later, back at your desk, watching COLMAP throw away half your images because it couldn't register them.

The question this book answers is narrow and practical: which rooms need tighter overlap than the baseline, and by how much.

## Why overlap has to flex

COLMAP's own tutorial is explicit that the target is "high visual overlap" between images, not a fixed percentage carved in stone, and it pairs that guidance with a related requirement: every surface should be seen in at least three images, more if possible. That "at least three" is not a suggestion. COLMAP's FAQ states it as a hard minimum for geo-registration and reliable point triangulation. Three images of a feature is the floor. It is not the target. The target is however many images it takes for the matcher to find enough correspondences to be confident, and that number goes up sharply in rooms where each individual match is weaker or less certain.

This is the same principle the aerial and industrial photogrammetry world has already priced into its published guidance. Pix4D's best-practices documentation doesn't give one overlap number for every job. It gives a baseline of 75% frontal and 60% side overlap for the general case, then raises that baseline for specific scenarios: 85% for forests and dense vegetation, 85% for city and urban reconstruction, 90% for large vertical objects at the same height. Notably, Pix4D calls out interior reconstruction by name as needing overlap around 90%, well above the general baseline, and pairs that with a recommendation to favor terrestrial (ground-based) imagery for interiors specifically. The CIPA 3x3 Rules for architectural photogrammetry land in a similar place from the other direction: a 60% minimum for successive photographs, one-third minimum for convergent pairs, treated explicitly as a floor rather than a comfortable operating point.

None of these sources hand you a single number for "texture-poor stairwell, tighter than 70-80%, use exactly X%." That specific guideline doesn't exist as a published standard, and this book will not pretend otherwise. What the aerial and architectural literature gives you is the pattern: every one of these sources raises overlap for a harder scene, and the size of the raise tracks how much harder the scene is. That pattern is what you apply indoors, reasoned from the same underlying cause the sources do name directly.

## The three failure modes, and why they demand more frames

**Texture-poor surfaces.** SIFT, COLMAP's default feature detector, needs local intensity variation to find a repeatable keypoint. A blank painted wall, a plain ceiling, a stretch of drywall with no visible seam gives it almost nothing to grab. Recent work on SfM over weak-texture and repetitive surfaces documents exactly this failure mode: hand-crafted detectors like SIFT degrade seriously on low-texture regions, correspondences there skew toward outliers or simply fail to appear at all, and that shortage becomes the bottleneck for the whole reconstruction, not just the one wall. *Texture-Poor Walls and Why Blank Surfaces Fail to Register* covers the underlying registration failure in depth; the overlap answer here is direct. If a keypoint match on a textured wall might succeed at a 40% overlap gap between frames because the detector has abundant features to work with, the same gap on a blank wall may produce nothing usable at all. Tightening overlap doesn't fix a feature-starved surface, but it increases the number of attempts the matcher gets at whatever weak signal is present, and increases the chance that at least one of the surrounding textured elements (a switchplate, a shadow line, a doorframe edge) rides along in enough shared frames to anchor the pose.

**Tight stairwells.** Two things compound here. First, the physical standoff distance is anchored to the width of the stairwell, and photogrammetry guidance on working distance treats standoff and overlap as the same discipline: holding a stable, consistent distance while walking a path is exactly what maintains that 50-80% frame-to-frame overlap in the first place. A stairwell forces you close to every surface, which shrinks your field of view per frame and means the same physical distance covers less new ground between shots. Second, and more structurally, a stairwell is a corridor of small, cramped rotational moves rather than clean lateral translation. Structure-from-motion is degenerate under pure rotation. Reliable triangulation depends on real translational baseline between views, and switchback turns on a landing tempt an operator into pivoting in place rather than genuinely walking a new position, which is precisely what COLMAP's tutorial warns against when it tells you not to shoot from the same location twice. In a stairwell, tighter overlap is partly compensating for reduced per-frame coverage and partly compensating for the operator's own tendency to rotate instead of translate on tight landings.

## What this means for the shoot

Treat 70-80% as the number you use when the room gives you no reason to distrust it. When you're walking into a room and you can already see textureless drywall, a repeating corridor, or a stairwell landing, tighten the overlap before you start shooting rather than reviewing the capture afterward and hoping. In practice that means shorter steps between frames, more images in the sequential-matcher window (COLMAP's sequential matcher looks at ±10 neighboring frames by default, so a denser image sequence widens the effective coverage that window sees), and no rotating in place to cover a corner you'd normally get from one step further back.

For repeating geometry specifically, tighter local overlap is necessary but not sufficient. A visually identical hallway segment can produce a confident, wrong feature match to the wrong instance of the pattern, which is a separate problem from insufficient overlap and is treated on its own terms in *Multi-Room Buildings with Repeating Geometry: Avoiding False Loop Closures*. Overlap density helps establish real local geometry; it does not, by itself, prevent the matcher from confusing one identical room for another three doors down.

The honest summary is that no published source hands you a precise multiplier for exactly how much tighter than 70-80% to shoot in a texture-poor or tight-stairwell room. What the aerial and architectural literature consistently supports is the direction and the reasoning: harder scenes get more overlap, scaled to how starved the scene is for reliable matches, with COLMAP's own three-image minimum as the absolute floor you should never approach as a target. Reading a room before you shoot it, and treating the standard overlap target as a default rather than a rule, is the actual skill this book teaches.
