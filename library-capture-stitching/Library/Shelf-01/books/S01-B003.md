# Overlap as the Currency of Structure-from-Motion

Why does everyone who touches this pipeline, from the COLMAP maintainers to Pix4D's field engineers, converge on the same demand: shoot with heavy overlap between consecutive frames? The answer is that overlap is not a nicety layered on top of a capture. It is the raw material the entire reconstruction is built from, and every stage downstream of your camera spends that material without being able to manufacture more.

Think about what a structure-from-motion pipeline actually has to do. It never sees the room. It sees a stack of flat JPGs and has to infer, purely from how the same physical points appear to shift between frames, where the camera was standing and where those points sit in 3D. That inference only works if a given point shows up in more than one photo, seen from a slightly different angle. One photo of a doorway is a picture. Two overlapping photos of that doorway, taken a step apart, are a triangulation problem the software can actually solve. Overlap is the thing that turns a photo collection into a solvable geometry problem.

COLMAP's own tutorial says this almost bluntly: capture images with high visual overlap, make sure every object is seen in at least three images, and don't take images from the same spot. The "at least three" number is not a suggestion buried in a forum post; it is stated as a hard minimum for geo-registration and object alignment in COLMAP's FAQ. Two overlapping views can triangulate a point, but a third view is what lets the system cross-check that triangulation and catch a bad match before it corrupts the pose estimate. Overlap of two isn't the target. Overlap of three-plus, sustained across the whole set, is.

This is why the industry numbers cluster where they do. Pix4D's baseline recommendation is at least 75% frontal overlap and 60% side overlap for general work, climbing to 85% or higher for harder scenarios: forests, snow, corridors, large vertical surfaces. Interior reconstruction, notably, is called out on its own as needing overlap around 90%, with Pix4D specifically recommending terrestrial (ground-based) capture over other methods for interiors. That is a strong signal for anyone doing Orbit Capture: your subject, a building interior, is already in the category photogrammetry treats as demanding, not average. The older CIPA 3x3 architectural documentation rules put the floor lower, around 60% for successive photos, but the source material itself flags those numbers as dated relative to modern practice. The center of gravity across every serious source, old and new, sits in the 70-90% band, and interiors sit at the high end of that band. That is where the "70-80%" target this shelf works from comes from: a genuinely conservative middle position, not a number pulled from nowhere.

It helps to see what happens mechanically when overlap runs thin, because the failure isn't abstract. Feature matching, SIFT by default in COLMAP, works by finding distinctive points in one image and locating the same points in another. When two consecutive frames only share a sliver of the room, there are fewer candidate points in common, and the ones that do survive are more likely to be mismatched or thrown out as unreliable. COLMAP's FAQ notes that SIFT performs well specifically under moderate-to-high overlap, and degrades on scenes with limited overlap; a companion body of matching research documents the same failure mode even more sharply on textured surfaces, where thin overlap compounds with weak texture to strangle the correspondence count entirely. Thin overlap doesn't just make matching harder. It shrinks the number of frames any given point survives in, which is exactly the count COLMAP's own three-image minimum is protecting. Push overlap too low and you fall under that floor without ever noticing, because nothing in the capture process warns you at the time.

There is a second, quieter failure mode that overlap protects against: baseline. Structure-from-motion needs the camera to actually move between frames, not just rotate. If you spin in place to cover an angle instead of physically stepping between shots, you can generate frames with plenty of visual overlap and almost no useful triangulation signal, because pure rotation is a documented degenerate case for two-view geometry. There isn't enough parallax to resolve depth reliably, no matter how many matched features exist. This is one reason overlap has to be reasoned about in terms of a step you take, not a percentage you eyeball on a screen; a companion volume in this shelf, "The 70-80% Overlap Target in Practice: Counting Steps, Not Percentages," works through exactly how to convert that target into a walking cadence you can execute without a calculator in your hand mid-shoot.

None of this means more overlap is free. COLMAP's tutorial is explicit that excess images beyond what reconstruction needs slow processing without improving quality, and matching cost that scales with image count is a real, measurable tax on a solo operator's laptop. Overlap has a floor you must clear and a ceiling past which you are just paying compute for nothing. The right target sits close enough to the floor to avoid waste, far enough above it to survive a hallway with blank walls or a room with repeated furniture. That is a genuinely different question from "did I hit the number," and this shelf treats it separately: "A Field Overlap Estimator: Comparing Consecutive Frames" gives you a way to check what you actually captured against what you intended, after the fact rather than by faith.

What this means in practice is that overlap deserves to be treated as the first-order variable of a capture, ahead of resolution, ahead of lighting, ahead of camera choice. A slightly noisy but well-overlapped photoset will out-reconstruct a razor-sharp one shot with big gaps between frames, because the pipeline literally cannot recover geometry it was never given the correspondences to see. This is also why overlap sits upstream of the broader question of whether a photoset will reconstruct at all; "What Makes a Photoset Reconstructable" treats overlap as one input among several, alongside texture, lighting consistency, and baseline, but it is worth saying here plainly: of those inputs, overlap is the one a solo operator controls most directly, frame by frame, just by choosing how far to step before pressing the shutter again.
