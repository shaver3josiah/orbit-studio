# Generative Fill and Its License Minefield

Here is the question this volume answers: when a generative model touches your bridge scan, is it telling the truth more completely, or is it making something up and handing you a license you can't legally ship? The honest answer splits cleanly once you look at what each tool actually does versus what its terms of service allow, and the split rarely lines up the way you'd hope. Some of the most capable models are the least usable. Some of the safest licenses attach to models too heavy for the hardware you actually have.

Start with what the twenty leveled equirectangular stills from an Insta360 X4 Air already give you, described in full in "Twenty Stations, One Bridge: A Sparse-View Playbook for Outdoor Splats." Twenty overlapping panoramas of a bridge and its roadways is genuine multi-view coverage. COLMAP can triangulate real camera poses from it, and gsplat can optimize real Gaussians against real photographic evidence. That is photogrammetry: every splat in the finished scene traces back to something a camera actually saw. The moment you introduce a generative model into that pipeline, you introduce the possibility of a splat that traces back to nothing but a training distribution's guess. For a hobby diorama, a guessed handrail is a curiosity. For a tool meant to document a real structure, a guessed handrail is a liability wearing the costume of a fact.

So the first and most important distinction is not about licenses at all. It's about role. Full generative world models, the kind that take a photo or two and hallucinate an entire explorable scene, are solving a different problem than the one this shelf cares about. The research is unambiguous that a from-scratch generative world model would hallucinate structure that doesn't match reality, a bad tradeoff when the point is documenting an actual bridge rather than dreaming up a plausible one. Used narrowly, though, generative fill earns its place: patching sky and horizon regions, which register as near-zero-parallax noise no matter how carefully you shoot, smoothing occlusion shadows behind railings, girders, and roadside vegetation that no camera position ever saw, and lightly densifying views to steady a reconstruction between sparse capture points. That's the whole legitimate job. Anything past it is a different product wearing a splat's clothing, and "Panorama-Native Splatting: The Promise and Stall of the Direct-From-Equirect Family" covers why that adjacent family of single-pano-to-world tools keeps stalling out on exactly this ambiguity.

## The territory you cannot ship into

Tencent's HunyuanWorld line is the most capable generative world pipeline in this space by a wide margin. HunyuanWorld 1.0 shipped in July 2025, HunyuanWorld 1.1 (also called WorldMirror) followed that October, and HY-World 2.0 arrived in April 2026 with companion releases through May. The 2.0 line takes a panorama through a four-stage pipeline, trajectory planning, world expansion, and Gaussian splatting optimization, and produces real 3DGS, mesh, or point-cloud assets that import cleanly into Unity or Unreal. On capability alone it would be the obvious pick.

It is also the clearest reason this volume exists. The Tencent HunyuanWorld license explicitly defines its usable territory as worldwide "excluding the European Union, United Kingdom and South Korea." Community discussion on Hacker News flagged this as non-standard the moment it appeared, and HY-World 2.0's own license carries the identical exclusion, plus a cap requiring separate written approval from Tencent once a product exceeds one million monthly active users, plus new prohibitions on military use and high-stakes automated decisions. None of that is a footnote you can quietly work around. An open-source tool that anyone, anywhere, can clone and run cannot vendor a model that is illegal to use in three named jurisdictions. If HunyuanWorld appears in Orbit Studio at all, it belongs as a manual, clearly labeled opt-in Colab cell, the same pattern already used for swappable components like Brush, never as a default path a new user stumbles into.

DreamScene360 sits in a smaller but similar trap. It directly accepts a real user-supplied equirectangular panorama, which makes it a tempting fit for this exact capture workflow. But it's built on the original Inria/MPII 3D Gaussian Splatting codebase, whose license grants rights for research purposes only and prohibits commercial use without explicit consent. A tool that inherits a research-only license cannot be repackaged into something others might use commercially, so it stays out of anything Orbit Studio distributes, however good its panorama-to-Gaussians math looks on paper.

## The territory you can, carefully

Two projects clear the bar, with real caveats attached.

WorldGen, from developer ZiYang-xie, is Apache-2.0 licensed and accepts a user's own 360 panorama directly through a `pano_image` parameter, no coaxing required. Its low-VRAM mode needs roughly 10GB, comfortably inside a free Colab T4's 16GB, and it was still being actively improved through April 2026, when a DA-2 depth estimator replaced the older UniK3D backend and the cubemap pipeline was reworked down to six faces. The catch is that "Apache-2.0" describes the orchestration code, not necessarily every sub-model bundled with it. The default DA-2 depth path looks clean, but the SHARP-derived cubemap option shares lineage with a family of models that turn up elsewhere in this research under Apple's CC BY-NC-4.0, non-commercial research license. Background inpainting for occluded regions is also still described as experimental. The safe posture is to restrict WorldGen to its default, Apache/MIT sub-backends only, and to treat it as an optional, off-by-default fill pass aimed only at known-bad regions like sky and small occlusion gaps, not as a scene generator running by default over the whole reconstruction.

SPAG4d is the smaller, scrappier option, and it fits the bridge scenario almost too neatly: it converts a single equirectangular photo straight to a 3D Gaussian splat via monocular depth, with an explicit disocclusion and sky-hole-filling stage built in, and it even ships a portable Windows distribution as of February 2026, a nice match for a Windows laptop reader. Its core and two of its four depth-to-Gaussian backends, DA360 and DAP, are MIT licensed and need under 3GB of VRAM. But the other two backends, SHARP 360 and PaGeR, run on Apple's CC BY-NC-4.0 research weights, and its higher-quality hole-filling option, a 14B-parameter model called ArtiFixer3D, sits under Adobe's non-commercial Research License and needs WSL2 or Docker with roughly 140GB of disk, well outside what this pipeline should ever default to. SPAG4d is also a genuinely small project, a couple hundred stars, one maintainer, no independent review of its quality claims beyond its own README. Worth watching, worth using restricted to its MIT backends, not worth trusting blind.

## The repair cluster, and why NVIDIA's name on it matters

A separate cluster of tools treats generative fill not as scene creation but as post-hoc repair: render a rough fly-through of an already-reconstructed scene, clean up the artifact-heavy frames with a diffusion model, and feed the cleaned frames back in as extra training signal. Every method in this cluster is a data-augmentation front-end sitting in front of the same gsplat optimizer, which means any of them can in principle slot into this pipeline without touching the trainer itself.

NVIDIA's Difix3D+ is the one actually usable today. It won a CVPR 2025 Oral and Best Paper Finalist nod, ships public weights on Hugging Face as `nvidia/difix` and `nvidia/difix_ref`, runs a single-step, SD-Turbo-scale diffusion model, the lightest architecture in the whole cluster, and documents a direct nerfstudio and gsplat integration point. It's plausible on a free T4. The condition is that its checkpoints and code sit under NVIDIA's non-commercial research-and-evaluation license, with a Stability AI Community License component layered on top. That makes Difix3D+ a reasonable optional post-process for a personal bridge scan, and reasonable to document as an opt-in Colab cell, but anything trained with it should be labeled non-commercial in the README, not treated as a default step in a tool meant for general redistribution.

Everything else in the repair cluster is currently out of reach for different reasons. GSFixer posts the strongest artifact-restoration numbers of the group but runs on a 5-billion-parameter CogVideoX-5B video-diffusion backbone that took eight H20 or H100 GPUs roughly four days to train, needs fifty denoising steps at inference, and, tellingly, has no LICENSE file in its repository at all, which defaults to all-rights-reserved. GSFix3D needs a per-scene diffusion fine-tuning pass and was only tested on a 24GB card, well past a free T4, under a license stack that includes the non-commercial Gaussian-Splatting-License and a RAIL++-M model license. GaMO is the closest conceptual match, reframing the whole problem as geometry-aware outpainting on top of a DUSt3R-plus-3DGS coarse reconstruction, running in about eight minutes a scene on an RTX 4090, and it's CC BY 4.0 with public code. But as of this writing it still needs three separate conda environments to run, ships only an indoor demo scene, and has no outdoor or panorama example to point at, so it's a project to watch, not to build against yet. VidSplat and OracleGS are further out still: promisingly licensed, aimed at exactly this sparse-outdoor problem, but neither had a confirmed working public code release as of this writing. And a structural caveat applies to the entire repair cluster: not one of these six methods has been evaluated on true 360-degree equirectangular captures. Every benchmark number comes from pinhole multi-view splits. Any PSNR gain you'd expect on twenty bridge panoramas is a guess until someone actually measures it.

## What this means for the build

None of this argues against generative fill. It argues for keeping it small, optional, and honestly labeled. The reconstruction from the twenty real photographs is the product; a fill pass is a patch applied only where the camera genuinely couldn't see, sky, tight occlusion shadows, thin capture-edge gaps, never a replacement for coverage the operator should have simply walked further to get. Ship WorldGen's Apache-licensed default path or SPAG4d's MIT backends as an off-by-default option for those narrow patches. Offer Difix3D+ as a clearly non-commercial opt-in cleanup pass. Leave HunyuanWorld, DreamScene360, GSFixer, and GSFix3D out of anything distributed, not because they don't work, but because their licenses were never written for a tool anyone in the world can pick up and run. And measure everything the industry hasn't measured yet, because as "What the Industry Shipped While You Were Capturing: Marble, Scaniverse, and the Open Lane" makes clear, nobody selling a splat product today accepts a handful of real still panoramas and treats them as ground truth the way this pipeline does. That gap is the whole reason a careful fill pass, rather than a generative substitute, is worth building at all.
