# Panorama-Native Splatting: The Promise and Stall of the Direct-From-Equirect Family

You have twenty leveled equirectangular JPGs of a bridge and its roadways, shot on an Insta360 X4 Air. Before any of them touch a Gaussian splat trainer, the current Orbit Studio pipeline explodes each one into a ring of perspective crops, feeds those crops to pycolmap for pose recovery, and only then starts training. It works, but it is an odd amount of preprocessing for a camera that already captured a full sphere in one shot. So the natural question: why not skip the cropping and train the splat directly on the panorama itself?

A handful of research groups have been asking exactly that since 2024, and the answer they have produced is genuinely interesting: it delivers real quality gains where it works, but the tooling around it is not ready to replace perspective-split for a shoot like this one. Understanding why requires separating two different things that all travel under the "panorama-native" banner: models that optimize one scene at a time against many panoramas, and models that predict a splat in a single feed-forward pass from just two.

## What "direct from equirect" actually buys you

The pitch is simple. An equirectangular image already covers 360 by 180 degrees. Cropping it into a cubemap or a ring of perspective views to feed a conventional 3D Gaussian Splatting pipeline introduces seams at the crop boundaries and wastes samples on overlapping regions near the poles. A rasterizer that understands the sphere natively, whether by splatting Gaussians straight onto equirectangular screen space or by sampling rays directly off the unit sphere, avoids both problems. OmniGS makes this case directly: it is a GPU-accelerated omnidirectional rasterizer that projects 3D Gaussians onto equirectangular screen space "with no cubemap or tangent-plane approximation needed." ODGS, its closest sibling, takes a slightly different tack and decomposes each omnidirectional image into six perspective cubemap faces internally before rasterizing, specifically to dodge the distortion a naive single-equirectangular rasterizer produces, but it still treats the panorama as one first-class capture rather than six independently-posed photos. Both report roughly 100x faster optimization and rendering than the NeRF-based omnidirectional baselines that came before them.

That is a real result. But it comes with a catch that shows up before training even starts: COLMAP has no spherical or equirectangular camera model. Every direct-equirect method surveyed here, without exception, recovers poses through OpenMVG instead, or in one hobbyist recipe, through OpenSfM configured with a spherical camera model. So "direct from equirect" does not mean pose-free end to end. It means you have swapped a mature, well-understood SfM stage (pycolmap, covered in depth in Splitting the Sphere: Perspective Rigs for Panorama SfM in Practice) for a less mature one, in exchange for a cleaner final rasterization step.

## The feed-forward family: fast, but pairwise

The second branch of this field skips per-scene optimization entirely. PanoSplatt3R, OmniSplat, and PanSplat all predict a Gaussian splat in a single forward pass, the same instinct behind the pointmap networks covered in Feed-Forward Geometry: VGGT, MapAnything, and the End of Mandatory Feature Matching. But where VGGT and MapAnything are built to ingest many images at once, all three of these panorama models take exactly two panoramas as input and nothing more.

PanoSplatt3R is the most ambitious of the three on paper. It is pose-free, built on a MASt3R and DUSt3R-style backbone with a "RoPE rolling" position encoding designed to model the horizontal periodicity of a panorama, and its project page states plainly, "Given two panorama images as input." Its code, weights, training scripts, and evaluation scripts are all fully released on GitHub. But the GitHub API reports the license as null and no LICENSE file exists anywhere in the repository, so despite the complete release there is currently no explicit grant to reuse it. Its only public training and test data is HM3D and Replica, both indoor scan datasets, so the paper's claim of "indoor and outdoor, egocentric, and roaming" generalization has no public outdoor artifact backing it. Training needs at least 48GB of GPU memory (the authors used NVIDIA L40 cards), and inference-time VRAM is not documented anywhere in the repo, paper, or project page. The repository has been dormant since October 17, 2025, and an open issue asking about unposed inference on custom data remains unresolved.

OmniSplat and PanSplat are both MIT licensed, which removes the reuse question, but neither is pose-free. OmniSplat runs on top of posed MVSplat and needs OpenMVG-derived poses per scene. Its saving grace is that it is the only one of the three with genuine outdoor evaluation, on 360Roam, OmniPhotos, OmniBlender, OmniScenes, and 360VO, several of which include roaming outdoor and urban scenes. But it also ships a known, unpatched pixel-misalignment bug in its equirectangular warping, and an open GitHub issue titled "How to infer on my own dataset?" sits unanswered. PanSplat produces a spherical 3D Gaussian pyramid from two wide-baseline panoramas and has been fine-tuned on the real-world 360Loc dataset, which does cover outdoor urban areas and parks, not just synthetic interiors. Its problem is hardware: the 4K checkpoint needs at least 24GB of VRAM, RTX 3090 class, trained on an A100. A free Colab T4, which carries 16GB, cannot run it. PanSplat's real-video workflow also runs stella_vslam SLAM first to recover poses, so it is not pose-free either.

None of these three fuses more than two panoramas into one globally consistent scene. That is the structural gap. For twenty bridge stations, adopting any of them would mean building your own pairwise-chaining and pose-graph fusion on top, which is new engineering for a capture tool, not a drop-in replacement for the multi-view registration that pycolmap already does across all twenty images at once.

## What is actually missing

Two gaps recur across almost every source in this family, and they are worth naming plainly because they are the reason this remains a watch-list rather than a pipeline swap.

The first is licensing. OmniGS is GPL-3.0, a viral license that sits awkwardly inside an otherwise permissively-licensed open pipeline. PanoSplatt3R ships complete code and weights with no license file at all. Spherical-GOF's repository claims an MIT license in its README but contains only a .gitignore, a LICENSE file, a README, and an assets folder, no actual source code, two commits total, with the README saying the implementation "will be released." ErpGS's project page says outright, "Code (Coming Soon)." TPGS claims MIT and has seventeen commits, but its README is missing dependency lists and setup instructions despite the paper's claim that code and models are available. SC-OmniGS, the self-calibrating pose-free model that would most directly help a sparse, still-only capture, has no license file and its GitHub repository is a project webpage only, no training code at all.

The second gap is multi-view fusion, and it cuts across both branches of the family. The feed-forward pairwise models cap out at two views by design. The per-scene optimizers that could in principle take twenty views, OmniGS and ODGS, both still depend on OpenMVG for pose recovery rather than any native multi-view step of their own, and both are showing their age: ODGS has had no commits since December 26, 2024, and OmniGS has seventeen commits and no formal release. Neither has been battle-tested on anything resembling twenty widely-spaced outdoor stills of a bridge and its surrounding roadways; their published benchmarks (OmniBlender, Ricoh360, 360Roam, OmniScenes, 360VO) mix indoor scans with driving-style capture, not a sparse discrete-still survey.

## The successors to watch

A newer wave of 2026 papers is chasing the same combination VGGT delivered for ordinary perspective images: pose-free reconstruction that scales past two views. CylinderSplat, accepted to ICLR 2026, is explicitly built to handle a variable number of input views, from a single panorama up to many, which is structurally closer to a twenty-still bridge capture than anything above. Its code is referenced but its actual contents were not independently verified as of this writing. Spherical-GOF claims a 57 percent reduction in depth-reprojection error over the strongest baseline by sampling rays directly on the unit sphere rather than through projection-based rasterization, an approach with real appeal for a sparse outdoor capture, but again, no source code ships yet. SC-OmniGS remains the cleanest statement of the end goal, joint optimization of Gaussians, poses, and camera model with no precomputed poses at all, but it too is webpage-only. None of these are usable today. All three are worth re-checking in perhaps six months, since none showed active feature development in the February through July 2026 window covered by this research.

## What this means for a twenty-panorama bridge shoot

For now, the honest recommendation is to leave the existing perspective-crop-plus-pycolmap stage alone. It is the only path surveyed here with proven multi-view fusion across dozens of outdoor images, and every direct-equirect alternative, from OmniGS's heavy native CUDA build to PanoSplatt3R's unlicensed weights, asks you to trade that proven step for something narrower, staler, or legally unclear. If you want to see the direct-from-equirect idea working end to end without waiting on any of these papers, the closest thing to a runnable recipe is a hobbyist project, inuex35/360-gaussian-splatting, which runs OpenSfM with a spherical camera model and trains a modified 3DGS with a panorama flag; it is unpublished and unverified for maintenance, but it is the fastest way to see what a seamless equirect rasterizer actually looks like on your own bridge photos before investing further. Whichever reconstruction path you choose, it feeds into a separate decision entirely, covered in The Trainer Field in Mid-2026: Brush, LichtFeld Studio, and the Quiet Stall of Nerfstudio, about which trainer actually consumes the poses and points you recover. Panorama-native splatting is a real and active research direction. It is just not, as of mid-2026, a finished tool.
