# What the Industry Shipped While You Were Capturing: Marble, Scaniverse, and the Open Lane

You spent an afternoon on a bridge deck with an Insta360 X4 Air, leveling the tripod at twenty stations along the span and the roadways underneath it, collecting still equirectangular JPGs one leveled shot at a time. It is slow, unglamorous work. Meanwhile, a few hundred miles away, companies with a combined market capitalization in the tens of billions were shipping "photo to 3D world" products at a pace that made trade press headlines every few weeks. The natural question, the one this volume answers, is simple: did any of them ship the thing you are building by hand? Between February and July of 2026, the honest answer is no. Not one mainstream product turns a sparse set of still 360 photos into a metrically faithful multi-view reconstruction. They all want something else from you, either a video, a generative prompt, or a headset strapped to your face. Understanding exactly what each of them wants, and why, tells you precisely where the open lane is and why your twenty photographs are not a hobbyist's shortcut around real infrastructure but a genuinely uncovered capability.

Start with the company that raised the most money to avoid answering your question directly. World Labs, Fei-Fei Li's world-model startup, launched a public World API on January 21, 2026, accepting text, single images, multi-image sets, 360-degree panoramas, and video, with export paths into simulators like NVIDIA Isaac Sim and MuJoCo. A month later, on February 18, the company announced a billion dollars in new funding from investors including AMD, Autodesk, Emerson Collective, Fidelity, NVIDIA, and Sea, timed to coincide with the commercial launch of its flagship product, Marble. On April 2, Marble shipped version 1.1 and 1.1 Plus, the latter auto-expanding a generated world's spatial coverage without the user manually pushing out boundaries. It is, by any measure, the most polished few-image-to-explorable-world product on the market. But read the product documentation carefully and the crucial qualifier appears: Marble's multi-image mode does not reconstruct your bridge, it generates a plausible one. The company's own language describes the process as combining photos or video of a real place to "generate a 3D world with elements of the real-world space," and a 360 panorama upload is offered as the more accurate, more controlled input option precisely because multi-image input is admittedly the looser, generative path. Feed Marble twenty photos of your bridge and you will get something that looks like a bridge, populated with elements drawn from what it saw, not a model where the railing is where the railing actually is. That distinction between generating elements and reconstructing geometry is the same one that runs through the licensing questions covered in Generative Fill and Its License Minefield, where the underlying world models doing this kind of synthesis carry restrictions and behaviors that matter a great deal once you ask a system to fill in what it did not actually see.

Niantic looked like the more promising candidate, if only because it is the closest thing to a company built around exactly your camera category. Now operating as Niantic Spatial after John Hanke sold off the studio's game business to Scopely, the company shipped a major Scaniverse update on April 7, 2026, adding 360-camera-to-splat processing and a new browser-based workspace built to scale up to industrial and city-scale environments, with multiple scans fusing into one incrementally updating model. This sounds like exactly what a bridge-and-roadway capture needs. Then you hit the documentation for the 360-camera scanning guide, and the door closes: the pipeline requires video only, either native .insv files from Insta360 X5, X4, X3, or One RS cameras, or equirectangular .mp4 from a handful of other 360 cameras. The guide states the limitation without hedging: "Still panoramic images (.jpg, .png, etc.) are not supported." Scaniverse processes 360 video files only. The feature is also gated behind a paid Plus or Pro subscription running $20 to $50 a month depending on how long a video you need to process. Your twenty leveled stills, taken precisely because still photography lets you control exposure and framing at each capture station rather than accepting whatever a moving video frame gives you, are not a format Scaniverse will even open. The one genuinely valuable thing Niantic Spatial handed the rest of the industry that same season was SPZ 4, an open-source compressed gaussian-splat file format the company released as a faster, smaller successor to the original SPZ, compressing three to five times faster and loading one and a half to two times faster than before while keeping files roughly a tenth the size of uncompressed PLY. Spark, the renderer already sitting underneath Orbit Studio's viewer, auto-detects and loads .spz natively, which makes swapping your output artifact to it close to a free win. The mechanics of that swap, and the rest of the current web splat stack around it, are the subject of Shipping a World: SOG, SPZ 4, and the New Web Splat Stack.

Insta360 itself, the company that actually made your camera, answered the same way. Its partnership with Splatica, announced April 24, 2026 in the days after NAB Show, turns X5 or Antigravity A1 360 video into gaussian splats with a web viewer and export paths into Unity, Unreal Engine, Isaac Sim, and Omniverse, promising a simulation-ready environment from five minutes of video footage in a matter of hours. Notice, again, video, and notice that it is the newer X5 and Antigravity cameras named, not the X4 Air you are shooting with. Splatica's pricing sets an unambiguous market anchor for what this convenience currently costs: a Basic plan at $49.95 a month buys 300 points, enough for roughly one scene from a five-minute video, with Pro and Studio tiers running $149.95 and $299.95 a month and no free trial offered at any level. A rumored Insta360 X6 surfaced in FCC filings on July 3, 2026, but nothing about its specs, pricing, or splat capability is confirmed as of this writing, so it tells you nothing you can build against today. What the Splatica pricing does tell you is that the industry has already decided this capability is worth fifty to three hundred dollars a month, which is worth keeping in mind when you tally what a free Colab T4 and an open pipeline cost you instead.

It would be a mistake to read all of this and conclude that outdoor, exterior gaussian splats are a niche use case nobody wants. The opposite is true, and it is the strongest piece of validation in this whole stretch of the industry. Zillow's SkyTour, an exterior 3D flyover built on gaussian splatting, launched in mid-2025 on Showcase Listings, letting a browsing home shopper orbit a property and adjust altitude around the exterior. Apartments.com and its parent CoStar followed with Matterport 3D Exteriors, a gaussian-splatting drone flyover viewer for apartment complexes with an "Enter Building" control that hands off from the exterior splat into Matterport's existing interior tour, now bundled into the platform's higher advertising tiers. Two of the largest real estate listing platforms in the country have independently decided that splatting the outside of a structure, not just a living room, is worth shipping at scale. A bridge and the roadways around it sit squarely inside that same category of target. You are not solving a problem nobody has; you are early to a shape of problem the market has already started paying to solve, just not yet for infrastructure, and not yet from a stack of still photographs.

Meta's contribution rounds out the picture by closing off yet another possible shortcut. Hyperscape Capture, the company's splat-capture tool for Quest headsets, is a live walkthrough experience: you put on the headset, physically walk the edges of a room, and lean in close to inspect objects, all in a space roughly three meters by three meters. There is no documented path for importing photos or video, no panorama input, and no outdoor or large-scale capture guidance at all. As of March 24, 2026, viewing and sharing these captures moved out of Horizon Worlds into a standalone app, and Horizon Worlds itself is being pulled off Quest headsets entirely by June 2026 to go mobile-only. Whatever Meta is building here, it assumes you and the headset are standing in the space together. A hundred-meter bridge over a highway is not that space.

Line all of it up and the shape of the open lane becomes obvious. Marble generates plausible worlds from photos but does not reconstruct real ones. Scaniverse and the Insta360-Splatica pipeline both do genuine reconstruction, but only from video, and only behind a subscription. Meta requires your physical presence with a headset. Zillow and Matterport validate that exterior splatting is a real market, but they are shipping that market with drone footage and dedicated capture crews, not a stack of stills a solo operator shot with a tripod. The one open-source project that comes close to your exact input, a still equirectangular photo, is a small hobbyist tool called SPAG-4D, which converts a single panorama into a splat using monocular depth estimation, with an MIT-licensed core and no need for COLMAP or camera poses at all. It is a genuinely useful fallback for a single viewpoint, and worth keeping in your back pocket for a station where multi-view registration fails. But it works one photo at a time. It does not fuse twenty overlapping stills of the same bridge into one coherent, metrically grounded scene the way multi-view photogrammetry does.

That fusion, COLMAP registering your twenty panoramas against each other and gsplat training a splat from the resulting point cloud and camera poses, is the thing nobody in this list ships as a product. It is not a compromise position or a workaround for lacking better tools. It is the actual capability the well-funded side of the industry has skipped in favor of either video capture or generative synthesis, because video is easier to sell as a five-minute service and generation is more impressive in a demo than patient reconstruction. Your twenty leveled stills, run through a free Colab T4 and a pipeline built for exactly this, are doing the more rigorous thing: producing a model of the bridge that is actually where the bridge is, not an impression of one. That is worth knowing when you decide how to describe what you are building, and it is worth knowing when you sit down with the reference build in The 2026 Orbit Studio Upgrade: A Reference Pipeline from Twenty Panoramas to Fly-Through, because the pipeline it walks you through is not chasing the industry's consumer products. It is occupying the lane they left empty.
