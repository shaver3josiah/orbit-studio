# Feed-Forward Geometry: VGGT, MapAnything, and the End of Mandatory Feature Matching

Twenty photos of a bridge. Wide gaps between stations, because you walked the span and stopped every ten meters rather than every ten centimeters. Low overlap, because a bridge deck doesn't give you the luxury of orbiting a subject. This is precisely the capture pattern that breaks classical structure-from-motion, and it's precisely the capture pattern a new generation of feed-forward geometry models was built to solve. The question this volume answers is simple to state and harder to settle: which of these models can you actually put in an open tool, and when do they beat a classical pipeline for a sparse, wide-baseline capture like yours?

## What "feed-forward" actually replaces

Classical SfM, the COLMAP way, is a loop. Detect features, match them across image pairs, triangulate, run bundle adjustment, repeat until the reprojection error settles. Every step in that loop can fail quietly on a bridge photo set: too little texture on concrete and steel, too few shared features between stations spaced ten meters apart, symmetric railings that confuse matching. That loop and its failure modes are the subject of the sibling volume "The Year SfM Collapsed Inward: COLMAP 4, the GLOMAP Merger, and the New Pose Baseline."

Feed-forward pointmap models skip the loop. A single neural network takes your images and directly regresses camera poses, depth maps, and a 3D point map in one pass, no iterative matching, no bundle adjustment. The lineage runs from DUSt3R through MASt3R to VGGT, then to Pi3 and Pi3X, then to MapAnything, each generation faster, more scalable, or more permissively licensed than the last.

## VGGT and the commercial-license turn

VGGT, the Visual Geometry Grounded Transformer, is a 1.2-billion-parameter feed-forward transformer that predicts camera extrinsics and intrinsics, depth maps, point maps, and 3D point tracks in under a second, from one image, a few, or hundreds. It won CVPR 2025's Best Paper award, and its own documentation states it works "even from sparse and low-overlap datasets," which is close to a direct description of a twenty-station bridge shoot.

For most of its life VGGT's weights carried a CC BY-NC-4.0 license, non-commercial only. That changed on 2025-07-29, when a separate VGGT-1B-Commercial checkpoint was released permitting commercial use, with a military-applications exclusion. This matters for an open tool: the base VGGT-1B checkpoint is off-limits if you want your pipeline usable by anyone for any purpose, but the commercial checkpoint is not. A successor, VGGT-Omega, arrived as a CVPR 2026 oral, using roughly 30 percent of VGGT's training memory and running about 1.6 times faster at inference, but its checkpoints are gated behind an access request, which makes it a research curiosity for now rather than a pipeline component.

## Pi3: no reference view, and the fastest of the family

Pi3 takes the permutation-equivariant idea further: it needs no fixed reference view at all, predicting affine-invariant poses and scale-invariant point maps regardless of input order. It is also the fastest of the group, benchmarked at 57.4 frames per second against VGGT's 43.2 and DUSt3R's 1.25. Its code is BSD-3-Clause, genuinely permissive, but its model weights are CC BY-NC 4.0, non-commercial only. A December 2025 update, Pi3X, adds smoother point clouds and approximate metric scale, but the license on the weights hasn't changed. For an open tool, Pi3's code is usable but its released weights are not, unless you retrain or wait for a commercial checkpoint the way VGGT got one.

## MapAnything: the pragmatic center of this lineage

Of everything in this family, MapAnything is the one built for exactly this job. It's developed by Meta Reality Labs and CMU, its code is Apache-2.0, and it functions as a wrapper: one interface that can run VGGT, DUSt3R, MASt3R, MUSt3R, or Pi3-X interchangeably underneath. It supports more than a dozen reconstruction tasks including SfM and multi-view stereo, and critically, it exports directly to COLMAP's cameras/images/points3D format, the same format Brush (Orbit Studio's already-vendored splat trainer) reads natively. That means swapping MapAnything in for the pycolmap matching stage requires no changes downstream.

The license story is where MapAnything earns its place. It ships two checkpoint tiers: the default facebook/map-anything checkpoint under CC-BY-NC-4.0, and a second tier, facebook/map-anything-apache, trained only on permissively licensed data and released under Apache-2.0, fully permissive. For a tool meant to be genuinely open, that second checkpoint is the one to use. MapAnything is also the most actively developed piece of this whole picture: four releases through 2026 alone, v1.1 in January, v1.1.1 in March adding Apple MPS support, v1.1.2 in May folding in VGGT Omega, and v1.1.3 on 2026-07-17. Its memory-efficient mode is documented up to 2000 views on 140GB of GPU memory, which is a datacenter figure, not a T4 one, but it tells you the architecture scales in the right direction rather than the wrong one. What isn't documented anywhere is the VRAM MapAnything actually needs for roughly 150 perspective crops on a free Colab T4, so that number has to be established empirically rather than assumed.

## AnySplat: skip the reconstruction step entirely, for a preview

A step beyond pointmap prediction, AnySplat predicts 3D Gaussian primitives directly, along with camera intrinsics and extrinsics, in one feed-forward pass from uncalibrated images, with no COLMAP and no separate pose stage at all. It's MIT-licensed, trained from a SIGGRAPH Asia 2025 paper without any 3D SfM or MVS supervision, and it renders novel views within seconds. For a solo operator, that makes it a genuinely useful sanity check: run your perspective crops through AnySplat before committing to a full Colab training run, just to see whether the geometry looks plausible at all. It is not, at this point, a demonstrated substitute for the final bridge-scale scene; it processes standard perspective images and hasn't been shown at the scale or outdoor complexity this capture needs.

## The licenses to actually avoid

Three names in this lineage are worth naming as traps rather than options. DUSt3R and MASt3R remain licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, non-commercial and share-alike, which rules them out for an open pipeline without separate permission from Naver. Pi3's released weights carry the same non-commercial restriction. And PanoSplatt3R, which sounds like the obvious panorama-native answer, turns out to have no usable license at all: its GitHub repository reports license: null in the GitHub API, with no LICENSE file anywhere in the tree, despite the code, weights, and evaluation scripts all being publicly released. Publicly visible is not the same as open-sourced. That question, and the broader stall of the direct-from-panorama model family, is the subject of the sibling volume "Panorama-Native Splatting: The Promise and Stall of the Direct-From-Equirect Family." PanoSplatt3R is also strictly pairwise, taking exactly two panoramas per inference with no multi-view fusion tool, and its only public training and test data is indoor (HM3D and Replica), so even setting the license aside, it isn't validated for a bridge.

## Why panoramas still need a detour through perspective crops

None of the production-ready models in this lineage, VGGT, VGGT-Omega, Pi3/Pi3X, MapAnything, or AnySplat, ingest equirectangular panoramas natively. All of them expect perspective images. The practical answer is to explode each of your 20 panoramas into perspective crops before feeding them in, either a plain six-to-eight-face cube split or a denser 18-view ring, roughly eight yaw angles across two pitch bands near plus and minus 35 degrees plus two polar views, which avoids the pole distortion a bare cube split introduces. Either way, that turns 20 panoramas into somewhere around 120 to 180 perspective images headed into the feed-forward network.

The purpose-built alternative, models like PanoVGGT that take a raw set of two to twenty-four equirectangular panoramas and predict poses, depth, and points in one pass with no crop step at all, exists only on paper as of today. PanoVGGT reports strong pose and depth accuracy on outdoor urban scenes in its CVPR 2026 paper, which is as close a match to a bridge shoot as any source in this research turned up, but its own text says only that "code and dataset will be released," and no public repository could be confirmed. Treat it, along with VGGT-360 and 360-GeoGS, as a 2026 Q4 watch item, not a component to build around today.

## When feed-forward actually wins

The case for swapping feed-forward geometry into the pose stage isn't just architectural elegance. It's that COLMAP's own authors agree the classical pipeline needs the help. GLUEMAP, a CVPR 2026 paper from Linfei Pan, Johannes Schönberger (the original author of COLMAP), and Marc Pollefeys, builds a hybrid pipeline that leans on feed-forward models, Pi3, SALAD, a VGGSfM tracker, specifically to patch COLMAP's own documented failure modes: low texture, limited overlap, wide baseline. That is not a competitor's marketing claim. That is the tool's own lineage conceding the gap. Separately, InstantSplat established the pattern years earlier at smaller scale: replacing SfM initialization with DUSt3R geometric priors cut reconstruction time by more than 30x versus SfM-plus-3DGS and raised SSIM on sparse-view benchmarks from 0.3755 to 0.7624.

What the evidence does not support is dropping per-scene splat training entirely. AnySplat and similar direct-to-Gaussian models are fast previews, not proven finals at bridge scale. The right shape for this pipeline, and the one worked out in full in "The 2026 Orbit Studio Upgrade: A Reference Pipeline from Twenty Panoramas to Fly-Through," is narrower than "replace COLMAP with a neural network." It's replace the matching and bundle-adjustment stage with MapAnything's Apache-licensed checkpoint, keep the perspective-crop step because nothing ingests your panoramas directly yet, and keep real per-scene optimization in Brush or gsplat, seeded by MapAnything's poses and metric point cloud instead of COLMAP's.

## What this means for your bridge

For twenty sparse, wide-baseline panoramas of a bridge and its roadways, feature matching was never going to be the strong part of your pipeline. It's the part most likely to silently drop a station or triangulate a bad seam where the railing repeats. Feed-forward pointmap prediction, specifically MapAnything running its Apache-2.0 checkpoint over your cube-face or 18-view crops, gives you a drop-in replacement for that fragile stage, with output that lands in a format your existing splat trainer already reads. Keep the crop step. Keep the training step. Skip the license traps in DUSt3R, MASt3R, Pi3's weights, and PanoSplatt3R. And keep an eye on the horizon, because a model that takes your twenty panoramas directly, no crops, no detour, is being promised for exactly this problem. It just isn't shipped yet.
