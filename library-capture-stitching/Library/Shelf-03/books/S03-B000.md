# The 2026 Frontier: What Changed, What Stalled, and What Still Splits the Sphere

You arrive at this shelf knowing two things. From Shelf 01 you know how to stand on a bridge deck with a leveled tripod and an Insta360 X4 Air and walk away with twenty clean equirectangular stills. From Shelf 02 you know the classic pipeline that turns them into a Gaussian splat: explode each panorama into perspective crops, hand the crops to COLMAP for camera poses, train with gsplat, view the result. The question this shelf answers is the one every practitioner faced in mid-2026: how much of that pipeline survived the last eighteen months, and which parts of the flood of new tools, models, and formats are worth letting into it?

The short answer, and the throughline of all ten books here, is that 2025 and 2026 were years of consolidation, not revolution. The classic pipeline did not get replaced. It got absorbed, hardened, and made partly optional, while the one step everyone expected to disappear, splitting the sphere into perspective crops before anything else can happen, stubbornly refused to die. Every stage of the Shelf 02 pipeline has a 2026 story, and each cluster of books on this shelf tells one of them.

## The pose cluster: COLMAP ate the field, and neural networks made it optional

Three books cover what happened to pose recovery, the stage where twenty photos become twenty camera positions.

"The Year SfM Collapsed Inward: COLMAP 4, the GLOMAP Merger, and the New Pose Baseline" is the anchor. Its story is that COLMAP stopped being one tool in a hand-assembled stack and started absorbing the stack: GLOMAP, the fast global mapper you may have bolted on as a separate binary, was archived in March 2026 and folded into COLMAP 4.0 as the built-in global mapper, and the ALIKED and LightGlue matching that used to require the stalling hloc toolbox now ships natively. COLMAP 4.1 even grew a spherical camera model that can eat an equirectangular JPG directly. The book's judgment call matters more than its news: the native spherical path is faster but less accurate by COLMAP's own account, and with only twenty panoramas, accuracy is the constraint. So the split-then-reconstruct workflow survives, but the recipe inside it changes: pin COLMAP 4.1.1, run the global mapper first, keep incremental as the retry.

"Splitting the Sphere: Perspective Rigs for Panorama SfM in Practice" covers that surviving workflow itself: how a panorama actually becomes a rig of perspective crops, what face counts and fields of view keep matching healthy, and how pycolmap drives the reconstruction. It is the craft manual for the step the rest of the shelf keeps deciding to keep.

"Feed-Forward Geometry: VGGT, MapAnything, and the End of Mandatory Feature Matching" tells the more radical half of the pose story. A new family of transformers, running from DUSt3R through VGGT to MapAnything, regresses poses, depth, and 3D points in a single forward pass, no feature matching, no bundle adjustment. This matters for your capture specifically because sparse, wide-baseline photo sets are exactly where classical matching fails quietly, and even COLMAP's own authors concede the gap: the GLUEMAP paper from Schönberger's group patches COLMAP's failure modes with these very models. The book's practical pick is MapAnything with its Apache-licensed checkpoint, which exports straight to COLMAP format so nothing downstream notices the swap. Its equally practical warning: the license traps. DUSt3R, MASt3R, and Pi3's weights are non-commercial, and several tempting repos ship no license at all.

## The panorama-native question: the shortcut that is not ready

"Panorama-Native Splatting: The Promise and Stall of the Direct-From-Equirect Family" answers the question you have been asking since Shelf 01: why crop a photo that already covers the whole sphere? The answer is that the research family built to skip the crop, OmniGS, ODGS, PanoSplatt3R, PanSplat and their kin, is real but stalled. The feed-forward members take exactly two panoramas and cannot fuse twenty. The per-scene members lean on OpenMVG or OpenSfM for poses and have gone quiet. Licenses range from GPL to nonexistent. The book's verdict is the shelf's most useful negative result: leave the perspective-crop stage alone, and put CylinderSplat, Spherical-GOF, and SC-OmniGS on a six-month watch list rather than in the pipeline.

## The training cluster: sparse-view recipes and a changed trainer landscape

Two books cover what happens after poses exist.

"Twenty Stations, One Bridge: A Sparse-View Playbook for Outdoor Splats" is the most code-forward volume on the shelf. Twenty outdoor stations sit at the hard end of sparse-view splatting, where floaters and view-overfitting are the default outcome, and the 2025-2026 literature (InstantSplat, FSGS, DNGaussian, Pi-GS) converges on a fixable trio: a monocular-depth prior scored by Pearson correlation rather than raw distance, sky and transient masks applied before the loss, and smarter floater pruning than a plain opacity reset. The book turns each into working gsplat code, along with the config flags gsplat already ships for exposure drift between stations. It is honest that no paper has benchmarked this exact scenario; the recipe is an extrapolation, clearly labeled.

"The Trainer Field in Mid-2026: Brush, LichtFeld Studio, and the Quiet Stall of Nerfstudio" answers the question Shelf 02 left open: which trainer? The landscape moved hard. Nerfstudio, the old default, has not seen a commit since July 2025 and is a foundation nobody maintains. Brush, CUDA-free and able to train on any GPU vendor or even in a browser, is the sane default for a Windows laptop of unknown hardware. LichtFeld Studio is the fast-moving CUDA-only alternative, and its Moshpit360 plugin is the closest thing in open source to a template for one-click 360 ingestion. The structural fact that frames the whole book: no production trainer rasterizes equirectangular images natively, so the crop step survives here too.

## The shipping cluster: a consolidated web stack

"Shipping a World: SOG, SPZ 4, and the New Web Splat Stack" covers the last mile, and it is the stage where 2026 consolidation is cleanest. The old GaussianSplats3D viewer retired itself in favor of Spark, the maintained MIT-licensed renderer. PlayCanvas's SOG format became the default browser container, with Niantic's SPZ 4 as the interchange format for engines and the candidate for the Khronos glTF extension. The book's working pieces are exactly what Orbit Studio needs: a splat-transform conversion script from trained PLY to web bundle, a Spark loader with LOD, and the SuperSplat timeline workflow that seeds a fly-through path from the very COLMAP poses the pipeline already computed.

## The judgment cluster: what to let in, and where you actually stand

Two books are about decisions rather than mechanics.

"Generative Fill and Its License Minefield" draws the line between reconstruction and hallucination. Its rule is worth memorizing: a fill pass belongs only where the camera genuinely could not see, sky, occlusion shadows, the tripod's nadir hole, and never as a substitute for coverage. Its licensing survey is the sharpest on the shelf: HunyuanWorld is banned in three jurisdictions, Difix3D+ is capable but non-commercial, and only WorldGen's Apache path and SPAG4d's MIT backends clear the bar for an open tool, carefully.

"What the Industry Shipped While You Were Capturing: Marble, Scaniverse, and the Open Lane" is the strategic read. It surveys what the funded side of the industry built in the same window and finds that nobody ships your exact capability. Marble generates plausible worlds rather than reconstructing real ones. Scaniverse and the Insta360-Splatica pipeline do real reconstruction but only from video, behind subscriptions. Meta wants you in a headset. Zillow and Matterport prove exterior splats are a paying market. The conclusion reframes the whole project: still-photo multi-view reconstruction is not a hobbyist workaround, it is an open lane the industry skipped.

## The synthesis

"The 2026 Orbit Studio Upgrade: A Reference Pipeline from Twenty Panoramas to Fly-Through" is where every verdict above becomes one runnable Python orchestrator: py360convert crops, COLMAP 4.1.1 with a global-then-incremental-then-MapAnything fallback chain, Brush or regularized gsplat depending on the GPU, splat-transform packaging, and a Spark fly-through. If the shelf has a spine, this is it.

## A reading path

Read "The 2026 Orbit Studio Upgrade" first, quickly, for the skeleton. Then follow the pipeline in order: "The Year SfM Collapsed Inward," "Splitting the Sphere," and "Feed-Forward Geometry" for poses; "Twenty Stations, One Bridge" and "The Trainer Field in Mid-2026" for training; "Shipping a World" for delivery. Save "Panorama-Native Splatting," "Generative Fill and Its License Minefield," and "What the Industry Shipped While You Were Capturing" for last; they are the books that tell you what to refuse, and refusals make more sense once you know what you are keeping.

## How this shelf sits on the other two

Shelf 01 taught you to capture; nothing here changes a single habit from it, and several books quietly vindicate it, since leveled stills with controlled exposure are precisely the input the video-only industry tools cannot accept. Shelf 02 taught you the classic COLMAP-plus-gsplat pipeline; this shelf is the diff against it. The shape survives intact. What changes are the versions (COLMAP 4.1.1 with GLOMAP inside), the defaults (ALIKED and LightGlue, Brush over nerfstudio, SOG over raw PLY on the web), the fallbacks (MapAnything when matching fails), and the guardrails (sparse-view regularization, license discipline, generative fill kept small and honest). What it means for you is unusually concrete: your twenty panoramas of the bridge were never the compromise. In 2026 they are the input to the one pipeline nobody else ships, and this shelf is the record of exactly which pieces of it to trust.
